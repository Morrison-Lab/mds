Theorem 1 (The dot product is linear in each slot) For vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\) and numbers \(a, b\),

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot (a\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + b\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) = a\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) + b\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) \]

and

\[ (a\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + b\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = a\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + b\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}). \]

Proof. Second slot.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot (a\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + b\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) &= \sum_{i=1}^px_i\,(a\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + b\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}})_i && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~10 in Vectors}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^px_i\,(a u_i + b w_i) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrices.qmd#def-scalar-mult}{\text{Definition~9 in Matrices}}\text{, }\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-vector-addition}{\text{Definition~9 in Vectors}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^p\mathopen{}\left(x_i a u_i + x_i b w_i\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(distribute each } x_i \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^p\mathopen{}\left(a\,x_i u_i + b\,x_i w_i\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(commute the factors in each product)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^pa\,x_i u_i + \sum_{i=1}^pb\,x_i w_i && \text{(split the finite sum)} \\ &= a \sum_{i=1}^px_i u_i + b \sum_{i=1}^px_i w_i && \text{(factor } a \text{ and } b \text{ out of the sums)} \\ &= a\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) + b\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}). && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~10 in Vectors}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

First slot.

\[ \begin{aligned} (a\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + b\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot (a\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + b\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#thm-lincom-symmetric}{\text{Theorem~1 in Vectors}}\text{)} \\ &= a\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) + b\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) && \text{(second slot)} \\ &= a\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + b\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}). && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#thm-lincom-symmetric}{\text{Theorem~1 in Vectors}}\text{, twice)} \end{aligned} \]

Two special cases are used often. With

\[ \begin{aligned} a &= b \\ &= 1, \end{aligned} \]

and \(1\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\), the theorem gives \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\). With \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) and \(b = 0\), and \(0\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), it gives \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot (a\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) = a\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}})\), and likewise in the first slot.

Example 1 (Splitting a dot product) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = (1, 2)\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = (3, 0)\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = (0, 1)\), \(a = 2\) and \(b = -1\). Directly, \(a\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + b\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = (6, -1)\) and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot (6, -1) &= 6 - 2 \\ &= 4. \end{aligned} \]

By Theorem 1,

\[ \begin{aligned} a\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) + b\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) &= 2 \cdot 3 + (-1) \cdot 2 \\ &= 4, \end{aligned} \]

the same number.

Exercise 1 (Finding a dot product from two known dot products) Theorem 1 lets a dot product with a combination be found from the dot products with its parts. Suppose that \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = 3\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = -1\).

Find \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot (2\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + 5\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}})\).

Hint

Use Theorem 1 with \(a = 2\) and \(b = 5\).

Solution 1. By Theorem 1,

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot (2\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + 5\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) = 2\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) + 5\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) = 2 \cdot 3 + 5 \cdot(-1) = 1. \]

Definition 1 (Orthogonal complement) The orthogonal complement [1] of a set \(\mathcal{X}\) of vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^p\) is the set of vectors orthogonal (Definition 22 in Vectors) to every vector of \(\mathcal{X}\):

\[ \mathcal{X}^\perp \stackrel{\text{def}}{=} \mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \in \mathbb{R}^p : \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = 0 \text{ for all } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathcal{X}\right\}\mathclose{}. \]

\(\mathcal{X}^\perp\) is read “\(\mathcal{X}\) perp”.

Example 2 (Orthogonal complements in \(\mathbb{R}^3\))  

  • A plane. Let \(\mathcal{X} = \mathopen{}\left\{(a, b, 0) : a, b \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{}\), the plane \(z = 0\). A vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) is in \(\mathcal{X}^\perp\) when

    \[ \begin{aligned} (a, b, 0) \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} &= a u_1 + b u_2 \\ &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

    for all \(a\) and \(b\). Taking \((a, b) = (1, 0)\) forces \(u_1 = 0\), and taking \((a, b) = (0, 1)\) forces \(u_2 = 0\); conversely, if

    \[ \begin{aligned} u_1 &= u_2 \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

    then \(a u_1 + b u_2 = 0\) for all \(a\) and \(b\). So \(\mathcal{X}^\perp = \mathopen{}\left\{(0, 0, c) : c \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{}\), the \(z\)-axis.

  • Two vectors. The two-element set \(\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\) is not a subspace, and its orthogonal complement is the \(z\)-axis too: the two conditions \(u_1 = 0\) and \(u_2 = 0\) are the same as for the plane.

  • The extremes. \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}^\perp = \mathbb{R}^3\), because \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = 0\) for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\). \((\mathbb{R}^3)^\perp = \mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\), because a \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) in it is orthogonal to itself, and

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} &= u_1^2 + u_2^2 + u_3^2 \\ &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

    forces \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

Exercise 2 (Testing whether a vector is in an orthogonal complement) A vector is in the orthogonal complement of a set when it is orthogonal to every vector of the set. Let \(\mathcal{X} = \mathopen{}\left\{(1, 2, 0), (0, 3, 1)\right\}\mathclose{}\), a set of two vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Decide whether the vector \((2, -1, 3)\) is in \(\mathcal{X}^\perp\).

Hint

Take the dot product of \((2, -1, 3)\) with each vector of \(\mathcal{X}\).

Solution 2. Here

\[ \begin{aligned} (1, 2, 0) \cdot (2, -1, 3) &= 2 - 2 + 0 = 0, \\ (0, 3, 1) \cdot (2, -1, 3) &= 0 - 3 + 3 = 0, \end{aligned} \]

so \((2, -1, 3)\) is orthogonal to both vectors of \(\mathcal{X}\), and it is in \(\mathcal{X}^\perp\).

Remark 1 (The orthogonal complement of a set that is not a subspace). \(\mathcal{X}\) in Definition 1 need not be a subspace.

Theorem 2 (An orthogonal complement is a subspace) For any set \(\mathcal{X}\) of vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^p\), \(\mathcal{X}^\perp\) (Definition 1) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (Definition 4 in Subspaces and Rank).

Proof. \(\mathcal{X}^\perp\) is not empty:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}&= \sum_i x_i \cdot 0 \\ &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\in \mathcal{X}^\perp\). Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \in \mathcal{X}^\perp\), let \(c \in \mathbb{R}\), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathcal{X}\).

Addition.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} && \text{(}\href{#thm-dot-linear}{\text{Theorem~1}}\text{, special case } a \text{ and } b \text{ both equal to } 1 \text{)} \\ &= 0 + 0 && \text{(} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \in \mathcal{X}^\perp \text{)} \\ &= 0. && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

Scalar multiplication.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot (c\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) &= c\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) && \text{(}\href{#thm-dot-linear}{\text{Theorem~1}}\text{, special case } b = 0 \text{)} \\ &= c \cdot 0 && \text{(} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \in \mathcal{X}^\perp \text{)} \\ &= 0. && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

These two equations hold for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathcal{X}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\) and \(c\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) are in \(\mathcal{X}^\perp\).

Example 3 (The complement of a single vector is a line) The one-element set \(\mathcal{X} = \mathopen{}\left\{(1, 2)\right\}\mathclose{}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is not a subspace: \(2\,(1, 2) = (2, 4)\) is not in it. Its orthogonal complement is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{X}^\perp &= \mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \in \mathbb{R}^2 : u_1 + 2 u_2 = 0\right\}\mathclose{} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\{c\,(-2, 1) : c \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{}, \end{aligned} \]

since \(u_1 + 2u_2 = 0\) means

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} &= (-2u_2, u_2) \\ &= u_2\,(-2, 1). \end{aligned} \]

That set is \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(-2, 1)\right\}\mathclose{}\), a subspace by Theorem 2 in Subspaces and Rank, as Theorem 2 says it must be. It contains \((-2, 1)\), the vector Remark 8 in Vectors found perpendicular to \((1, 2)\).

Exercise 3 (Finding the orthogonal complement of two vectors) Theorem 2 says that an orthogonal complement is always a subspace, even when the original set is not. Let \(\mathcal{X} = \mathopen{}\left\{(1, 0, 1), (1, 0, -1)\right\}\mathclose{}\), a set of two vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Find \(\mathcal{X}^\perp\) as a span.

Hint

A vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) is in \(\mathcal{X}^\perp\) when \(u_1 + u_3 = 0\) and \(u_1 - u_3 = 0\).

Solution 3. A vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) is in \(\mathcal{X}^\perp\) exactly when \((1, 0, 1) \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = u_1 + u_3 = 0\) and \((1, 0, -1) \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = u_1 - u_3 = 0\). Adding the two equations gives \(u_1 = 0\), and then \(u_3 = 0\), while \(u_2\) is free. So

\[ \mathcal{X}^\perp = \mathopen{}\left\{(0, u_2, 0) : u_2 \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{} = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(0, 1, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}, \]

the second coordinate axis, which is a subspace, as Theorem 2 says.

Theorem 3 (The orthogonal complement of a column space is a null space) For any \(m \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\),

\[ \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})^\perp = \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}). \]

Proof. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_n\) be the columns of \(\mathbf{A}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j = (a_{1j}, \ldots, a_{mj})\). For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^m\), entry \(j\) of \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} ({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})_j &= \sum_{i=1}^{m} ({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{ji}\, x_i && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#def-matvec-mult}{\text{Definition~8 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_{ij}\, x_i && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrices.qmd#def-matrix-transpose}{\text{Definition~6 in Matrices}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}. && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~10 in Vectors}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

So \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\) (Definition 3 in Column Space and Null Space) exactly when \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = 0\) for every column \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j\).

\(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})^\perp \subseteq \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\). Each column \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j\) is in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) (Theorem 1 in Column Space and Null Space), so a vector orthogonal to all of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) is orthogonal to every column.

\(\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) \subseteq \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})^\perp\). Suppose \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = 0\) for every \(j\), and take any \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}\) for some \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) (Definition 1 in Column Space and Null Space). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= \sum_{i=1}^{m} (\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}})_i\, x_i && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~10 in Vectors}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{m} \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{j=1}^na_{ij} z_j\right)\mathclose{}\, x_i && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#def-matvec-mult}{\text{Definition~8 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^na_{ij}\, z_j\, x_i && \text{(distribute each } x_i \text{ over the inner sum)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^n\sum_{i=1}^{m} a_{ij}\, z_j\, x_i && \text{(swap the order of the finite sums)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^nz_j \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_{ij}\, x_i && \text{(commute, then factor } z_j \text{ out of the inner sum)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^nz_j\, (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~10 in Vectors}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^nz_j \cdot 0 && \text{(each } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = 0 \text{)} \\ &= 0. && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

So \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is orthogonal to every vector of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\).

Theorem 4 (A subspace and its orthogonal complement make up the whole space) Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^p\). Then

  1. \(\mathcal{S} \cap \mathcal{S}^\perp = \mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\);
  2. \(\dim(\mathcal{S}) + \dim(\mathcal{S}^\perp) = p\);
  3. \(\mathbb{R}^p = \mathcal{S} \oplus \mathcal{S}^\perp\) (Definition 3 in Sums and Direct Sums).

So every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\) can be written as \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \in \mathcal{S}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \in \mathcal{S}^\perp\) in only one way (Theorem 4 in Sums and Direct Sums).

Proof. \(\mathcal{S}^\perp\) is a subspace (Theorem 2), so it has a dimension (Definition 4 in Bases and Dimension).

Part 1. Both \(\mathcal{S}\) and \(\mathcal{S}^\perp\) contain \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) (Theorem 1 in Subspaces and Rank). If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is in both, then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is orthogonal to every vector of \(\mathcal{S}\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) itself included, so

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= x_1^2 + \cdots + x_p^2 \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

which forces every \(x_i = 0\).

Part 2. Let \(d = \dim(\mathcal{S})\). If \(d = 0\), then \(\mathcal{S}\) has a basis (Theorem 4 in Bases and Dimension) with \(0\) vectors (Definition 4 in Bases and Dimension), the empty list, whose span is \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\) (Definition 5 in Subspaces and Rank); so \(\mathcal{S} = \mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\), \(\mathcal{S}^\perp = \mathbb{R}^p\) (every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) satisfies \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = 0\)), and \(0 + p = p\) (Example 6 in Bases and Dimension). If \(d \ge 1\), choose a basis \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_d\) of \(\mathcal{S}\) (Theorem 4 in Bases and Dimension), and let \(\mathbf{A}\) be the \(p \times d\) matrix with these columns. The basis spans \(\mathcal{S}\), so \(\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) (Theorem 1 in Column Space and Null Space), and \(\mathcal{S}^\perp = \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\) (Theorem 3). \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\) is \(d \times p\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \dim(\mathcal{S}^\perp) &= \dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(}\href{#thm-complement-null-space}{\text{Theorem~3}}\text{)} \\ &= \operatorname{nullity}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-rank-nullity.qmd#def-nullity}{\text{Definition~1 in Rank-Nullity and Rank Factorization}}\text{)} \\ &= p - \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-rank-nullity.qmd#thm-rank-nullity}{\text{Theorem~2 in Rank-Nullity and Rank Factorization}}\text{, for the } p \text{ columns of } {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \text{)} \\ &= p - \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-rank-nullity.qmd#thm-rank-transpose}{\text{Theorem~4 in Rank-Nullity and Rank Factorization}}\text{)} \\ &= p - \dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-rank-nullity.qmd#thm-rank-dim}{\text{Theorem~1 in Rank-Nullity and Rank Factorization}}\text{)} \\ &= p - d. && \text{(} \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathcal{S} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Part 3. \(\mathcal{S} + \mathcal{S}^\perp\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (Theorem 1 in Sums and Direct Sums), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \dim(\mathcal{S} + \mathcal{S}^\perp) &= \dim(\mathcal{S}) + \dim(\mathcal{S}^\perp) - \dim(\mathcal{S} \cap \mathcal{S}^\perp) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-direct-sums.qmd#thm-dim-sum}{\text{Theorem~2 in Sums and Direct Sums}}\text{)} \\ &= p - \dim(\mathcal{S} \cap \mathcal{S}^\perp) && \text{(part 2)} \\ &= p - 0 && \text{(part 1, and } \dim(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}) = 0 \text{ by }\href{linear-algebra-bases.qmd#exm-dimension}{\text{Example~6 in Bases and Dimension}}\text{)} \\ &= p. && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\mathcal{S} + \mathcal{S}^\perp \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p\), \(\mathbb{R}^p\) is a subspace (Example 3 in Subspaces and Rank) and \(\dim(\mathbb{R}^p) = p\) (Example 6 in Bases and Dimension), Theorem 6 in Bases and Dimension gives \(\mathcal{S} + \mathcal{S}^\perp = \mathbb{R}^p\). Together with part 1, \(\mathbb{R}^p = \mathcal{S} \oplus \mathcal{S}^\perp\). The uniqueness of the split then follows from Theorem 4 in Sums and Direct Sums (statement 1 implies statement 3).

Example 5 (Splitting a vector of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) along a line and its complement) Let \(\mathcal{S} = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 1, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). A single nonzero vector is linearly independent (\(c\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) forces \(c = 0\)), so \((1, 1, 0)\) is a basis of \(\mathcal{S}\) and \(\dim(\mathcal{S}) = 1\). A vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) is in \(\mathcal{S}^\perp\) exactly when \(u_1 + u_2 = 0\): that condition says \((1, 1, 0) \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = 0\), and then

\[ \begin{aligned} c\,(1, 1, 0) \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} &= c\,(u_1 + u_2) \\ &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

for every \(c\). So

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{S}^\perp &= \mathopen{}\left\{(a, -a, c) : a, c \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{} \\ &= \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, -1, 0), (0, 0, 1)\right\}\mathclose{}. \end{aligned} \]

Those two vectors are linearly independent, because \(a\,(1, -1, 0) + c\,(0, 0, 1) = (a, -a, c)\) is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) only if

\[ \begin{aligned} a &= c \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

so \(\dim(\mathcal{S}^\perp) = 2\), and \(1 + 2 = 3\), as part 2 says.

To split \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = (3, 1, 2)\), look for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = t\,(1, 1, 0)\) with

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \\ &= (3 - t, 1 - t, 2) \end{aligned} \]

in \(\mathcal{S}^\perp\): that membership needs \((3 - t) + (1 - t) = 0\), so \(t = 2\). Then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = (2, 2, 0)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = (1, -1, 2)\), with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = (3, 1, 2)\) and

\[ \begin{aligned} (1, 1, 0) \cdot (1, -1, 2) &= 1 - 1 + 0 \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

Theorem 5 (The orthogonal complement of the orthogonal complement) For any subspace \(\mathcal{S}\) of \(\mathbb{R}^p\),

\[ (\mathcal{S}^\perp)^\perp = \mathcal{S}. \]

Proof. \(\mathcal{S} \subseteq (\mathcal{S}^\perp)^\perp\). Take \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{s}}\vphantom{s}} \in \mathcal{S}\) and any \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \in \mathcal{S}^\perp\). By Definition 1, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{s}}\vphantom{s}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = 0\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{s}}\vphantom{s}} = 0\) too (Theorem 1 in Vectors). So \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{s}}\vphantom{s}}\) is orthogonal to every vector of \(\mathcal{S}^\perp\), that is, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{s}}\vphantom{s}} \in (\mathcal{S}^\perp)^\perp\).

Equal dimensions. \(\mathcal{S}^\perp\) is a subspace (Theorem 2), so part 2 of Theorem 4 applies to it as well as to \(\mathcal{S}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \dim\mathopen{}\left((\mathcal{S}^\perp)^\perp\right)\mathclose{} &= p - \dim(\mathcal{S}^\perp) && \text{(}\href{#thm-orthogonal-direct-sum}{\text{Theorem~4}}\text{, applied to } \mathcal{S}^\perp \text{)} \\ &= p - \mathopen{}\left(p - \dim(\mathcal{S})\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(}\href{#thm-orthogonal-direct-sum}{\text{Theorem~4}}\text{, applied to } \mathcal{S} \text{)} \\ &= \dim(\mathcal{S}). && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

\((\mathcal{S}^\perp)^\perp\) is a subspace (Theorem 2) that contains \(\mathcal{S}\) and has the same dimension, so it equals \(\mathcal{S}\) (Theorem 6 in Bases and Dimension).

2 The fundamental theorem of linear algebra

Theorem 6 (Fundamental theorem of linear algebra) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be an \(m \times n\) matrix with \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = r\). Then

  1. \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})^\perp = \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\), and \(\mathbb{R}^m = \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}) \oplus \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\);

  2. \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^\perp\);

  3. \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})^\perp = \mathcal{C}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\), and \(\mathbb{R}^n = \mathcal{C}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) \oplus \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\);

  4. \[ \begin{aligned} \dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\right)\mathclose{} &= \dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= r, \end{aligned} \]

    \(\dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\right)\mathclose{} = n - r\), and \(\dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\right)\mathclose{} = m - r\).

Proof. Part 1. The first equation is Theorem 3. \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^m\) (Theorem 1 in Column Space and Null Space), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{R}^m &= \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}) \oplus \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})^\perp && \text{(}\href{#thm-orthogonal-direct-sum}{\text{Theorem~4}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}) \oplus \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}). && \text{(first equation)} \end{aligned} \]

Part 2.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}) &= \mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})^\perp\right)\mathclose{}^\perp && \text{(}\href{#thm-double-complement}{\text{Theorem~5}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^\perp. && \text{(part 1)} \end{aligned} \]

Part 3. Apply parts 2 and 1 to the \(n \times m\) matrix \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\), using \({({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})}^{\top} = \mathbf{A}\): part 2 gives \(\mathcal{C}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})^\perp\), and part 1 gives \(\mathbb{R}^n = \mathcal{C}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) \oplus \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\).

Part 4. \(\dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\right)\mathclose{} = r\) by Theorem 1 in Rank-Nullity and Rank Factorization. \(\dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\right)\mathclose{} = \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\) by Theorem 1 in Rank-Nullity and Rank Factorization, and that is \(r\) by Theorem 4 in Rank-Nullity and Rank Factorization. \(\mathbf{A}\) has \(n\) columns, so \(\dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\right)\mathclose{} = n - r\) (Definition 1 in Rank-Nullity and Rank Factorization, Theorem 2 in Rank-Nullity and Rank Factorization). \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\) has \(m\) columns and rank \(r\), so \(\dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\right)\mathclose{} = m - r\) in the same way.

Example 8 (The four subspaces of the matrix in Example 1 in Column Space and Null Space) For \(\mathbf{A}\) in Example 1 in Column Space and Null Space, \(m = 2\), \(n = 3\) and \(r = 1\) (Example 1 in Rank-Nullity and Rank Factorization).

  • In \(\mathbb{R}^3\): the row space \(\mathcal{C}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, -2, -2)\right\}\mathclose{}\)

    1. has dimension \(1 = r\), and \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(2, 1, 0), (2, 0, 1)\right\}\mathclose{}\) has dimension \(2 = n - r\) (Example 2 in Rank-Nullity and Rank Factorization). The spanning vectors are orthogonal:

    \[ \begin{aligned} (1, -2, -2) \cdot (2, 1, 0) &= 2 - 2 + 0 \\ &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

    and

    \[ \begin{aligned} (1, -2, -2) \cdot (2, 0, 1) &= 2 + 0 - 2 \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

  • In \(\mathbb{R}^2\): \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 3)\right\}\mathclose{}\) has dimension \(1 = r\), and \(\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(-3, 1)\right\}\mathclose{}\) has dimension \(1 = m - r\) (Example 4).

By part 3, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = (1, 0, 0)\) splits uniquely into a row-space part \(t\,(1, -2, -2)\) and a null-space part. The null-space part \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - t\,(1, -2, -2)\) lies in \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\) (part 3), and \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathcal{C}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^\perp\) (Theorem 3 applied to \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\), with \({({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})}^{\top} = \mathbf{A}\)), so it must be orthogonal to \((1, -2, -2)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} 0 &= (1, -2, -2) \cdot \mathopen{}\left((1, 0, 0) - t\,(1, -2, -2)\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(orthogonality)} \\ &= (1, -2, -2) \cdot \mathopen{}\left(1 \cdot(1, 0, 0) + (-t)\,(1, -2, -2)\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(write the difference as a linear combination)} \\ &= 1 \cdot\mathopen{}\left((1, -2, -2) \cdot (1, 0, 0)\right)\mathclose{} + (-t)\,\mathopen{}\left((1, -2, -2) \cdot (1, -2, -2)\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(}\href{#thm-dot-linear}{\text{Theorem~1}}\text{)} \\ &= 1 \cdot 1 + (-t) \cdot 9 && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~10 in Vectors}}\text{)} \\ &= 1 - 9t, && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

so \(t = \frac{1}{9}\). The row-space part is \(\mathopen{}\left(\frac{1}{9}, -\frac{2}{9}, -\frac{2}{9}\right)\mathclose{}\) and the null-space part is \(\mathopen{}\left(\frac{8}{9}, \frac{2}{9}, \frac{2}{9}\right)\mathclose{}\); as a check, row 1 of \(\mathbf{A}\) gives \(\frac{8}{9} - 2 \cdot\frac{2}{9} - 2 \cdot\frac{2}{9} = 0\), and row 2 is \(3\) times row 1, so \(\mathbf{A}\) sends the null-space part to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_2\).

These four statements are the fundamental theorem of linear algebra [2].

Exercise 7 (Testing whether a linear system has a solution through the left null space) Part 2 of Theorem 6 says that \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^\perp\), so a linear system \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) has a solution exactly when \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) is orthogonal to every vector of the left null space. Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}. \]

Decide whether \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) has a solution, by testing \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) against \(\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\).

Hint

Find one nonzero vector of \(\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\) whose third entry is \(0\).

Solution 7. Here \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = (y_1 + y_2, y_1 + y_2)\), so \((1, -1, 0)\) is in \(\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\). But

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \cdot (1, -1, 0) = 1 - 2 + 0 = -1 \neq 0, \]

so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) is not in \(\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^\perp = \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\), and \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) has no solution. Directly, the first two entries of \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) are both \(x_1 + x_2\), while those of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) are \(1\) and \(2\).
