Design Matrices and Projections

Published

Last modified: 2026-11-19 18:34:41 (PST)

1 Design Matrix

DefDefinition 1

Definition 1  

Design matrix, model matrix

In a regression model with \(n\) observations and \(p\) predictors, the design matrix [1] (or model matrix) \(\mathbf{X}\) is the \(n \times p\) matrix whose \(i\)-th row \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i}^{\top}\) holds, for observation \(i\), the \(p\) entries the model uses. These can be covariates, a \(1\) for the intercept, \(0/1\) columns for categories, or functions of covariates, such as \(x^2\). For a straight-line fit, for example, the design matrix has a first column of 1s, which allows the intercept to be estimated [1], [2, Sec. 2.4]. In symbols,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{X} &= \begin{bmatrix} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_1}^{\top} \\ {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_2}^{\top} \\ \vdots \\ {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_n}^{\top} \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1p} \\ x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2p} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{np} \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

RemarkRemark 1 (Products with the design matrix)

Remark 1 (Products with the design matrix). The product \(\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\) collects the values \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\) for all \(n\) observations into a single \(n \times 1\) vector:

\[ \mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= \begin{bmatrix} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_1}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\\ \vdots \\ {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_n}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} \end{bmatrix} \]

For example, take the rank-\(2\) matrix from Example 2 in Subspaces and Rank as \(\mathbf{X}\), with \(n = 3\) observations and \(p = 2\) columns: an intercept column and one covariate. With \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= (1, 2)\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 5 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

The matrix \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) is \(p \times p\) and symmetric, since

\[ \begin{aligned} {({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})}^{\top} &= {\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\,{({\mathbf{X}}^{\top})}^{\top} \\ &= {\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X} \end{aligned} \]

(Theorem 6 in Matrix Multiplication). When \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) is invertible, it appears in the least squares solution (Definition 4 in Solving Linear Systems and Least Squares, defined later in this chapter) \(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}= ({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\).

LemTheorem 1 (\({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) is invertible when \(\mathbf{X}\) has full column rank)

Theorem 1 (\({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) is invertible when \(\mathbf{X}\) has full column rank) If \(\mathbf{X}\) is an \(n \times p\) matrix with \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p\) (Definition 2 in Subspaces and Rank), then the \(p \times p\) matrix \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) is invertible.

ProofProof

Proof. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\) be a vector of length \(p\) with \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} 0 &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} && \text{(multiply } {\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\text{ on the left by } {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}}^{\top} \text{)} \\ &= {\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(transpose of a product)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(definition of the Euclidean norm)} \end{aligned} \]

so \(\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). Because \(\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} = c_1 (\text{column } 1) + \cdots + c_p (\text{column } p)\) and the columns of \(\mathbf{X}\) are linearly independent, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). So the only solution of \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), which says that the columns of the square matrix \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) are linearly independent, and a square matrix with linearly independent columns is invertible [3], Corollary 5.7, p. 143.

ExmExample 1 (Inverting \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\))

Example 1 (Inverting \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\)) For the rank-\(2\) matrix \(\mathbf{X}= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\) from Example 2 in Subspaces and Rank:

\[ {\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad {\mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\right)\mathclose{}}^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \]

and multiplying the two out gives \(\mathbf{I}_2\).

DefDefinition 2 (Hat matrix)

Definition 2 (Hat matrix) For an \(n \times p\) design matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) (Definition 1) with \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p\), the hat matrix [4] is the \(n \times n\) matrix

\[ \underbrace{\mathbf{H}}_{n \times n} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=} \underbrace{\mathbf{X}}_{n \times p} \underbrace{({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}}_{p \times p} \underbrace{{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}}_{p \times n} \]

The inverse exists by Theorem 1.

ExmExample 2 (The hat matrix of an intercept-only model)

Example 2 (The hat matrix of an intercept-only model) With \(n = 2\) observations and only an intercept, \(\mathbf{X}= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) (\(2 \times 1\), rank \(1\)), so \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}= 2\) and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot\frac{1}{2} \cdot\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

Then \(\mathbf{H}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= (\bar{y}, \bar{y})\), where \(\bar{y} = (y_1 + y_2)/2\): the fitted values of an intercept-only model are the sample mean.

LemTheorem 2 (Hat matrix is a projection matrix)

Theorem 2 (Hat matrix is a projection matrix) If \(\mathbf{X}\) is an \(n \times p\) design matrix with \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p\), then the hat matrix \(\mathbf{H}\) (Definition 2) is an orthogonal projection matrix (Definition 8 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace).

ProofProof

Proof. We verify symmetry and idempotency. Both use that \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\) is symmetric,

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} &= {\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\,{\mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \\ &= {\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X} \end{aligned} \]

(Theorem 6 in Matrix Multiplication), so its inverse is symmetric too (Corollary 1 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace).

Symmetry: \[\begin{aligned} {\mathbf{H}}^{\top} &= {\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{X}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} && \text{(definition of } \mathbf{H} \text{)} \\ &= {({\mathbf{X}}^{\top})}^{\top} \cdot {\mathopen{}\left(({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \cdot {\mathbf{X}}^{\top} && \text{(transpose of a product, twice)} \\ &= \mathbf{X}\cdot {\mathopen{}\left(({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \cdot {\mathbf{X}}^{\top} && \text{(transposing twice changes nothing)} \\ &= \mathbf{X}\cdot ({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1} \cdot {\mathbf{X}}^{\top} && \text{(the inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric)} \\ &= \mathbf{H} && \text{(definition of } \mathbf{H} \text{)} \end{aligned}\]

Idempotency: \[\begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}^2 &= \mathbf{X}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top} \cdot \mathbf{X}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top} && \text{(definition of } \mathbf{H} \text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{X}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top} && \text{(regroup; matrix multiplication is associative)} \\ &= \mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{I}_p\,({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top} && \text{(definition of the inverse)} \\ &= \mathbf{X}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top} && \text{(identity matrix)} \\ &= \mathbf{H} && \text{(definition of } \mathbf{H} \text{)} \end{aligned}\]

ExmExample 3 (The intercept-only hat matrix is a projection)

Example 3 (The intercept-only hat matrix is a projection) For \(\mathbf{H} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\) from Example 2, \({\mathbf{H}}^{\top} = \mathbf{H}\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}^2 &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \cdot 0.5 + 0.5 \cdot 0.5 & 0.5 \cdot 0.5 + 0.5 \cdot 0.5 \\ 0.5 \cdot 0.5 + 0.5 \cdot 0.5 & 0.5 \cdot 0.5 + 0.5 \cdot 0.5 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \mathbf{H}, \end{aligned} \]

as Theorem 2 says.

RemarkRemark 2 (Why it is called the hat matrix)

Remark 2 (Why it is called the hat matrix). The hat matrix gives the fitted values of the least squares solution (Definition 4 in Solving Linear Systems and Least Squares, defined later in this chapter):

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}} &= \mathbf{X}\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\\ &= \mathbf{X}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\\ &= \mathbf{H}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}. \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying by \(\mathbf{H}\) “puts a hat” on \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\), which is where the name comes from. In Example 2, \(\mathbf{H}\) puts a hat on \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) by replacing each \(y_i\) with the sample mean \(\bar{y}\).

1.1 Orthogonal projection onto a subspace

NoteSource

This section is adapted from the second half of Zhou [5], used under the MIT License (see the license text in Section  in Subspaces and Rank). It connects the orthogonal projection matrices of Definition 8 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace, defined by an algebraic property, to the geometric decomposition of Theorem 4 in Orthogonal Complements and the Fundamental Theorem.

DefDefinition 3 (Orthogonal projection onto a subspace)

Definition 3 (Orthogonal projection onto a subspace) Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\). By Theorem 4 in Orthogonal Complements and the Fundamental Theorem, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) for exactly one \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \in \mathcal{S}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \in \mathcal{S}^\perp\). The vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) onto \(\mathcal{S}\). It is the projection of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) onto \(\mathcal{S}\) along \(\mathcal{S}^\perp\) (Definition 4 in Sums and Direct Sums).

ExmExample 4 (Projecting onto a line in \(\mathbb{R}^3\))

Example 4 (Projecting onto a line in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)) In Example 5 in Orthogonal Complements and the Fundamental Theorem, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = (3, 1, 2)\) splits as \((2, 2, 0) + (1, -1, 2)\), with \((2, 2, 0) \in \mathcal{S} = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 1, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\) and \((1, -1, 2) \in \mathcal{S}^\perp\). So the orthogonal projection of \((3, 1, 2)\) onto \(\mathcal{S}\) is \((2, 2, 0)\). A vector already in \(\mathcal{S}\), such as \((5, 5, 0)\), is its own projection, since \((5, 5, 0) = (5, 5, 0) + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\in \mathcal{S}^\perp\) (Theorem 2 in Orthogonal Complements and the Fundamental Theorem, Theorem 1 in Subspaces and Rank); a vector of \(\mathcal{S}^\perp\), such as \((1, -1, 2)\), projects to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), since \((1, -1, 2) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}+ (1, -1, 2)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\in \mathcal{S}\) (Theorem 1 in Subspaces and Rank).

ExmExample 5 (A split that is not the orthogonal one)

Example 5 (A split that is not the orthogonal one) \((3, 1, 2) = (3, 3, 0) + (0, -2, 2)\) also writes \((3, 1, 2)\) as a vector of \(\mathcal{S} = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 1, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\) plus a remainder, but \((1, 1, 0) \cdot (0, -2, 2) = -2 \ne 0\), so the remainder is not in \(\mathcal{S}^\perp\), and \((3, 3, 0)\) is not the orthogonal projection of \((3, 1, 2)\) onto \(\mathcal{S}\).

LemTheorem 3 (The orthogonal projection is the closest point)

Theorem 3 (The orthogonal projection is the closest point) Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^p\), let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) be the orthogonal projection of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) onto \(\mathcal{S}\) (Definition 3). Then for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \in \mathcal{S}\),

\[ \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \le \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}, \]

with equality only when \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\).

ProofProof

Proof. By Definition 3, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \in \mathcal{S}^\perp\). Both \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\) are in \(\mathcal{S}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + (-1)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \in \mathcal{S}\) (Definition 4 in Subspaces and Rank), and therefore \((\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) \perp (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}})\) (Definition 1 in Orthogonal Complements and the Fundamental Theorem). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) + (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}})\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(add and subtract } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \text{)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 + \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(Pythagorean theorem, }\href{linear-algebra-inner-products.qmd#thm-norm-sum-square}{\text{Theorem~2 in Inner Products and the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality}}\text{)} \\ &\ge \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2, && \text{(} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 \ge 0 \text{, }\href{linear-algebra-inner-products.qmd#thm-norm-properties}{\text{Theorem~1 in Inner Products and the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

with equality exactly when \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} = 0\), that is, when \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) (Theorem 1 in Inner Products and the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality, part 1). Taking nonnegative square roots preserves the inequality.

LemTheorem 4 (Projecting with an orthonormal basis)

Theorem 4 (Projecting with an orthonormal basis) Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) with \(\dim(\mathcal{S}) = r \ge 1\), and let \(\mathbf{Q}\) be the \(p \times r\) matrix whose columns \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_r\) are an orthonormal basis of \(\mathcal{S}\) (Definition 1 in Orthonormal Bases, Outer Products and Hilbert Spaces, Corollary 1 in Orthonormal Bases, Outer Products and Hilbert Spaces). Then for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\), \(\mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) onto \(\mathcal{S}\), and \(\mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\) is an orthogonal projection matrix (Definition 8 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace).

ProofProof

Proof. \({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}_r\). Write \(q_{ki}\) for entry \(k\) of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i\), which is entry \((k, i)\) of \(\mathbf{Q}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} ({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q})_{ij} &= \sum_{k=1}^{p} ({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top})_{ik}\, q_{kj} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#def-matrix-mult}{\text{Definition~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^{p} q_{ki}\, q_{kj} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrices.qmd#def-matrix-transpose}{\text{Definition~6 in Matrices}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j, && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~10 in Vectors}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

which is \(1\) if \(i = j\) and \(0\) otherwise (Definition 28 in Vectors), so \({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}_r\) (Definition 7 in Matrix Multiplication).

The split. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = \mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\). \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = \mathbf{Q}\,({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\) (Theorem 1 in Matrix Multiplication) is in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{Q})\) (Definition 1 in Column Space and Null Space), which is \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_r\right\}\mathclose{} = \mathcal{S}\) (Theorem 1 in Column Space and Null Space). For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} &= {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) && \text{(substitute)} \\ &= {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-subspaces.qmd#thm-matvec-linear}{\text{Theorem~4 in Subspaces and Rank}}\text{, with coefficients } 1 \text{ and } -1 \text{)} \\ &= {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - ({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q})\,{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathbf{I}_r\,{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} && \text{(} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}_r \text{, from the first part of the proof)} \\ &= {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-identity}{\text{Theorem~4 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_r, && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \in \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top})\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}) &= \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{Q})^\perp \\ &= \mathcal{S}^\perp \end{aligned} \]

(Theorem 3 in Orthogonal Complements and the Fundamental Theorem). By the uniqueness in Theorem 4 in Orthogonal Complements and the Fundamental Theorem, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) onto \(\mathcal{S}\).

\(\mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\) is symmetric and idempotent.

\[ \begin{aligned} {(\mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top})}^{\top} &= {({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top})}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-transpose-product}{\text{Theorem~6 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}, && \text{(transposing twice, }\href{linear-algebra-matrices.qmd#def-matrix-transpose}{\text{Definition~6 in Matrices}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top})(\mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}) &= \mathbf{Q}\,({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q})\,{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{I}_r\,{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} && \text{(} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}_r \text{, from the first part of the proof)} \\ &= \mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}. && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-identity}{\text{Theorem~4 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 5 (A projection matrix projects onto its column space, and is the only one that does)

Theorem 5 (A projection matrix projects onto its column space, and is the only one that does)  

  1. If \(\mathbf{P}\) is a \(p \times p\) orthogonal projection matrix (Definition 8 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace), then for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\), \(\mathbf{P} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) onto \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{P})\) (Definition 3).
  2. If \(\mathbf{P}_1\) and \(\mathbf{P}_2\) are \(p \times p\) matrices such that, for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\), both \(\mathbf{P}_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) and \(\mathbf{P}_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) are the orthogonal projection of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) onto the same subspace \(\mathcal{S}\), then \(\mathbf{P}_1 = \mathbf{P}_2\).
ProofProof

Proof. Part 1. Write \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = \mathbf{P} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} + (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathbf{P} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\). The first term is in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{P})\) (Definition 1 in Column Space and Null Space). For the second,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{P}\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathbf{P} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) &= \mathbf{P} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathbf{P}\,(\mathbf{P} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-subspaces.qmd#thm-matvec-linear}{\text{Theorem~4 in Subspaces and Rank}}\text{, with coefficients } 1 \text{ and } -1 \text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{P} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - (\mathbf{P} \mathbf{P})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{P} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathbf{P}^2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-special-matrices.qmd#def-matrix-power}{\text{Definition~2 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{P} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathbf{P} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} && \text{(} \mathbf{P} \text{ is idempotent)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p, && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} - \mathbf{P} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} \in \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{P})\). Since \(\mathbf{P} = {\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{P}) &= \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{P}}^{\top}) \\ &= \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{P})^\perp \end{aligned} \]

(Theorem 3 in Orthogonal Complements and the Fundamental Theorem). By the uniqueness in Theorem 4 in Orthogonal Complements and the Fundamental Theorem, \(\mathbf{P} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) onto \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{P})\).

Part 2. The orthogonal projection of a vector onto \(\mathcal{S}\) is unique (Definition 3), so \(\mathbf{P}_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j = \mathbf{P}_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j\) for each indicator vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j\) (Definition 21 in Vectors). \(\mathbf{P}_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j\) is column \(j\) of \(\mathbf{P}_1\): by Theorem 3 in Subspaces and Rank it is the combination of the columns of \(\mathbf{P}_1\) with coefficients the entries of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j\), which are \(1\) in place \(j\) and \(0\) elsewhere (Definition 21 in Vectors). Likewise for \(\mathbf{P}_2\), so the two matrices have the same columns.

ExmExample 8 (Two routes to the same projection matrix)

Example 8 (Two routes to the same projection matrix) The matrix \(\mathbf{P} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) of Example 12 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace is an orthogonal projection matrix, and its column space is \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\), the horizontal axis. as part 2 and Theorem 4 require.

LemTheorem 6 (The hat matrix projects onto the column space of the design matrix)

Theorem 6 (The hat matrix projects onto the column space of the design matrix) If \(\mathbf{X}\) is an \(n \times p\) design matrix with \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p\), then for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in \mathbb{R}^n\), \(\mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) (Definition 2) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) onto \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\). So \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\) for any \(n \times p\) matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) whose columns are an orthonormal basis of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\).

ProofProof

Proof. \(\mathbf{H}\) is an orthogonal projection matrix (Theorem 2), so \(\mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) onto \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H})\) (Theorem 5, part 1). It remains to show \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H}) = \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\).

\(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H}) \subseteq \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\). \(\mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} = \mathbf{X}\,\mathopen{}\left(({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1} {\mathbf{X}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}\right)\mathclose{}\) (Theorem 1 in Matrix Multiplication), which is in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) (Definition 1 in Column Space and Null Space).

\(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}) \subseteq \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H})\). First,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H} \mathbf{X} &= \mathbf{X}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1} {\mathbf{X}}^{\top} \mathbf{X} && \text{(}\href{#def-hat-matrix}{\text{Definition~2}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{X}\,\mathopen{}\left(({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1} ({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X})\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{I}_p && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-special-matrices.qmd#def-matrix-inverse}{\text{Definition~5 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{X}. && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-identity}{\text{Theorem~4 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

So for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\), \(\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = (\mathbf{H} \mathbf{X})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\), since \(\mathbf{H} \mathbf{X}= \mathbf{X}\), and \((\mathbf{H} \mathbf{X})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = \mathbf{H}\,(\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}})\) (Theorem 1 in Matrix Multiplication), which is in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H})\).

\(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) has dimension \(p \ge 1\) (Theorem 1 in Rank-Nullity and Rank Factorization), so an orthonormal basis of it has \(p\) vectors (Definition 4 in Bases and Dimension), and \(\mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\) also gives the orthogonal projection onto \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) (Theorem 4, with \(n\) in place of \(p\) and \(r = p\)). By Theorem 5, part 2, \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\).

ExmExample 9 (The intercept-only hat matrix, from an orthonormal basis)

Example 9 (The intercept-only hat matrix, from an orthonormal basis) For the intercept-only design \(\mathbf{X}= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) of Example 2, \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}) = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 1)\right\}\mathclose{}\), with orthonormal basis \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1, 1)\), since

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}} \\ &= 1. \end{aligned} \]

With \(\mathbf{Q} = [\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1]\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} &= \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}, \end{aligned} \]

the hat matrix found in Example 2. The fitted values \((\bar{y}, \bar{y})\) are the closest point to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) on the line of constant vectors (Theorem 3), and the residuals \((y_1 - \bar{y}, y_2 - \bar{y})\) are orthogonal to \((1, 1)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} (1, 1) \cdot (y_1 - \bar{y}, y_2 - \bar{y}) &= y_1 + y_2 - 2\bar{y} \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

since \(2\bar{y} = y_1 + y_2\).
