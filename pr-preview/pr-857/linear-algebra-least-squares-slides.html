1 Solving linear systems and least squares

Source

This section is adapted from Zhou [1] and Zhou [2], used under the MIT License (see the license text in Section  in Subspaces and Rank). The first source states that every invertible matrix has an LU factorization; that is false without reordering rows (the \(2 \times 2\) row swap \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) has none), so this section states only how an LU factorization, when one exists, solves a system. The least squares results are proved here from the closest-point theorem (Theorem 3 in Design Matrices and Projections) rather than by differentiating, and the QR factorization is derived from Gram-Schmidt (Theorem 4 in Orthonormal Bases, Outer Products and Hilbert Spaces). These parts of the source are not part of this section:

  • the operation counts for the triangular solves, LU and QR
  • solving a system by inverting a matrix through its QR factorization, and the comparison of that with solving it directly
  • the pivoted factorization \(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{U}\), which Example 10 only touches

Definition 1 (Upper triangular matrix) A square matrix \(\mathbf{U}\) is upper triangular [3] if every entry below the diagonal is \(0\): \(u_{ij} = 0\) whenever \(i > j\).

Definition 2 (Lower triangular matrix) A square matrix \(\mathbf{L}\) is lower triangular if every entry above the diagonal is \(0\): \(\ell_{ij} = 0\) whenever \(i < j\).

Definition 3 (Unit lower triangular matrix) A lower triangular matrix (Definition 2) whose diagonal entries are all \(1\) is unit lower triangular.

Example 1 (Triangular and not)  

  • \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\) is upper triangular: its entries below the diagonal, in positions \((2, 1)\), \((3, 1)\) and \((3, 2)\), are \(0\).
  • \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) is unit lower triangular.
  • A diagonal matrix (Definition 4 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace) is both upper and lower triangular.
  • \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) is neither: its \((2, 1)\) entry is not \(0\), and neither is its \((1, 2)\) entry.

Theorem 1 (Solving a triangular system by substitution) Let \(\mathbf{U}\) be an \(n \times n\) upper triangular matrix (Definition 1) whose diagonal entries \(u_{11}, \ldots, u_{nn}\) are all nonzero. For every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \in \mathbb{R}^n\), the system \(\mathbf{U} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) has exactly one solution, given for \(i = n, n - 1, \ldots, 1\) in turn by

\[ x_i = \frac{1}{u_{ii}} \mathopen{}\left(b_i - \sum_{j=i+1}^{n} u_{ij}\,x_j\right)\mathclose{} \]

(back substitution). Likewise, if \(\mathbf{L}\) is lower triangular with nonzero diagonal, \(\mathbf{L} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) has exactly one solution, given for \(i = 1, \ldots, n\) by \(y_i = \frac{1}{\ell_{ii}} \mathopen{}\left(b_i - \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \ell_{ij}\,y_j\right)\mathclose{}\) (forward substitution).

Proof. Row \(i\) of \(\mathbf{U} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) reads

\[ \begin{aligned} b_i &= \sum_{j=1}^nu_{ij}\,x_j && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#def-matvec-mult}{\text{Definition~8 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=i}^{n} u_{ij}\,x_j && \text{(} u_{ij} = 0 \text{ for } j < i \text{)} \\ &= u_{ii}\,x_i + \sum_{j=i+1}^{n} u_{ij}\,x_j. && \text{(split off the } j = i \text{ term)} \end{aligned} \]

This equation is equivalent to each of

\[ \begin{aligned} u_{ii}\,x_i &= b_i - \sum_{j=i+1}^{n} u_{ij}\,x_j && \text{(subtract the sum from both sides)} \\ x_i &= \frac{1}{u_{ii}} \mathopen{}\left(b_i - \sum_{j=i+1}^{n} u_{ij}\,x_j\right)\mathclose{}. && \text{(divide by } u_{ii} \ne 0 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Row \(n\) involves only \(x_n\), so it fixes \(x_n\); once \(x_{i+1}, \ldots, x_n\) are fixed, row \(i\) fixes \(x_i\). So the rows, taken from the last to the first, hold exactly when \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the vector the formula builds: there is one solution, and only one. For \(\mathbf{L}\), row \(i\) reads \(\sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \ell_{ij}\,y_j + \ell_{ii}\,y_i = b_i\), and the same argument runs from the first row to the last.

Example 2 (Back substitution on a \(3 \times 3\) system) Solve \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\):

  1. \[ \begin{aligned} x_3 &= \frac{1}{-1} \cdot 1 \\ &= -1; \end{aligned} \]

  2. \[ \begin{aligned} x_2 &= \frac{1}{1/2} \mathopen{}\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot(-1)\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= 2 \cdot\tfrac{3}{2} \\ &= 3; \end{aligned} \]

  3. \[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= \frac{1}{2} \mathopen{}\left(8 - 1 \cdot 3 - (-1)(-1)\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 4 \\ &= 2. \end{aligned} \]

So \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = (2, 3, -1)\). Without a nonzero diagonal the method fails: \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = (1, 1)\) has no solution, since its second row reads \(0 = 1\).

Theorem 2 (QR factorization) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be an \(m \times n\) matrix with \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = n\). Then

\[ \underbrace{\mathbf{A}}_{m \times n} = \underbrace{\mathbf{Q}}_{m \times n}\,\underbrace{\mathbf{R}}_{n \times n}, \]

where the columns of \(\mathbf{Q}\) are orthonormal (Definition 28 in Vectors) and \(\mathbf{R}\) is upper triangular (Definition 1) with positive diagonal entries.

Proof. The \(n\) columns \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_n\) of \(\mathbf{A}\) are linearly independent (Definition 3 in Subspaces and Rank), so the Gram-Schmidt process (Definition 2 in Orthonormal Bases, Outer Products and Hilbert Spaces) runs all \(n\) steps and gives orthonormal \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_n\) (Theorem 4 in Orthonormal Bases, Outer Products and Hilbert Spaces, parts 1 and 3); let \(\mathbf{Q}\) have these columns. At step \(i\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), and rearranging the orthogonalize step,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i + \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j && \text{(add the sum to both sides of the orthogonalize step)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i\right\rVert\mathclose{}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i + \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j. && \text{(normalize step: } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i\right\rVert\mathclose{}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Let \(\mathbf{R}\) be the \(n \times n\) matrix with \(r_{ji} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i\) for \(j < i\), \(r_{ii} = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i\right\rVert\mathclose{} > 0\), and \(r_{ji} = 0\) for \(j > i\); it is upper triangular with positive diagonal. Column \(i\) of \(\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R}\) is \(\mathbf{Q}\) times column \(i\) of \(\mathbf{R}\) (Definition 1 in Matrix Multiplication), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{Q}\,(r_{1i}, \ldots, r_{ni}) &= \sum_{j=1}^nr_{ji}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-subspaces.qmd#thm-matvec-columns}{\text{Theorem~3 in Subspaces and Rank}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{i} r_{ji}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j && \text{(} r_{ji} = 0 \text{ for } j > i \text{)} \\ &= r_{ii}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i + \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} r_{ji}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j && \text{(split off the } j = i \text{ term)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i\right\rVert\mathclose{}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i + \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j && \text{(the entries of } \mathbf{R} \text{)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i. && \text{(the display)} \end{aligned} \]

So \(\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{A}\).

Example 3 (A QR factorization from Gram-Schmidt) For \(\mathbf{A}\) with columns \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 = (1, 1, 0)\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 = (1, 0, 1)\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_3 = (0, 1, 1)\), Example 6 in Orthonormal Bases, Outer Products and Hilbert Spaces and Example 8 in Orthonormal Bases, Outer Products and Hilbert Spaces found \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1, 1, 0)\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\,(1, -1, 2)\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\,(-1, 1, 1)\), with \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_1\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \sqrt{2}\), \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_2\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \sqrt{3/2}\), \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_3\right\rVert\mathclose{} = 2/\sqrt{3}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_3 \\ &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \end{aligned} \]

and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\). So

\[ \mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{2} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ 0 & \sqrt{3/2} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}} \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \end{bmatrix}. \]

As a check on the second column,

\[ \begin{aligned} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 + \sqrt{3/2}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 &= \tfrac{1}{2}\,(1, 1, 0) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,(1, -1, 2) \\ &= (1, 0, 1) \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2. \end{aligned} \]

Definition 4 (Least squares solution) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be \(m \times n\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \in \mathbb{R}^m\). A least squares solution [4] of \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) is a vector \(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) such that

\[ \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \le \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \quad \text{for every } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^n. \]

Example 4 (Fitting a constant) Let \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = (1, 3)\). The system \(x\,(1, 1) = (1, 3)\) is inconsistent (Definition 3 in Generalized Inverses and Linear Systems): its first entry needs \(x = 1\) and its second needs \(x = 3\). For any number \(x\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A} x\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 &= (1 - x)^2 + (3 - x)^2 \\ &= 2\,(x - 2)^2 + 2, \end{aligned} \]

which is smallest at \(x = 2\), so \(\hat{x} = 2\) is the least squares solution. The value \(x = 1\) is not one: it gives \(0 + 4 = 4 > 2\). The normal equations read \(2x = 4\), and their solution is \(x = 2\) too.

Definition 5 (Normal equations) For \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) as in Definition 4, the equations \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) are the normal equations [5].

Theorem 3 (Least squares solutions solve the normal equations) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be \(m \times n\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \in \mathbb{R}^m\).

  1. \(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\) is a least squares solution (Definition 4) exactly when it solves the normal equations \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\).
  2. The normal equations are consistent (Definition 3 in Generalized Inverses and Linear Systems).
  3. Every least squares solution gives the same fitted vector \(\mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\): the orthogonal projection of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) onto \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) (Definition 3 in Design Matrices and Projections).
  4. The least squares solution is unique exactly when \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = n\), and then \(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = ({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A})^{-1} {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\).

Proof. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) be the orthogonal projection of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) onto \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\). The vectors \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), as \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) ranges over \(\mathbb{R}^n\), are exactly the points of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) (Definition 1 in Column Space and Null Space). Least squares solutions are the solutions of \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\). If \(\mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\), then for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\), so \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \le \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\) (Theorem 3 in Design Matrices and Projections), and \(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\) is a least squares solution. Conversely, let \(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\) be a least squares solution. \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_0\) for some \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_0\) (Definition 1 in Column Space and Null Space), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} &\le \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_0\right\rVert\mathclose{} && \text{(}\href{#def-least-squares}{\text{Definition~4}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} && \text{(} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_0 \text{)} \\ &\le \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}, && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-projections.qmd#thm-closest-point}{\text{Theorem~3 in Design Matrices and Projections}}\text{, with } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}) \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

so the last inequality is an equality, and the equality case of Theorem 3 in Design Matrices and Projections gives \(\mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\).

Parts 1 and 3. \(\mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) holds exactly when \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})^\perp\): if \(\mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})^\perp\) (Definition 3 in Design Matrices and Projections); conversely, if \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})^\perp\), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} + (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}})\) is a split into \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) and \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})^\perp\), so \(\mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) by the uniqueness in Theorem 4 in Orthogonal Complements and the Fundamental Theorem. And \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})^\perp = \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\) (Theorem 3 in Orthogonal Complements and the Fundamental Theorem), where

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}) &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-generalized-inverses.qmd#thm-scalar-matmul}{\text{Theorem~1 in Generalized Inverses and Linear Systems}}\text{)} \\ &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}, && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

which is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_n\) exactly when the normal equations hold.

Part 2. The vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_0\) above satisfies \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_0 = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\), so it is a least squares solution, and it solves the normal equations by part 1.

Part 4. The least squares solutions are exactly the solutions of \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\), a consistent system, so there is exactly one when \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = n\) and more than one otherwise (Corollary 2 in Generalized Inverses and Linear Systems). When \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = n\), \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}\) is invertible (Theorem 1 in Design Matrices and Projections), and with \(\mathbf{M} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} &= \mathbf{I}_n\,\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-identity}{\text{Theorem~4 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= (\mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathbf{M})\,\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-special-matrices.qmd#def-matrix-inverse}{\text{Definition~5 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{M}^{-1}\,(\mathbf{M} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{M}^{-1}\,{\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}. && \text{(the normal equations)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 5 (A least squares line) Fit \(y = x_1 + x_2\,t\) to the points \((t, y) = (1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 2)\): \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\) (the \(\mathbf{X}\) of Example 1 in Design Matrices and Projections) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = (1, 2, 2)\). \(\mathbf{A}\) has rank \(2\) (Example 2 in Subspaces and Rank), so the solution is unique (part 4). \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}\) has inverse \(\frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\) (Example 1 in Design Matrices and Projections), and

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} &= (1 + 2 + 2,\; 1 + 4 + 6) \\ &= (5, 11), \end{aligned} \]

so

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} &= \frac{1}{6} \mathopen{}\left(14 \cdot 5 - 6 \cdot 11,\; -6 \cdot 5 + 3 \cdot 11\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= \frac{1}{6}\,(4, 3) \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\mathclose{}. \end{aligned} \]

The fitted values are \(\mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = \mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{7}{6}, \tfrac{5}{3}, \tfrac{13}{6}\right)\mathclose{}\), and the residuals \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = \mathopen{}\left(-\tfrac{1}{6}, \tfrac{1}{3}, -\tfrac{1}{6}\right)\mathclose{}\) are orthogonal to both columns of \(\mathbf{A}\): \(-\tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{3} - \tfrac{1}{6} = 0\) and \(-\tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{1}{2} = 0\).

Example 6 (Many least squares solutions, one fitted vector) With the rank-\(1\) matrix \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) (Example 2 in Subspaces and Rank) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = (1, 2, 2)\), \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 12 \end{bmatrix}\) and \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = (5, 10)\), so both normal equations say \(3 x_1 + 6 x_2 = 5\), that is, \(x_1 + 2 x_2 = \tfrac{5}{3}\). There are infinitely many least squares solutions, such as \((\tfrac{5}{3}, 0)\) and \((0, \tfrac{5}{6})\), as part 4 predicts for \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = 1 < 2\); but every one gives the same fitted vector

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} &= (x_1 + 2 x_2)\,(1, 1, 1) \\ &= \tfrac{5}{3}\,(1, 1, 1), \end{aligned} \]

as part 3 says.

Theorem 4 (Solving least squares by QR) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be \(m \times n\) with \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = n\), with QR factorization \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R}\) (Theorem 2), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \in \mathbb{R}^m\). The least squares solution (Definition 4) is the unique solution of

\[ \underbrace{\mathbf{R}}_{n \times n}\,\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = \underbrace{{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}}_{n \times m}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}, \]

which back substitution computes (Theorem 1). When \(m = n\), it is the unique solution of \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\).

Proof. \(\mathbf{R}\) is upper triangular with positive diagonal, so \(\mathbf{R} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) has exactly one solution \(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\) (Theorem 1). \({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}_n\), as in the proof of Theorem 4 in Design Matrices and Projections. Then

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} &= {(\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R})}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R})\,\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} && \text{(substitute } \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R} \text{)} \\ &= {\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R})\,\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-transpose-product}{\text{Theorem~6 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= {\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\,({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q})\,(\mathbf{R} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= {\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{I}_n\,(\mathbf{R} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}) && \text{(} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}_n \text{)} \\ &= {\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{R} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-identity}{\text{Theorem~4 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= {\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\,({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}) && \text{(} \mathbf{R} \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \text{)} \\ &= ({\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= {(\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R})}^{\top}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-transpose-product}{\text{Theorem~6 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}, && \text{(} \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

so \(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\) solves the normal equations and is the least squares solution (Theorem 3), which is unique because \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = n\). When \(m = n\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) &= n \\ &= m, \end{aligned} \]

so \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) is consistent for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) (Corollary 2 in Generalized Inverses and Linear Systems, part 1), so its solution leaves residual \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), is a least squares solution, and is therefore \(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\).

Example 7 (The least squares line by QR) For \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) of Example 5, Gram-Schmidt gives \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\,(1, 1, 1)\); then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 \cdot (1, 2, 3) &= \tfrac{6}{\sqrt{3}} \\ &= 2\sqrt{3}, \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_2 &= (1, 2, 3) - 2\,(1, 1, 1) \\ &= (-1, 0, 1) \end{aligned} \]

with norm \(\sqrt{2}\), and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(-1, 0, 1)\). So

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R} &= \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{3} & 2\sqrt{3} \\ 0 & \sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}, \\ {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} &= \mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{1 + 2 + 2}{\sqrt{3}},\; \tfrac{-1 + 0 + 2}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{5}{\sqrt{3}}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\mathclose{}. \end{aligned} \]

Back substitution gives

\[ \begin{aligned} x_2 &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2} \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{5}{\sqrt{3}} - 2\sqrt{3} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= \tfrac{5}{3} - 1 \\ &= \tfrac{2}{3}, \end{aligned} \]

the solution found in Example 5.

Theorem 5 (The Moore-Penrose inverse gives a least squares solution) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be \(m \times n\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \in \mathbb{R}^m\). Then \(\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) is a least squares solution of \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\), and a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is a least squares solution if and only if

\[ \mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}. \]

Proof. Let \(\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^+\). By conditions 3 and 1 of Definition 2 in Generalized Inverses and Linear Systems, \({\mathbf{P}}^{\top} = \mathbf{P}\) and \(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}\). Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P}) &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} - {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{P} && \text{(distributing)} \\ &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} - {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{P}}^{\top} && ({\mathbf{P}}^{\top} = \mathbf{P}) \\ &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} - {(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A})}^{\top} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-transpose-product}{\text{Theorem~6 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} - {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} && (\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}) \\ &= \mathbf{0}. \end{aligned} \]

For every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^n\), write \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) with

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{P}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = \mathbf{P}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}). \]

Their inner product is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} &= {(\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-transpose-product}{\text{Theorem~6 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= 0, && ({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P}) = \mathbf{0}) \end{aligned} \]

so \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 + \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2\). That is,

\[ \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{P}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 + \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2. \]

The first summand does not involve \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), and the second is nonnegative, with a zero at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\). So \(\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) minimizes \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\), and the smallest norm is \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{P}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\). A vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) attains that norm if and only if \(\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), which is \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\).

Theorem 6 (The Moore-Penrose inverse gives the least squares solution of smallest norm) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be \(m \times n\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \in \mathbb{R}^m\). If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is a least squares solution (Definition 4) of \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\), then

\[ \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 + \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} - \mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2. \]

So \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \ge \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\), with equality only if \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\).

Proof. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} - \mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\). By Theorem 5, \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\), so \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = {(\mathbf{A}^+)}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} &= \mathbf{A}^+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} && \text{(condition 2 of }\href{linear-algebra-generalized-inverses.qmd#def-moore-penrose}{\text{Definition~2 in Generalized Inverses and Linear Systems}}\text{)} \\ &= {(\mathbf{A}^+\mathbf{A})}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} && \text{(condition 4 of }\href{linear-algebra-generalized-inverses.qmd#def-moore-penrose}{\text{Definition~2 in Generalized Inverses and Linear Systems}}\text{)} \\ &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\,{(\mathbf{A}^+)}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-transpose-product}{\text{Theorem~6 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}. && \text{(definition of } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Therefore

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} && (\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \cdot \mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-transpose-product}{\text{Theorem~6 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= 0. && (\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) \end{aligned} \]

Expanding \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2\) then gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 + 2\,\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} + \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(expanding)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 + \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2. && (\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} = 0) \end{aligned} \]

Equality in \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \ge \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\) holds only if \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} = 0\), that is, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\).

Example 8 (A rank-deficient least squares problem) Let \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = (2, 3)\). By Example 5 in Generalized Inverses and Linear Systems, \(\mathbf{A}^+ = \mathbf{A}\), so \(\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = (2, 0)\) and \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = (2, 0)\).

By Theorem 5, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = (x_1, x_2)\) is a least squares solution if and only if \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = (x_1, 0) = (2, 0)\), that is, \(x_1 = 2\) with \(x_2\) arbitrary. Each of them leaves the same residual \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} - \mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = (0, 3)\), of norm \(3\).

For each such \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\),

\[ \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 = 4 + x_2^2 = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 + \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} - \mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2, \]

as Theorem 6 states. The smallest is \(4\), at \(x_2 = 0\), which is \(\mathbf{A}^+\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = (2, 0)\).

Definition 6 (LU factorization) An LU factorization [6] of an \(n \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) is a product \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{U}\) with \(\mathbf{L}\) unit lower triangular (Definition 3) and \(\mathbf{U}\) upper triangular (Definition 1).

Example 9 (An LU factorization of a \(3 \times 3\) matrix) Let \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ -3 & -1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -\frac{3}{2} & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) (unit lower triangular) and \(\mathbf{U} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\) (upper triangular). By Definition 1 in Matrix Multiplication, entry \((i, j)\) of \(\mathbf{L} \mathbf{U}\) is \(\sum_k \ell_{ik}\,u_{kj}\), so row \(i\) of \(\mathbf{L} \mathbf{U}\) is \(\sum_k \ell_{ik}\) times row \(k\) of \(\mathbf{U}\). Row 1 is \(1 \cdot(2, 1, -1) = (2, 1, -1)\); row 2 is \(-\tfrac{3}{2}\,(2, 1, -1) + (0, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}) = (-3, -1, 2)\), and row 3 is \(-(2, 1, -1) + 4\,(0, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}) + (0, 0, -1) = (-2, 1, 2)\). These are the rows of \(\mathbf{A}\), so \(\mathbf{L} \mathbf{U} = \mathbf{A}\) is an LU factorization. The below-diagonal entries of \(\mathbf{L}\) are the negatives of the multiples of earlier rows that Gaussian elimination adds to reduce \(\mathbf{A}\) to \(\mathbf{U}\).

Example 10 (An invertible matrix with no LU factorization) \(\mathbf{P} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) is invertible: \(\mathbf{P}^2 = \mathbf{I}_2\), so \(\mathbf{P}^{-1} = \mathbf{P}\) (Definition 5 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace); but it has no LU factorization. If \(\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{U}\), the \((1, 1)\) entry gives \(0 = 1 \cdot u_{11}\), so \(u_{11} = 0\), and then the \((2, 1)\) entry gives

\[ \begin{aligned} 1 &= \ell_{21}\,u_{11} \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

which is impossible. Swapping the two rows first removes the obstacle: the swapped matrix is \(\mathbf{I}_2 = \mathbf{I}_2\,\mathbf{I}_2\).

Theorem 7 (Solving a system with an LU factorization) If \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{U}\) is an LU factorization (Definition 6) and the diagonal entries of \(\mathbf{U}\) are all nonzero, then for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) the system \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) has exactly one solution: solve \(\mathbf{L} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) by forward substitution, then \(\mathbf{U} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) by back substitution (Theorem 1).

Proof. \(\mathbf{L}\) has diagonal entries \(1\) and \(\mathbf{U}\) has nonzero ones, so each of the two triangular systems has exactly one solution (Theorem 1). If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) are those solutions, then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= \mathbf{L}\,(\mathbf{U} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \\ &= \mathbf{L} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \end{aligned} \]

(Theorem 1 in Matrix Multiplication), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is a solution. Conversely, if \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathbf{U} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) satisfies

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{L} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} &= \mathbf{L}\,(\mathbf{U} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \\ &= (\mathbf{L} \mathbf{U})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \\ &= \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \end{aligned} \]

(Theorem 1 in Matrix Multiplication), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) is the unique solution of that system, and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is then the unique solution of \(\mathbf{U} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\).

Example 11 (Two triangular solves) With \(\mathbf{L}\) and \(\mathbf{U}\) of Example 9, solve \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = (8, -11, -3)\). Forward substitution on \(\mathbf{L} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = (8, -11, -3)\) gives \(y_1 = 8\),

\[ \begin{aligned} y_2 &= -11 + \tfrac{3}{2} \cdot 8 \\ &= 1, \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} y_3 &= -3 + 8 - 4 \cdot 1 \\ &= 1. \end{aligned} \]

Back substitution on \(\mathbf{U} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = (8, 1, 1)\) is Example 2, which gives \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = (2, 3, -1)\). As a check,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}\,(2, 3, -1) &= (4 + 3 + 1,\; -6 - 3 - 2,\; -4 + 3 - 2) \\ &= (8, -11, -3). \end{aligned} \]
