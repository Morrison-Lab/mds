Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace

Published

Last modified: 2026-11-19 23:44:37 (PST)

1 Special Matrices

Some special matrices appeared earlier:

  • the zero matrix (Definition 7 in Matrices), in the section on matrix addition
  • square matrices (Definition 3 in Matrix Multiplication), in the section on square and identity matrices
  • the identity matrix (Definition 7 in Matrix Multiplication), in that same section
DefDefinition 1 (Order of a square matrix)

Definition 1 (Order of a square matrix) The order of a square matrix (Definition 3 in Matrix Multiplication) is its number of rows, which equals its number of columns.

ExmExample 1 (Orders of square matrices)

Example 1 (Orders of square matrices) \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\) has order \(2\), and \([5]\) has order \(1\). A \(2 \times 3\) matrix has no order, since it is not square (Example 5 in Matrix Multiplication).

DefDefinition 2 (Matrix power)

Definition 2 (Matrix power) For a square matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) of order \(p\) and a positive integer \(k\), the \(k\)-th power of \(\mathbf{A}\) is:

\[\mathbf{A}^k = \underbrace{\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{A}\cdots\mathbf{A}}_{k \text{ copies}}\]

In particular, \(\mathbf{A}^2 = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}\).

ExmExample 2 (Powers of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix)

Example 2 (Powers of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix) Let \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}^2 &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 & 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 \\ 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of matrix multiplication)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, && \text{(multiply and add)} \\ \mathbf{A}^3 = \mathbf{A}^2 \mathbf{A} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0 & 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 \\ 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of matrix multiplication)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. && \text{(multiply and add)} \end{aligned} \]

A \(2 \times 3\) matrix \(\mathbf{B}\) is not square (Definition 3 in Matrix Multiplication), so \(\mathbf{B}^2\) is not defined; indeed \(\mathbf{B} \mathbf{B}\) would multiply a matrix with \(3\) columns by one with \(2\) rows, which Definition 1 in Matrix Multiplication does not allow.

DefDefinition 3 (Symmetric matrix)

Definition 3 (Symmetric matrix) A square matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) is symmetric [1] if \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} = \mathbf{A}\), i.e., \(a_{ij} = a_{ji}\) for all \(i\) and \(j\).

RemarkRemark 1 (Symmetric matrices in statistics)

Remark 1 (Symmetric matrices in statistics). Covariance matrices are symmetric: entry \((i, j)\) of the covariance matrix of a random vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{Y}}\vphantom{Y}}\) is \({\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(Y_i, Y_j\right)\mathclose{}}\), and \({\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(Y_i, Y_j\right)\mathclose{}} = {\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(Y_j, Y_i\right)\mathclose{}}\). For example, if \({\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y_1\right)\mathclose{}} = 4\), \({\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y_2\right)\mathclose{}} = 9\), and \({\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(Y_1, Y_2\right)\mathclose{}} = 1\), the covariance matrix of \((Y_1, Y_2)\) is

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 9 \end{bmatrix}. \]

ExmExample 3 (A matrix that is not symmetric)

Example 3 (A matrix that is not symmetric) \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\) is not symmetric:

\[ \begin{aligned} a_{12} &= 2 \\ &\ne 3 \\ &= a_{21}. \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 4 (Diagonal matrix)

Definition 4 (Diagonal matrix) A square matrix \(\mathbf{D}\) is a diagonal matrix [2] if all off-diagonal entries are zero: \(d_{ij} = 0\) whenever \(i \neq j\):

\[ \mathbf{D} = \begin{bmatrix} d_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & d_2 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & d_p \end{bmatrix} \]

RemarkRemark 2 (\(\operatorname{diag}\) notation)

Remark 2 (\(\operatorname{diag}\) notation). For numbers \(d_1, \ldots, d_p\), \(\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \ldots, d_p)\) is the \(p \times p\) diagonal matrix with diagonal entries \(d_1, \ldots, d_p\). For example,

\[ \operatorname{diag}(2, 5) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}, \]

and the \(p \times p\) identity matrix is \(\mathbf{I}_p = \operatorname{diag}(1, \ldots, 1)\).

ExmExample 4 (A matrix that is not diagonal)

Example 4 (A matrix that is not diagonal) \(\mathbf{D} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\) is not diagonal: its off-diagonal entry \(d_{12} = 1 \ne 0\).

DefDefinition 5 (Matrix inverse)

Definition 5 (Matrix inverse) For a square \(p \times p\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\), the inverse \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\) (if it exists) is the unique matrix satisfying:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{A}^{-1} &= \mathbf{A}^{-1}\,\mathbf{A} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 6 (Invertible matrix, non-singular matrix)

Definition 6 (Invertible matrix, non-singular matrix) A \(p \times p\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) is invertible [3] (or non-singular) if some \(p \times p\) matrix \(\mathbf{B}\) satisfies

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} &= \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p, \end{aligned} \]

where \(\mathbf{I}_p\) is the identity matrix (Definition 7 in Matrix Multiplication). A square matrix that is not invertible is singular.

ExmExample 5 (An invertible matrix and a singular one)

Example 5 (An invertible matrix and a singular one) The matrix \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) is invertible, with \(\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -0.5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\): multiplying out gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{-1} &= \mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{A} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_2. \end{aligned} \]

The matrix \(\mathbf{M} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) is singular: for any \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(\mathbf{C}\), the two rows of \(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{C}\) are equal, so \(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{C}\) can never be \(\mathbf{I}_2\), whose two rows differ.

RemarkRemark 3 (Invertible matrices and inverses)

Remark 3 (Invertible matrices and inverses). Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be a \(p \times p\) matrix, and suppose that two \(p \times p\) matrices \(\mathbf{B}\) and \(\mathbf{C}\) satisfy

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} &= \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}\mathbf{C} &= \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p. \end{aligned} \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{B} &= \mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{I}_p && \text{(identity matrix)} \\ &= \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}) && \text{(} \mathbf{A}\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{I}_p \text{)} \\ &= (\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})\mathbf{C} && \text{(matrix multiplication is associative)} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p\,\mathbf{C} && \text{(} \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{I}_p \text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{C} && \text{(identity matrix)} \end{aligned} \]

The steps use Theorem 1 in Matrix Multiplication and Theorem 4 in Matrix Multiplication. Since \(\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{C}\), at most one matrix satisfies these equations, which is the uniqueness that Definition 5 asserts. When \(\mathbf{A}\) is invertible, the matrix \(\mathbf{B}\) in Definition 6 is therefore the inverse \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\) of \(\mathbf{A}\), and \(\mathbf{A}\) is invertible exactly when it has an inverse. For example, for \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) in Example 5, \(\begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -0.5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) is the only \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(\mathbf{B}\) with

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} &= \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_2, \end{aligned} \]

so it is \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\).

LemTheorem 1 (Inverse of a product)

Theorem 1 (Inverse of a product) If \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are invertible \(p \times p\) matrices, then \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\) is invertible, and

\[(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^{-1} = \mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\]

ProofProof

Proof. Multiply \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\) by the candidate inverse on the right:

\[ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}) &= \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{-1})\mathbf{A}^{-1} && \text{(matrix multiplication is associative)} \\ &= \mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{I}_p\,\mathbf{A}^{-1} && \text{(definition of } \mathbf{B}^{-1} \text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{-1} && \text{(identity matrix)} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p && \text{(definition of } \mathbf{A}^{-1} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

and on the left:

\[ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1})(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}) &= \mathbf{B}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{A})\mathbf{B} && \text{(matrix multiplication is associative)} \\ &= \mathbf{B}^{-1}\,\mathbf{I}_p\,\mathbf{B} && \text{(definition of } \mathbf{A}^{-1} \text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{B} && \text{(identity matrix)} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p && \text{(definition of } \mathbf{B}^{-1} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

So \(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\) satisfies Definition 5 for \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\). The steps use Theorem 1 in Matrix Multiplication and Theorem 4 in Matrix Multiplication.

LemTheorem 2 (Inverse of a transpose)

Theorem 2 (Inverse of a transpose) If \(\mathbf{A}\) is an invertible \(p \times p\) matrix, then \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\) is invertible, and

\[{\mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}}^{-1} = {\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}\]

ProofProof

Proof. Multiply \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\) by the candidate inverse on each side:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\,{\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} &= {\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} && \text{(transpose of a product)} \\ &= {\mathbf{I}_p}^{\top} && \text{(definition of } \mathbf{A}^{-1} \text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p && \text{(} \mathbf{I}_p \text{ is symmetric)} \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{A}}^{\top} &= {\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} && \text{(transpose of a product)} \\ &= {\mathbf{I}_p}^{\top} && \text{(definition of } \mathbf{A}^{-1} \text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p && \text{(} \mathbf{I}_p \text{ is symmetric)} \end{aligned} \]

So \({\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}\) satisfies Definition 5 for \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\). The first step of each display is Theorem 6 in Matrix Multiplication.

ExmExample 6 (Inverting a transpose)

Example 6 (Inverting a transpose) For \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), Example 5 gives \(\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -0.5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), so Theorem 2 says

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}}^{-1} &= {\mathopen{}\left(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\right)\mathclose{}}^{-1} \\ &= {\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0 \\ -0.5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \end{aligned} \]

and multiplying \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0 \\ -0.5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) out does give \(\mathbf{I}_2\).

CorCorollary 1 (The inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric)

Corollary 1 (The inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric) If \(\mathbf{A}\) is symmetric and invertible, then \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\) is symmetric.

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} {\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} &= {\mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}}^{-1} && \text{(inverse of a transpose)} \\ &= \mathbf{A}^{-1} && \text{(} \mathbf{A} \text{ is symmetric)} \end{aligned} \]

The first step is Theorem 2.

ExmExample 7 (Inverting a symmetric matrix)

Example 7 (Inverting a symmetric matrix) \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) is symmetric, and \(\mathbf{S}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) is symmetric too, as Corollary 1 says.

LemTheorem 3 (An invertible factor can be canceled)

Theorem 3 (An invertible factor can be canceled) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be an invertible \(p \times p\) matrix (Definition 6), and let \(\mathbf{B}\) and \(\mathbf{C}\) be \(p \times n\) matrices. If \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}\), then \(\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{C}\).

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{B} &= \mathbf{I}_p\,\mathbf{B} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-identity}{\text{Theorem~4 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= (\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{B} && \text{(}\href{#def-matrix-inverse}{\text{Definition~5}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{A}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{A}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}) && \text{(} \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{C} \text{)} \\ &= (\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{C} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p\,\mathbf{C} && \text{(}\href{#def-matrix-inverse}{\text{Definition~5}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{C}. && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-identity}{\text{Theorem~4 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

\(\square\)

CorCorollary 2 (An invertible factor can be canceled on the right)

Corollary 2 (An invertible factor can be canceled on the right) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be an invertible \(p \times p\) matrix, and let \(\mathbf{B}\) and \(\mathbf{C}\) be \(n \times p\) matrices. If \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}\), then \(\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{C}\).

The proof of Theorem 3 works with every product reversed: multiply both sides on the right by \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\).

ExmExample 8 (Solving for a matrix by canceling an invertible factor)

Example 8 (Solving for a matrix by canceling an invertible factor) Let \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), which is invertible with \(\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -0.5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) (Example 5). If \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) for a \(2 \times 1\) matrix \(\mathbf{B}\), then multiplying both sides on the left by \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\), as in the proof of Theorem 3,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{B} &= \mathbf{A}^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

Check: \(\mathbf{A} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 1 \\ 0 + 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\).

ExmExample 9 (A product of two nonzero matrices can be the zero matrix)

Example 9 (A product of two nonzero matrices can be the zero matrix) With

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \]

neither matrix is the zero matrix (Definition 7 in Matrices), but

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 & 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 \\ 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \mathbf{0}_{2 \times 2}. \end{aligned} \]

For numbers, \(ab = 0\) forces \(a = 0\) or \(b = 0\). For matrices it does not.

Because \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{0}_{2 \times 2} = \mathbf{0}_{2 \times 2} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\) while \(\mathbf{B} \neq \mathbf{0}_{2 \times 2}\), the factor \(\mathbf{A}\) cannot be canceled from \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{0}_{2 \times 2}\). This does not contradict Theorem 3, because \(\mathbf{A}\) is singular: its second row is zero, so \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}\) has a zero second row for every \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(\mathbf{C}\), and \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}\) can never be \(\mathbf{I}_2\).

RemarkRemark 4 (Matrix arithmetic compared with scalar arithmetic)

Remark 4 (Matrix arithmetic compared with scalar arithmetic). Sums and differences. Matrix addition has every property that addition of numbers has: it is commutative (Theorem 3 in Matrices), associative (Theorem 4 in Matrices), has the zero matrix as its identity (Theorem 5 in Matrices), and gives each matrix a negative (Theorem 6 in Matrices). The only new condition is that \(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}\) is defined only when \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) have the same dimensions.

Products. Matrix multiplication keeps three properties of multiplication of numbers: it is associative (Theorem 1 in Matrix Multiplication), it is distributive over addition (Theorem 2 in Matrix Multiplication), and it has an identity, \(\mathbf{I}_p\) (Theorem 4 in Matrix Multiplication). It differs from multiplication of numbers in four ways:

  • \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\) is defined only when the number of columns of \(\mathbf{A}\) equals the number of rows of \(\mathbf{B}\) (Definition 1 in Matrix Multiplication).
  • It is not commutative: \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} \neq \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\) in general (Remark 1 in Matrix Multiplication).
  • A nonzero square matrix need not have an inverse (Example 5).
  • \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{0}\) can hold with \(\mathbf{A} \neq \mathbf{0}\) and \(\mathbf{B} \neq \mathbf{0}\) (Example 9), so a factor cannot be canceled from \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}\) unless it is invertible (Theorem 3).
DefDefinition 7 (Idempotent matrix)

Definition 7 (Idempotent matrix) A square matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) is idempotent [4] if

\[\mathbf{A}^2 = \mathbf{A}\]

ExmExample 10 (An idempotent matrix)

Example 10 (An idempotent matrix) For \(\mathbf{P} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{P}^2 &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 & 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 \\ 0 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of matrix multiplication)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \mathbf{P}, && \text{(multiply and add)} \end{aligned} \]

so \(\mathbf{P}\) is idempotent.

ExmExample 11 (A matrix that is not idempotent)

Example 11 (A matrix that is not idempotent) For \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}^2 &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot 0 & 2 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 \\ 0 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \\ &\ne \mathbf{A}, \end{aligned} \]

so \(\mathbf{A}\) is not idempotent.

DefDefinition 8 (Orthogonal projection matrix)

Definition 8 (Orthogonal projection matrix) A square matrix \(\mathbf{P}\) is an orthogonal projection matrix (also called an orthogonal projector) if it is both symmetric (Definition 3) and idempotent (Definition 7):

\[{\mathbf{P}}^{\top} = \mathbf{P} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mathbf{P}^2 = \mathbf{P}\]

DefDefinition 9 (Oblique projection)

Definition 9 (Oblique projection) A square matrix is an oblique projection if it is idempotent (Definition 7) but not symmetric (Definition 3).

ExmExample 12 (An orthogonal projection and an oblique one)

Example 12 (An orthogonal projection and an oblique one) \(\mathbf{P} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) is symmetric, and \(\mathbf{P}^2 = \mathbf{P}\), so \(\mathbf{P}\) is an orthogonal projection matrix; it maps \((v_1, v_2)\) to \((v_1, 0)\).

\(\mathbf{Q} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) is idempotent:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{Q}^2 &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 & 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 \\ 0 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \mathbf{Q}, \end{aligned} \]

but \({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \neq \mathbf{Q}\), so \(\mathbf{Q}\) is an oblique projection, not an orthogonal projection matrix.

RemarkRemark 5 (What “projection matrix” means in these notes)

Remark 5 (What “projection matrix” means in these notes). Some texts call any idempotent matrix a projection matrix, so that both \(\mathbf{P}\) and \(\mathbf{Q}\) in Example 12 would count as one. Regression texts often say “projection matrix” when they mean an orthogonal one. In these notes, “projection matrix” always means an orthogonal projection matrix in the sense of Definition 8, such as \(\mathbf{P}\) in Example 12, and never an oblique projection (Definition 9) such as \(\mathbf{Q}\).

LemTheorem 4 (Complement of a projection matrix)

Theorem 4 (Complement of a projection matrix) If \(\mathbf{P}\) is a \(p \times p\) orthogonal projection matrix (Definition 8), then \(\mathbf{I}_p - \mathbf{P}\) is also an orthogonal projection matrix.

ProofProof

Proof. We verify symmetry and idempotency.

Symmetry: \[ \begin{aligned} {(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P})}^{\top} &= {\mathbf{I}}^{\top} - {\mathbf{P}}^{\top} \\ &= \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P} \end{aligned} \]

Idempotency: \[\begin{aligned} (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P})^2 &= (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P}) \\ &= \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P} - \mathbf{P} + \mathbf{P}^2 \\ &= \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P} - \mathbf{P} + \mathbf{P} \\ &= \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P} \end{aligned}\]

LemTheorem 5 (Projection matrices produce orthogonal decompositions)

Theorem 5 (Projection matrices produce orthogonal decompositions) If \(\mathbf{P}\) is a \(p \times p\) orthogonal projection matrix (Definition 8) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) is any vector of length \(p\), then the two components of the decomposition

\[\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = \underbrace{\mathbf{P}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}_{\text{projected}} + \underbrace{(\mathbf{I}_p - \mathbf{P})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}_{\text{residual}}\]

are orthogonal:

\[\mathbf{P}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \;\perp\; (\mathbf{I}_p - \mathbf{P})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\]

ProofProof

Proof. \[\begin{aligned} {(\mathbf{P}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}})}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{P}\,(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{P} - \mathbf{P}^2)\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{P} - \mathbf{P})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{0}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \\ &= 0 \end{aligned}\]

where the second line uses symmetry (\({\mathbf{P}}^{\top} = \mathbf{P}\)) and the fourth line uses idempotency (\(\mathbf{P}^2 = \mathbf{P}\)).

LemTheorem 6 (The diagonal of an orthogonal projection matrix lies between 0 and 1)

Theorem 6 (The diagonal of an orthogonal projection matrix lies between 0 and 1) Let \(\mathbf{P}\) be an \(n \times n\) orthogonal projection matrix with entries \(p_{ij}\). Then

\[ 0 \le p_{ii} \le 1 \qquad \text{for every } i = 1, \dots, n. \]

ProofProof

Proof. Since \(\mathbf{P}\) is idempotent and symmetric, \(p_{ij} = p_{ji}\) and \(\mathbf{P}^2 = \mathbf{P}\). Compare the \((i, i)\) entries of \(\mathbf{P}^2\) and \(\mathbf{P}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} p_{ii} &= (\mathbf{P}^2)_{ii} && \text{(idempotent)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{n} p_{ij}\, p_{ji} && \text{(definition of matrix multiplication)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{n} p_{ij}^2 && \text{(symmetric)} \\ &= p_{ii}^2 + \sum_{j \ne i} p_{ij}^2. && \text{(separate the term } j = i \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Each \(p_{ij}^2\) is non-negative, so \(p_{ii} \ge 0\). The same equation also gives \(p_{ii} \ge p_{ii}^2\), so \(p_{ii}(1 - p_{ii}) \ge 0\). Since \(p_{ii} \ge 0\), this requires \(p_{ii} \le 1\).

ExmExample 13 (Diagonal of a projection onto a line)

Example 13 (Diagonal of a projection onto a line) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = (1, 1, 0)\). The matrix \(\mathbf{P} = \frac{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}^{\top}}{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}\) is the orthogonal projection onto the line through \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\). To see this, take the unit vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}/\sqrt{2}\), for which \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}}^{\top} = \mathbf{P}\), and apply projecting with an orthonormal basis:

\[ \mathbf{P} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Its diagonal is \((\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, 0)\). Each entry is between \(0\) and \(1\), as Theorem 6 says. The entry \(0\) belongs to a coordinate that \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) does not involve. For \(\mathbf{P} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) from Example 12 the diagonal is \((1, 0)\), so \(p_{ii}\) can be \(0\) and can be \(1\).

DefDefinition 10 (Orthogonal matrix)

Definition 10 (Orthogonal matrix) A \(p \times p\) matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) is orthogonal [5] if

\[ \underbrace{{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}}_{p \times p}\,\underbrace{\mathbf{Q}}_{p \times p} = \mathbf{I}_p \]

ExmExample 14 (A rotation matrix)

Example 14 (A rotation matrix) The matrix

\[ \mathbf{Q} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & -0.8 \\ 0.8 & 0.6 \end{bmatrix} \]

rotates each vector in the plane counterclockwise by the angle \(\theta\) with \(\cos\theta= 0.6\) and \(\sin\theta= 0.8\) (about \(53\) degrees) [6, Ch. 8], Example 8.1, p. 207. It is orthogonal:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\mathbf{Q} &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & 0.8 \\ -0.8 & 0.6 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & -0.8 \\ 0.8 & 0.6 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of the transpose)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.36 + 0.64 & -0.48 + 0.48 \\ -0.48 + 0.48 & 0.64 + 0.36 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of matrix multiplication)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 15 (Orthogonal columns are not enough)

Example 15 (Orthogonal columns are not enough) \(\mathbf{Q} = 2 \mathbf{I}_2\) has orthogonal columns \((2, 0)\) and \((0, 2)\), but

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q} &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \\ &\ne \mathbf{I}_2, \end{aligned} \]

so \(\mathbf{Q}\) is not an orthogonal matrix: its columns have norm \(2\), not \(1\).

RemarkRemark 6 (The columns of an orthogonal matrix are orthonormal)

Remark 6 (The columns of an orthogonal matrix are orthonormal). Entry \((i, j)\) of \({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\) is the dot product of column \(i\) and column \(j\) of \(\mathbf{Q}\), so \({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}_p\) says that the columns of \(\mathbf{Q}\) are orthonormal (Definition 28 in Vectors). For a square matrix, \({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}_p\) also implies \(\mathbf{Q}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} = \mathbf{I}_p\), so \(\mathbf{Q}^{-1} = {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\) (Definition 5) [6, Ch. 8], Theorem 8.1 and Definition 8.1, p. 209.

For example, the columns of \(\mathbf{Q}\) in Example 14 are \((0.6, 0.8)\) and \((-0.8, 0.6)\). The diagonal entries \(0.36 + 0.64 = 1\) of \({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\) are their squared norms, and the off-diagonal entries \(-0.48 + 0.48 = 0\) are their dot product. Multiplying in the other order also gives \(\mathbf{I}_2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{Q}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & -0.8 \\ 0.8 & 0.6 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & 0.8 \\ -0.8 & 0.6 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of the transpose)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.36 + 0.64 & 0.48 - 0.48 \\ 0.48 - 0.48 & 0.64 + 0.36 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of matrix multiplication)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

So \(\mathbf{Q}^{-1} = {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\).

LemTheorem 7 (Orthogonal matrices preserve length)

Theorem 7 (Orthogonal matrices preserve length) If \(\mathbf{Q}\) is a \(p \times p\) orthogonal matrix (Definition 10) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is a vector of length \(p\), then

\[ \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{Q}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \]

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{Q}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 &= (\mathbf{Q}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \cdot (\mathbf{Q}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(definition of the Euclidean norm)} \\ &= {(\mathbf{Q}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{Q}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(dot product as a matrix product)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{Q}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(transpose of a product)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\mathbf{Q})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(regroup; matrix multiplication is associative)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{I}_p\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(definition of an orthogonal matrix)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(identity matrix)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(definition of the Euclidean norm)} \end{aligned} \]

Both norms are nonnegative square roots, so equal squares give \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{Q}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\). The second step is Example 3 in Matrix Multiplication, the third is Theorem 6 in Matrix Multiplication, and the fourth is Theorem 1 in Matrix Multiplication.

ExmExample 16 (Rotating a vector keeps its length)

Example 16 (Rotating a vector keeps its length) With \(\mathbf{Q}\) from Example 14 and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (3, 4)\) from Example 18 in Vectors:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{Q}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 \cdot 3 - 0.8 \cdot 4 \\ 0.8 \cdot 3 + 0.6 \cdot 4 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of matrix-vector multiplication)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1.8 - 3.2 \\ 2.4 + 2.4 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} -1.4 \\ 4.8 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{Q}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= \sqrt{(-1.4)^2 + 4.8^2} && \text{(definition of the norm)} \\ &= \sqrt{1.96 + 23.04} && \text{(square)} \\ &= \sqrt{25} && \text{(add)} \\ &= 5 && \text{(take the square root)} \end{aligned} \]

which is \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} = 5\).

DefDefinition 11 (Bilinear form)

Definition 11 (Bilinear form) The bilinear form of a \(p \times p\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) [7] is the function \({B_{\mathbf{A}}}\) that maps each pair of vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) to the scalar

\[ {{B_{\mathbf{A}}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\right)\mathclose{}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}. \]

ExmExample 17 (A bilinear form that depends on the order of its vectors)

Example 17 (A bilinear form that depends on the order of its vectors)  

Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The bilinear form of \(\mathbf{A}\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} {{B_{\mathbf{A}}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\right)\mathclose{}} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(}\href{#def-bilinear-form}{\text{Definition~11}}\text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot(-1) \\ 0 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot(-1) \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply the matrix by } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply and add)} \\ &= 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot(-3) && \text{(multiply the row by the column)} \\ &= -5. && \text{(multiply and add)} \end{aligned} \]

With the vectors swapped, it is

\[ \begin{aligned} {{B_{\mathbf{A}}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{}} &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(}\href{#def-bilinear-form}{\text{Definition~11}}\text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 \\ 0 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 2 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply the matrix by } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply and add)} \\ &= 3 \cdot 5 + (-1) \cdot 6 && \text{(multiply the row by the column)} \\ &= 9. && \text{(multiply and add)} \end{aligned} \]

The two orders give different values.

DefDefinition 12 (Symmetric bilinear form)

Definition 12 (Symmetric bilinear form) A symmetric bilinear form [7] is a bilinear form \({B_{\mathbf{A}}}\) whose matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) is symmetric:

\[ {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} = \mathbf{A}. \]

LemTheorem 8 (A symmetric bilinear form does not depend on the order of its vectors)

Theorem 8 (A symmetric bilinear form does not depend on the order of its vectors) If \(\mathbf{A}\) is a \(p \times p\) symmetric matrix, then its bilinear form is the same with its vectors swapped, for all \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\):

\[ {{B_{\mathbf{A}}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{}} = {{B_{\mathbf{A}}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\right)\mathclose{}}. \]

ProofProof

Proof. Swapping the vectors relies on the fact that a \(1 \times 1\) product equals its own transpose, which justifies the step labeled with that fact:

\[ \begin{aligned} {{B_{\mathbf{A}}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{}} &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}^{\top}\, \mathbf{A}\, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(}\href{#def-bilinear-form}{\text{Definition~11}}\text{)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\, {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} && \text{(a } 1 \times 1 \text{ product equals its own transpose)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\, \mathbf{A}\, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} && \text{(}\href{#def-symmetric-matrix}{\text{Definition~3}}\text{)} \\ &= {{B_{\mathbf{A}}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\right)\mathclose{}}. && \text{(}\href{#def-bilinear-form}{\text{Definition~11}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 18 (A symmetric bilinear form)

Example 18 (A symmetric bilinear form)  

Let

\[ \mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The matrix \(\mathbf{S}\) is symmetric, because its two off-diagonal entries are both \(2\), so its bilinear form is a symmetric bilinear form. At \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\), that form is

\[ \begin{aligned} {{B_{\mathbf{S}}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\right)\mathclose{}} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(}\href{#def-bilinear-form}{\text{Definition~11}}\text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot(-1) \\ 2 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot(-1) \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply the matrix by } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply and add)} \\ &= 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 5 && \text{(multiply the row by the column)} \\ &= 11. && \text{(multiply and add)} \end{aligned} \]

With the vectors swapped, it is

\[ \begin{aligned} {{B_{\mathbf{S}}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{}} &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(}\href{#def-bilinear-form}{\text{Definition~11}}\text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 \\ 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply the matrix by } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply and add)} \\ &= 3 \cdot 5 + (-1) \cdot 4 && \text{(multiply the row by the column)} \\ &= 11, && \text{(multiply and add)} \end{aligned} \]

the same value, as Theorem 8 says it must be. By contrast, \(\mathbf{A}\) in Example 17 is not symmetric, so its bilinear form is not a symmetric bilinear form, and swapping the vectors changed its value from \(-5\) to \(9\).

DefDefinition 13 (Quadratic form)

Definition 13 (Quadratic form) A quadratic form [8] is a mathematical expression of the structure

\[{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\, \mathbf{S}\, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\]

where \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is a \(p \times 1\) vector and \(\mathbf{S}\) is a \(p \times p\) matrix.

RemarkRemark 7 (Quadratic forms in statistics)

Remark 7 (Quadratic forms in statistics). A quadratic form extends the scalar expression \(c x^2\) to vectors: with \(p = 1\) and \(\mathbf{S} = [c]\), \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{S}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= c x^2\). With \(p = 2\),

\[ {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = x_1^2 + 2 x_1 x_2 + 3 x_2^2, \]

which is \(1 + 4 + 12 = 17\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (1, 2)\).

Quadratic forms occur often in statistics:

  • The residual sum of squares in linear regression (see Vector Calculus) is a quadratic form.
  • The variance of a linear combination of estimates (see Inference about Gaussian Linear Regression Models) is a quadratic form: \({\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\right)\mathclose{}} = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,{\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\right)\mathclose{}}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).
RemarkRemark 8 (A quadratic form is a bilinear form with equal vectors)

Remark 8 (A quadratic form is a bilinear form with equal vectors).

Setting \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) in the bilinear form of \(\mathbf{S}\) gives the quadratic form of \(\mathbf{S}\):

\[ {{B_{\mathbf{S}}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{}} = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}. \]

LemTheorem 9 (A quadratic form is additive in its matrix)

Theorem 9 (A quadratic form is additive in its matrix) If \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are \(p \times p\) matrices, \(c\) is a number and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is a vector of length \(p\), then

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}&= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \\ {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}(c\,\mathbf{A})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}&= c\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}. \end{aligned} \]

So for a fixed \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), the map \(\mathbf{S} \mapsto {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is linear in \(\mathbf{S}\).

ProofProof

Proof. By the distributive law (Theorem 2 in Matrix Multiplication), applied on the left and then on the right,

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= \mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\mathbf{A} + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\right)\mathclose{}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(distribute } {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \text{ over the sum)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(distribute } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\text{ over the sum)} \end{aligned} \]

For the scalar, a number can be moved out of any product, so \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}(c\,\mathbf{A})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= c\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).

ExmExample 19 (Combining two quadratic forms into one)

Example 19 (Combining two quadratic forms into one) Take \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{I}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (1, 2)\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= 1 + 4 + 12 = 17, \\ {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{I} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= 1 + 4 = 5, \\ {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{I})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\\ &= 2 + 4 + 16 \\ &= 22, \end{aligned} \]

which equals \(17 + 5\). In particular, \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}(\mathbf{A} + c\,\mathbf{I})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} + c\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2\) for every number \(c\), because \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{I} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2\). This is how two quadratic terms in the same vector combine into a single quadratic form.

DefDefinition 14 (Symmetric part of a square matrix)

Definition 14 (Symmetric part of a square matrix) The symmetric part of a \(p \times p\) matrix \(\mathbf{S}\) is

\[\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{S} + {\mathbf{S}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}\]

ExmExample 20 (The symmetric part of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix)

Example 20 (The symmetric part of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix) For \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\), the symmetric part is

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\left( \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \right)\mathclose{} &= \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \end{aligned} \]

which is symmetric.

LemTheorem 10 (A quadratic form depends only on the symmetric part)

Theorem 10 (A quadratic form depends only on the symmetric part) If \(\mathbf{S}\) is a \(p \times p\) matrix and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is a vector of length \(p\), then

\[ {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\mathopen{}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{S}+{\mathbf{S}}^{\top})\right)\mathclose{}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}. \]

So the value of a quadratic form depends only on the symmetric part (Definition 14) of \(\mathbf{S}\).

ProofProof

Proof. The quadratic form \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is a \(1 \times 1\) matrix, so it equals its own transpose:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= {\mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} && \text{(a } 1 \times 1 \text{ matrix is symmetric)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{S}}^{\top}\,{\mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} && \text{(transpose of a product, twice)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{S}}^{\top}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(transposing twice changes nothing)} \end{aligned} \]

Averaging the two expressions for \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= \frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{S}}^{\top}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(average of two equal numbers)} \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{S} + {\mathbf{S}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(distributive law)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\mathopen{}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{S}+{\mathbf{S}}^{\top})\right)\mathclose{}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(move the scalar } \tfrac{1}{2} \text{ inside)} \end{aligned} \]

The distributive step is Theorem 2 in Matrix Multiplication, and the transpose step is Theorem 6 in Matrix Multiplication.

ExmExample 21 (Replacing a matrix by its symmetric part)

Example 21 (Replacing a matrix by its symmetric part) With \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\) from Example 20 and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (1, 1)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= 1 + 2 + 0 + 3 \\ &= 6 \\ {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= 1 + 1 + 1 + 3 \\ &= 6 \end{aligned} \]

Both quadratic forms equal the sum of their matrix’s entries, because every entry of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is \(1\).

3 Trace and Matrix Inner Product

DefDefinition 15 (Trace)

Definition 15 (Trace) The trace [9] of a \(p \times p\) matrix \(\mathbf{M}\) is the sum of its diagonal entries:

\[\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{M}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^pM_{ii}\]

ExmExample 22 (The trace of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix)

Example 22 (The trace of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix) \[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\right)\mathclose{}} &= 1 + 4 \\ &= 5 \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 11 (The trace of a product does not depend on the order)

Theorem 11 (The trace of a product does not depend on the order) For an \(m \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) and an \(n \times m\) matrix \(\mathbf{B}\):

\[ {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}_{m \times m}\right)\mathclose{}} = {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}}_{n \times n}\right)\mathclose{}} \]

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}) &= \sum_{i=1}^{m} (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})_{ii} && \text{(definition of the trace)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{s=1}^{n} a_{is}\, b_{si} && \text{(definition of matrix multiplication)} \\ &= \sum_{s=1}^{n} \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_{is}\, b_{si} && \text{(swap the order of two finite sums)} \\ &= \sum_{s=1}^{n} \sum_{i=1}^{m} b_{si}\, a_{is} && \text{(multiplication of numbers is commutative)} \\ &= \sum_{s=1}^{n} (\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})_{ss} && \text{(definition of matrix multiplication)} \\ &= \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}) && \text{(definition of the trace)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 23 (Traces of the two products of a \(2 \times 3\) and a \(3 \times 2\) matrix)

Example 23 (Traces of the two products of a \(2 \times 3\) and a \(3 \times 2\) matrix) Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

The products are

\[ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 5 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

The trace needs only the diagonal entries. For \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\), which is \(2 \times 2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}) &= (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})_{11} + (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})_{22} && \text{(definition of the trace)} \\ &= (1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot 0) + (0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 1) && \text{(definition of matrix multiplication)} \\ &= (1 + 4 + 0) + (0 + 1 + 3) && \text{(multiply)} \\ &= 5 + 4 && \text{(add within each entry)} \\ &= 9 && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

For \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\), which is \(3 \times 3\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}) &= (\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})_{11} + (\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})_{22} + (\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})_{33} && \text{(definition of the trace)} \\ &= (1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0) + (2 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1) + (0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 3) && \text{(definition of matrix multiplication)} \\ &= (1 + 0) + (4 + 1) + (0 + 3) && \text{(multiply)} \\ &= 1 + 5 + 3 && \text{(add within each entry)} \\ &= 9 && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

The products have different sizes, but their traces agree.

RemarkRemark 9 (Moving a factor of a triple product)

Remark 9 (Moving a factor of a triple product). Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be \(m \times n\), \(\mathbf{B}\) be \(n \times k\), and \(\mathbf{C}\) be \(k \times m\), so that \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\) is \(m \times k\) and \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}\) is square. Applying Theorem 11 to the \(m \times k\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\) and the \(k \times m\) matrix \(\mathbf{C}\) moves the last factor of the triple product to the front [6, Ch. 1], Theorem 1.5 and eq. 1.17, p. 19:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}) &= {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left((\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\,\mathbf{C}\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(matrix multiplication is associative)} \\ &= {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{C}\,(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(the theorem, applied to the two factors } \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} \text{ and } \mathbf{C} \text{)} \\ &= \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}) && \text{(matrix multiplication is associative)} \end{aligned} \]

For example, let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

Then

\[ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 1 \\ 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 & 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1 \\ 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 & 1 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

so

\[ (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\,\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 1 & 3 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0 \\ 1 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad (\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 & 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 \\ 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

and both traces are the same:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}) &= 2 + 1 \\ &= 3 \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}) &= 1 + 2 \\ &= 3. \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 12 (The trace of an outer product is a dot product)

Theorem 12 (The trace of an outer product is a dot product) For two vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) with the same number \(n\) of entries, the trace of the outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\) and the dot product \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) are the same number:

\[ {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}}_{n \times n}\right)\mathclose{}} = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \]

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}_{ii} && \text{(definition of the trace)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i v_i && \text{(entries of an outer product)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} && \text{(definition of the dot product)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 24 (The trace of an outer product)

Example 24 (The trace of an outer product) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} = (1, 2)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = (3, 4)\), the remark on outer and dot products found the outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top}\) and the dot product \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = 11\). The trace of the outer product is

\[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}} &= {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix}\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(the outer product found in the remark)} \\ &= 3 + 8 && \text{(definition of the trace)} \\ &= 11 && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

The trace and the dot product are the same number, as Theorem 12 says.

CorCorollary 3 (The trace of a self outer product is a squared norm)

Corollary 3 (The trace of a self outer product is a squared norm) For a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), the trace of the self outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\) and the square of the Euclidean norm \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\) are the same number:

\[ {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}} = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 \]

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}} &= {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(definition of the self outer product)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(the trace of an outer product is a dot product)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(the Euclidean norm from the dot product, squared)} \end{aligned} \]

The second step is Theorem 12, with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) in place of both \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\). The third step takes the square of both sides of the formula for the Euclidean norm from the dot product.

ExmExample 25 (The trace of a self outer product)

Example 25 (The trace of a self outer product) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (1, 2)\), the example of a self outer product found \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\). Its trace and the squared Euclidean norm of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) are

\[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}} &= 1 + 4 && \text{(definition of the trace)} \\ &= 5 && \text{(add)} \\ &= 1^2 + 2^2 && \text{(write each diagonal entry as a square)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(definition of the Euclidean norm, squared)} \end{aligned} \]

as Corollary 3 says.

DefDefinition 16 (Matrix inner product)

Definition 16 (Matrix inner product) The inner product of two \(n \times p\) matrices \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) is

\[ {\mathopen{}\left\langle \mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}\right\rangle\mathclose{}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=} {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\underbrace{{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{B}}_{p \times p}\right)\mathclose{}} \]

Banerjee and Roy [6, Ch. 15], eq. 15.7, p. 491 defines the same inner product on \(n \times p\) matrices with the trace.

LemTheorem 13 (The matrix inner product multiplies matching entries)

Theorem 13 (The matrix inner product multiplies matching entries) For two \(n \times p\) matrices \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\):

\[ {\mathopen{}\left\langle \mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}\right\rangle\mathclose{}} = \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^pa_{ij}\, b_{ij} \]

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} {\mathopen{}\left\langle \mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}\right\rangle\mathclose{}} &= \operatorname{tr}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{B}) && \text{(definition of the inner product)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^p({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{B})_{jj} && \text{(definition of the trace)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^p\sum_{i=1}^n({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{ji}\, b_{ij} && \text{(definition of matrix multiplication)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^p\sum_{i=1}^na_{ij}\, b_{ij} && \text{(definition of the transpose)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^pa_{ij}\, b_{ij} && \text{(swap the order of two finite sums)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 26 (The inner product of two \(2 \times 2\) matrices)

Example 26 (The inner product of two \(2 \times 2\) matrices) Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

From Definition 16:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathopen{}\left\langle \mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}\right\rangle\mathclose{}} &= {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(definition of the inner product)} \\ &= {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left( {\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}}^{\top} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(substitute)} \\ &= {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left( \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(definition of the transpose)} \\ &= {\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\left(\begin{bmatrix} -3 & 7 \\ -4 & 10 \end{bmatrix}\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(multiply)} \\ &= -3 + 10 && \text{(definition of the trace)} \\ &= 7 && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

From Theorem 13:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathopen{}\left\langle \mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}\right\rangle\mathclose{}} &= 1 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot(-1) + 4 \cdot 2 && \text{(multiply matching entries and add)} \\ &= 0 + 2 - 3 + 8 && \text{(multiply)} \\ &= 7 && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

RemarkRemark 10 (The matrix inner product as a dot product)

Remark 10 (The matrix inner product as a dot product). Theorem 13 says that the matrix inner product is the dot product (Definition 10 in Vectors) of the two matrices’ entries, each listed as one vector of length \(np\), with both matrices’ entries listed in the same order.

For example, listing the entries of \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) in Example 26 row by row gives \((1, 2, 3, 4)\) and \((0, 1, -1, 2)\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} (1, 2, 3, 4) \cdot (0, 1, -1, 2) &= 7 \\ &= {\mathopen{}\left\langle \mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}\right\rangle\mathclose{}}. \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 17 (Frobenius norm)

Definition 17 (Frobenius norm) The Frobenius norm [10] of an \(n \times p\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) is

\[ \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert\mathclose{}_F \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sqrt{{\mathopen{}\left\langle \mathbf{A}, \mathbf{A}\right\rangle\mathclose{}}} \]

where \({\mathopen{}\left\langle \cdot, \cdot\right\rangle\mathclose{}}\) is the matrix inner product (Definition 16).

ExmExample 27 (The Frobenius norm of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix)

Example 27 (The Frobenius norm of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix) For \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert\mathclose{}_F &= \sqrt{{\mathopen{}\left\langle \mathbf{A}, \mathbf{A}\right\rangle\mathclose{}}} && \text{(definition of the Frobenius norm)} \\ &= \sqrt{1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 3 + 4 \cdot 4} && \text{(sum of products of matching entries)} \\ &= \sqrt{1 + 4 + 9 + 16} && \text{(multiply)} \\ &= \sqrt{30} && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

The second step is Theorem 13.

RemarkRemark 11 (The Frobenius norm is the Euclidean norm of the entries)

Remark 11 (The Frobenius norm is the Euclidean norm of the entries). By Theorem 13, \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert\mathclose{}_F^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^pa_{ij}^2\), a sum of squares, so the square root is always defined. \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert\mathclose{}_F\) is the Euclidean norm (Definition 23 in Vectors) of the entries of \(\mathbf{A}\), listed as one vector of length \(np\) [6, Ch. 15], Definition 15.4, p. 492.

For example, listing the entries of \(\mathbf{A}\) in Example 27 row by row gives the vector \((1, 2, 3, 4)\) of length \(2 \cdot 2 = 4\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert(1, 2, 3, 4)\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= \sqrt{1 + 4 + 9 + 16} \\ &= \sqrt{30} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert\mathclose{}_F. \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 14 (The squared Frobenius norm adds up the squared column norms)

Theorem 14 (The squared Frobenius norm adds up the squared column norms) If \(\mathbf{A}\) is an \(n \times p\) matrix with columns \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_p\), then

\[ \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert\mathclose{}_F^2 = \sum_{j=1}^p\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2. \]

ProofProof

Proof. Entry \(i\) of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j\) is \(a_{ij}\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert\mathclose{}_F^2 &= \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^pa_{ij}^2 && \text{(}\href{#rem-frobenius-norm-entries}{\text{Remark~11}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^p\sum_{i=1}^na_{ij}^2 && \text{(swap the order of two finite sums)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^p\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-euclidean-norm}{\text{Definition~23 in Vectors}}\text{, applied to each column)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 28 (The Frobenius norm of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix, column by column)

Example 28 (The Frobenius norm of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix, column by column) The matrix \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\) in Example 27 has columns \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 = (1, 3)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 = (2, 4)\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert\mathclose{}_F^2 &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 + \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(}\href{#thm-frobenius-norm-columns}{\text{Theorem~14}}\text{)} \\ &= (1^2 + 3^2) + (2^2 + 4^2) && \text{(definition of the norm)} \\ &= 10 + 20 && \text{(square and add)} \\ &= 30 && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

which matches \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert\mathclose{}_F = \sqrt{30}\) from Example 27.
