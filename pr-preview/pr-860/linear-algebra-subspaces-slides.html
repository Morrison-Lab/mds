Subspaces and Rank

Last modified: 2026-11-19 18:41:16 (PST)

1 Rank

Definition 1 (Linearly independent vectors) Vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\) of the same length \(p\) are linearly independent [1] if the only numbers \(c_1, \ldots, c_k\) with

\[c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + c_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\]

are

\[ \begin{aligned} c_1 &= \cdots \\ &= c_k \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

Vectors that are not linearly independent are linearly dependent.

Example 1 (Independent and dependent pairs of vectors)  

  • \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 = (1, 0)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2 = (1, 1)\) are linearly independent: \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + c_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2 = (c_1 + c_2, c_2)\), which is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) only if \(c_2 = 0\) and then \(c_1 = 0\).
  • \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 = (1, 2)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2 = (2, 4)\) are not: \(2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2 = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

Exercise 1 (Testing whether two features and their total are linearly independent) A table of data that stores two features and also their total holds redundant information, which the test below detects. Let

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_3 = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Are \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_3\) linearly independent?

Hint

Compare \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_3\) with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2\).

Solution 1. No. \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2 = {(1, 3, 1)}^{\top} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_3\), so

\[ 1 \cdot\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + 1 \cdot\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2 + (-1) \cdot\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_3 = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}, \]

with numbers \(c_1 = 1\), \(c_2 = 1\) and \(c_3 = -1\) that are not all \(0\). By Definition 1, the three vectors are linearly dependent.

Definition 2 (Rank) The rank [2] of a matrix \(\mathbf{A}\), written \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\), is the largest number of columns of \(\mathbf{A}\) that are linearly independent (Definition 1).

Example 2 (The rank of two \(3 \times 2\) matrices) The matrix \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\) has rank \(2\): if \(c_1 (1, 1, 1) + c_2 (1, 2, 3) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), then subtracting the first entry from the second gives \(c_2 = 0\), and then \(c_1 = 0\).

The matrix \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) has rank \(1\): its second column is twice its first, so the two columns are not linearly independent, but the first column on its own is.

Definition 3 (Full column rank) An \(n \times p\) matrix has full column rank if its rank (Definition 2) is \(p\), so that all of its columns are linearly independent.

Remark 1 (Full column rank needs at least as many rows as columns). In Example 2, the first matrix has full column rank: its rank is \(2\), and it has \(2\) columns. The second does not, because its rank is \(1\).

An \(n \times p\) matrix can have full column rank only when \(p \le n\), because more than \(n\) vectors of length \(n\) are never linearly independent. For example, the \(2 \times 3\) matrix \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) has \(3\) columns of length \(2\), and \(1 \cdot(1, 0) + 1 \cdot(0, 1) - 1 \cdot(1, 1) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), with coefficients that are not all zero. So its \(3\) columns are not linearly independent, and its rank is less than \(3\).

Exercise 2 (Finding the rank of a \(3 \times 3\) matrix) The rank of a data matrix counts how many of its columns carry new information. Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 3\end{bmatrix}. \]

Find \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}}\).

Hint

Add the first two columns of \(\mathbf{A}\) and compare them with the third. Then check whether the first two are linearly independent.

Solution 2. \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}} = 2\). The third column of \(\mathbf{A}\) adds up the first two: \({(1, 0, 2)}^{\top} + {(0, 1, 1)}^{\top} = {(1, 1, 3)}^{\top}\), so the three are not linearly independent. The first two are: if \(c_1 {(1, 0, 2)}^{\top} + c_2 {(0, 1, 1)}^{\top} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), then the first entry gives \(c_1 = 0\), and the second gives \(c_2 = 0\). By Definition 2, the rank is \(2\).

Exercise 3 (Testing whether an all-ones column and one feature have full column rank) Regression software often puts an all-ones column in the input matrix, beside the features. With one feature, measured at \(2\), \(4\) and \(6\), the input matrix is

\[ \mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 1 & 4 \\ 1 & 6\end{bmatrix}. \]

Does \(\mathbf{X}\) have full column rank?

Hint

By Definition 3, check whether the two columns of \(\mathbf{X}\) are linearly independent.

Solution 3. Yes. Suppose \(c_1 {(1, 1, 1)}^{\top} + c_2 {(2, 4, 6)}^{\top} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). The first two entries give

\[ c_1 + 2 c_2 = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad c_1 + 4 c_2 = 0. \]

Subtracting the first equation from the second gives \(2 c_2 = 0\), so \(c_2 = 0\), and then \(c_1 = 0\). So the two columns of \(\mathbf{X}\) are linearly independent, the rank of \(\mathbf{X}\) is \(2\), and \(\mathbf{X}\) has full column rank.

Definition 4 (Subspace, linear subspace) A set \(\mathcal{S}\) of vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^p\) is a subspace [4] of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) if it is not empty and it is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication:

  1. if \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \in \mathcal{S}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \in \mathcal{S}\), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \in \mathcal{S}\);
  2. if \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \in \mathcal{S}\) and \(c \in \mathbb{R}\), then \(c \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \in \mathcal{S}\).

A subspace is also called a linear subspace.

Example 3 (A line through the origin is a subspace) Let \(\mathcal{S} = \mathopen{}\left\{c\,(1, 2) : c \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{}\), the line in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) through \((0, 0)\) and \((1, 2)\).

  • Addition: \(a\,(1, 2) + b\,(1, 2) = (a + b)\,(1, 2)\), which is in \(\mathcal{S}\).
  • Scalar multiplication: \(c\,\mathopen{}\left(a\,(1, 2)\right)\mathclose{} = (ca)\,(1, 2)\), which is in \(\mathcal{S}\).

So \(\mathcal{S}\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Two other subspaces of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) are the set \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\) that holds only the zero vector, and \(\mathbb{R}^p\) itself.

Example 4 (A line that misses the origin is not a subspace) Let \(\mathcal{T} = \mathopen{}\left\{(x, 1) : x \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{}\), the horizontal line in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) at height \(1\). The vectors \((0, 1)\) and \((2, 1)\) are in \(\mathcal{T}\), but their sum \((2, 2)\) is not, because its second entry is \(2\), not \(1\). So \(\mathcal{T}\) is not closed under addition, and it is not a subspace.

Exercise 4 (Testing whether vectors with a nonnegative second entry are a subspace) A constraint such as “this entry is at least \(0\)” cuts out a set of vectors, and it is worth knowing whether that set is a subspace. Let

\[ \mathcal{H} = \mathopen{}\left\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : y \ge 0\right\}\mathclose{}, \]

the vectors whose second entry is nonnegative.

Is \(\mathcal{H}\) a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)?

Hint

Check condition 2 of Definition 4 with \(c = -1\).

Solution 4. No. The vector \({(0, 1)}^{\top}\) is in \(\mathcal{H}\), but \((-1) \cdot{(0, 1)}^{\top} = {(0, -1)}^{\top}\) is not, because its second entry is negative. So \(\mathcal{H}\) fails condition 2 of Definition 4, and it is not a subspace. \(\mathcal{H}\) does satisfy condition 1, which shows that one condition alone is not enough.

Theorem 1 (Every subspace contains the zero vector) If \(\mathcal{S}\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (Definition 4), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\in \mathcal{S}\).

Proof. A subspace is not empty, so it contains some vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\). Closure under scalar multiplication with \(c = 0\) puts \(0 \cdot\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) in \(\mathcal{S}\), and \(0 \cdot\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

Example 5 (Using the zero vector to rule out a subspace) The plane \(\mathopen{}\left\{(x, y, z) : x + y + z = 1\right\}\mathclose{}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) does not contain \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}= (0, 0, 0)\), because \(0 + 0 + 0 = 0 \neq 1\). So, by Theorem 1, it is not a subspace. The line \(\mathcal{T}\) in Example 4 fails the same test: \((0, 0)\) is not in \(\mathcal{T}\), because its second entry is not \(1\).

Containing \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is necessary but not sufficient. The union of the two coordinate axes in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), \(\mathopen{}\left\{(x, 0) : x \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{} \cup \mathopen{}\left\{(0, y) : y \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{}\), contains \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), but \((1, 0) + (0, 1) = (1, 1)\) lies on neither axis, so the union is not closed under addition and is not a subspace.

Exercise 5 (Testing whether \(p_1 + p_2 = 1\) defines a subspace) A coin toss can land heads or tails, and the probabilities \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) of those two results satisfy \(p_1 + p_2 = 1\). That condition defines the set

\[ \mathcal{P} = \mathopen{}\left\{(p_1, p_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : p_1 + p_2 = 1\right\}\mathclose{}. \]

Is \(\mathcal{P}\) a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)?

Hint

Use Theorem 1.

Solution 5. No. The zero vector \({(0, 0)}^{\top}\) is not in \(\mathcal{P}\), because \(0 + 0 = 0 \neq 1\). By Theorem 1, every subspace contains \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), so \(\mathcal{P}\) is not a subspace.

Definition 5 (Span) The span [5] of vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k \in \mathbb{R}^p\) is the set of all their linear combinations:

\[ \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\right\}\mathclose{} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=} \mathopen{}\left\{c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + c_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k : c_1, \ldots, c_k \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{}. \]

The vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\) span a set \(\mathcal{S}\) if their span equals \(\mathcal{S}\).

By convention, the span of the empty list (\(k = 0\)) is \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\), and the empty list counts as linearly independent (Definition 1), since it has no coefficients that could fail to be zero.

Example 6 (Two spans in \(\mathbb{R}^3\))  

  • \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\) is the set of vectors \((c_1, c_2, 0)\), the plane \(z = 0\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).
  • \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 2, 3), (2, 4, 6)\right\}\mathclose{}\) is only the line \(\mathopen{}\left\{c\,(1, 2, 3) : c \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{}\), because \((2, 4, 6) = 2\,(1, 2, 3)\), so \(c_1 (1, 2, 3) + c_2 (2, 4, 6) = (c_1 + 2 c_2)\,(1, 2, 3)\). Adding a vector to a list does not always enlarge its span.

Exercise 6 (Testing whether a vector lies in the span of two others) Whether a target can be written as a linear combination of a few given vectors is a question about a span. Let

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = \begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 5 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}. \]

Is the vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\) in \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2\right\}\mathclose{}\)?

Hint

Look for numbers \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) with \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + c_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2 = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\), one entry at a time.

Solution 6. Yes. Entry by entry, \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + c_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2 = {(c_1,\ c_1 + c_2,\ c_2)}^{\top}\). Matching \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\), the first entry gives \(c_1 = 3\) and the third gives \(c_2 = 2\). Then the second entry is \(3 + 2 = 5\), which matches too, so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = 3 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + 2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2\) is in the span.

Theorem 2 (A span is a subspace) For any vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k \in \mathbb{R}^p\), \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\right\}\mathclose{}\) (Definition 5) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (Definition 4).

Proof. The span is not empty: it contains \(0 \cdot\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + 0 \cdot\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

Addition. Take two vectors in the span, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = a_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + a_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = b_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + b_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} &= (a_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + a_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k) + (b_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + b_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k) && \text{(substitute the two combinations)} \\ &= (a_1 + b_1)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + (a_k + b_k)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k && \text{(regroup, and distribute each } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_i \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

which is a linear combination of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\), so it is in the span.

Scalar multiplication. For \(c \in \mathbb{R}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} c\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} &= c\,(a_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + a_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k) && \text{(substitute the combination)} \\ &= (c a_1)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + (c a_k)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k && \text{(distribute } c \text{ over the sum)} \end{aligned} \]

which is again in the span.

Example 7 (The line in Example 3 is a span) The line \(\mathcal{S} = \mathopen{}\left\{c\,(1, 2) : c \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{}\) in Example 3 is \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 2)\right\}\mathclose{}\), so Theorem 2 gives a second proof that it is a subspace. Likewise, the plane \(z = 0\) in Example 6 is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\), because it is \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\).

Exercise 7 (Recognizing a set of vectors as a span) Describing a set by free parameters often shows that it is a span. Let

\[ \mathcal{S} = \mathopen{}\left\{(a + b,\ a - b,\ 2a) : a, b \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{}. \]

Is \(\mathcal{S}\) a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)?

Hint

Write \((a + b,\ a - b,\ 2a)\) as \(a\) times one vector plus \(b\) times another, then use Theorem 2.

Solution 7. Yes. For every \(a, b \in \mathbb{R}\),

\[ \begin{bmatrix}a + b \\ a - b \\ 2a\end{bmatrix} = a \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} + b \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}, \]

so \(\mathcal{S} = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{{(1, 1, 2)}^{\top}, {(1, -1, 0)}^{\top}\right\}\mathclose{}\). By Theorem 2, \(\mathcal{S}\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Theorem 3 (A matrix-vector product combines the columns) If \(\mathbf{A}\) is an \(m \times n\) matrix with columns \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_n\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^n\), then

\[ \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = x_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + \cdots + x_n \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_n. \]

Proof. Compare entry \(i\) of the two sides, for each \(i = 1, \ldots, m\). Entry \(i\) of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j\) is \(a_{ij}\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})_i &= a_{i1} x_1 + \cdots + a_{in} x_n && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#def-matvec-mult}{\text{Definition~8 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= x_1 a_{i1} + \cdots + x_n a_{in} && \text{(multiplication of numbers is commutative)} \\ &= (x_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + \cdots + x_n \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_n)_i && \text{(entry } i \text{ of each } x_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j \text{ is } x_j a_{ij} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 8 (A product as a combination of columns) \[ \begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} &= 1 \cdot\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + (-1) \cdot\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(}\href{#thm-matvec-columns}{\text{Theorem~3}}\text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

the same answer as computing each entry as a row-times-vector dot product (Remark 3 in Matrix Multiplication).

Exercise 8 (Computing a matrix-vector product as a combination of columns) A linear model’s predictions for every observation at once are a matrix-vector product \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), and Theorem 3 writes it as a combination of the columns of \(\mathbf{A}\). Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}. \]

Find \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) with Theorem 3.

Hint

Use Theorem 3 with \(x_1 = 3\) and \(x_2 = 2\).

Solution 8. By Theorem 3,

\[ \mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = 3 \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} + 2 \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 3 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}6 + 2 \\ 0 + 6 \\ 3 - 2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}8 \\ 6 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Theorem 4 (A matrix maps a combination to the same combination of images) If \(\mathbf{A}\) is an \(m \times n\) matrix, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_k \in \mathbb{R}^n\) and \(c_1, \ldots, c_k \in \mathbb{R}\), then

\[ \mathbf{A}\,(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_1 + \cdots + c_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_k) = c_1\,\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_1 + \cdots + c_k\,\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_k. \]

Proof. The function \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\mapsto \mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is a linear map by Theorem 2 in Linear and Affine Maps, and a linear map preserves linear combinations by Theorem 3 in Linear and Affine Maps. Applying that theorem with \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_i = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_i\) gives the identity.

Example 9 (Applying a matrix to a combination) Let \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_1 = (1, 0)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_2 = (0, 1)\). Then \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_1 = (1, 3)\) and \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_2 = (2, 4)\), and for the combination \(2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_1 - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_2 = (2, -1)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}\,(2, -1) &= (1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot(-1),\; 3 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot(-1)) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#def-matvec-mult}{\text{Definition~8 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= (0, 2) && \text{(arithmetic)} \\ &= 2\,(1, 3) - (2, 4), && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

which is \(2\,\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_1 - \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_2\), as Theorem 4 says.

Exercise 9 (Applying a matrix to a combination without knowing the matrix) Theorem 4 lets a matrix-vector product of a combination be found from the matrix-vector product of each part, even when the matrix itself is not known. Suppose that a matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) satisfies

\[ \mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Find \(\mathbf{A}\,(3 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_1 + 2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_2)\).

Hint

Use Theorem 4 with \(c_1 = 3\) and \(c_2 = 2\).

Solution 9. By Theorem 4,

\[ \mathbf{A}\,(3 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_1 + 2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}_2) = 3 \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} + 2 \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}. \]

Theorem 5 (More than \(p\) vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^p\) are linearly dependent) If \(k > p\), then any \(k\) vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k \in \mathbb{R}^p\) are not linearly independent (Definition 1): some numbers \(c_1, \ldots, c_k\), not all zero, satisfy \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + c_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p\).

Proof. Induction on \(p\).

Base case, \(p = 1\). Each \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_i\) is a single number \(v_i\), and \(k \ge 2\). If \(v_1 = 0\), take \(c_1 = 1\) and every other \(c_i = 0\). Otherwise take \(c_1 = v_2\), \(c_2 = -v_1\) and every other \(c_i = 0\); then \(c_2 \neq 0\), and \(v_2 v_1 - v_1 v_2 = 0\).

Inductive step. Let \(p \ge 2\), assume the theorem holds for vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^{p-1}\), and take \(k > p\) vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^p\). Write \(v_{i,p}\) for the last entry of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_i\).

Case 1: every \(v_{i,p} = 0\). Deleting the last entry gives \(k > p - 1\) vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^{p-1}\), which by the inductive hypothesis have a combination with coefficients not all zero that equals \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p-1}\). The same coefficients combine the original vectors to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p\), since every last entry is \(0\).

Case 2: some \(v_{i,p} \neq 0\). Renumber the vectors so that \(v_{k,p} \neq 0\), and for \(i = 1, \ldots, k-1\) let

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}_i \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_i - \frac{v_{i,p}}{v_{k,p}}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k. \]

The last entry of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}_i\) is \(v_{i,p} - \frac{v_{i,p}}{v_{k,p}}\,v_{k,p} = 0\). Deleting that zero last entry gives \(k - 1 > p - 1\) vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^{p-1}\), so by the inductive hypothesis there are numbers \(c_1, \ldots, c_{k-1}\), not all zero, whose combination of the shortened vectors is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p-1}\). Putting the zero last entries back gives \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}_1 + \cdots + c_{k-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}_{k-1} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p &= c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}_1 + \cdots + c_{k-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}_{k-1} && \text{(choice of } c_1, \ldots, c_{k-1} \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} c_i \mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_i - \frac{v_{i,p}}{v_{k,p}}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(definition of } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}_i \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} c_i \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_i - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \frac{c_i v_{i,p}}{v_{k,p}}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k && \text{(distribute each } c_i \text{, and distribute the sum over the difference)} \\ &= c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + c_{k-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_{k-1} - \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \frac{c_i v_{i,p}}{v_{k,p}}\right)\mathclose{}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k, && \text{(factor } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k \text{ out of the second sum)} \end{aligned} \]

a combination of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\) equal to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p\) whose first \(k - 1\) coefficients are not all zero.

Example 10 (Three vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)) Theorem 5 says the three vectors \((1, 0)\), \((0, 1)\) and \((1, 1)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) are linearly dependent, and indeed \(1 \cdot(1, 0) + 1 \cdot(0, 1) - 1 \cdot(1, 1) = (0, 0)\). The theorem says nothing about \(k \le p\) vectors: \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\) are linearly independent, while \((1, 2)\) and \((2, 4)\) are not (Example 1).

Exercise 10 (Testing four vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) for linear independence) A table of data with more features than observations has more columns than each column has entries, and Theorem 5 settles such cases without any arithmetic. Let

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 5 \\ 6\end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_3 = \begin{bmatrix}7 \\ 8 \\ 10\end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_4 = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Are these four vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) linearly independent?

Hint

Compare the number of vectors with \(p = 3\) in Theorem 5.

Solution 10. No. There are \(k = 4\) vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and \(4 > 3\), so by Theorem 5 they are linearly dependent.
