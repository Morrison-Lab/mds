Sums, Products and Quotients

Published

Last modified: 2026-11-20 12:51:52 (PST)

1 Sums

DefDefinition 1 (Term of a sum)

Definition 1 (Term of a sum) In a sum \(a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n\), each of the numbers or expressions \(a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n\) being added is a term of the sum.

ExmExample 1 (Terms of a sum)

Example 1 (Terms of a sum)  

  • The sum \(3 + 5 + 9\) has three terms: \(3\), \(5\) and \(9\).
  • The sum \(x^2 - 3x + 7 = x^2 + (-3x) + 7\) has three terms: \(x^2\), \(-3x\) and \(7\). A subtracted expression counts as a term with a minus sign.
DefDefinition 2 (Identity element)

Definition 2 (Identity element) Let \(S\) be a set, such as the real numbers \(\mathbb{R}\), and let \(\star\) be an operation that combines two elements \(a\) and \(b\) of \(S\) into an element \(a \star b\) of \(S\), such as addition (\(a + b\)) or multiplication (\(a \times b\)) of real numbers. An element \(u\) of \(S\) is an identity element for \(\star\) [1] if \(a \star u = a\) and \(u \star a = a\) for every \(a \in S\).

ExmExample 2 (Identity elements for addition and multiplication)

Example 2 (Identity elements for addition and multiplication)  

  • \(0\) is the identity element for addition: for example, \(5 + 0 = 5\) and \(0 + 5 = 5\) (Theorem 1).
  • \(1\) is the identity element for multiplication: for example, \(5 \times 1 = 5\) and \(1 \times 5 = 5\) (Theorem 4).
  • \(0\) is not an identity element for subtraction: \(5 - 0 = 5\), but \(0 - 5 = -5 \ne 5\).
DefDefinition 3 (Commutative operation)

Definition 3 (Commutative operation) An operation \(\star\) on a set \(S\) (Definition 2) is commutative [2] if \(a \star b = b \star a\) for all \(a, b \in S\): the order of the two inputs does not matter. Some sources call a commutative operation symmetric, as in the theorem titles “Sums are symmetric” and “Products are symmetric”.

ExmExample 3 (A commutative operation, and one that is not)

Example 3 (A commutative operation, and one that is not)  

  • Addition is commutative: for example,

    \[ \begin{aligned} 2 + 5 &= 7 \\ &= 5 + 2. \end{aligned} \]

  • Subtraction is not commutative: \(5 - 3 = 2\), but \(3 - 5 = -2\).

DefDefinition 4 (Associative operation)

Definition 4 (Associative operation) An operation \(\star\) on a set \(S\) (Definition 2) is associative [3] if \((a \star b) \star c = a \star (b \star c)\) for all \(a, b, c \in S\): which pair is combined first does not matter.

ExmExample 4 (An associative operation, and one that is not)

Example 4 (An associative operation, and one that is not)  

  • Multiplication is associative: for example,

    \[ \begin{aligned} (2 \times 3) \times 4 &= 6 \times 4 \\ &= 24 \end{aligned} \]

    and

    \[ \begin{aligned} 2 \times (3 \times 4) &= 2 \times 12 \\ &= 24. \end{aligned} \]

  • Subtraction is not associative:

    \[ \begin{aligned} (8 - 4) - 2 &= 4 - 2 \\ &= 2, \end{aligned} \]

    but

    \[ \begin{aligned} 8 - (4 - 2) &= 8 - 2 \\ &= 6. \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 1 (Adding zero changes nothing)

Theorem 1 (Adding zero changes nothing) \[a+0=a\]

LemTheorem 2 (Sums are symmetric)

Theorem 2 (Sums are symmetric) \[a+b = b+a\]

LemTheorem 3 (Sums are associative)

Theorem 3 (Sums are associative) When adding three numbers, it does not matter which pair you add first:

\[(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)\]

ExmExample 5 (Grouping a sum two ways)

Example 5 (Grouping a sum two ways) \[ \begin{aligned} (2 + 3) + 4 &= 5 + 4 \\ &= 9, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} 2 + (3 + 4) &= 2 + 7 \\ &= 9. \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 5 (Products are symmetric)

Theorem 5 (Products are symmetric) \[a \times b = b \times a\]

Theorem 6 (Products are associative) \[(a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c)\]

4 Sums and products together

DefDefinition 5 (Distributes over)

Definition 5 (Distributes over) An operation \(\star\) on a set \(S\) distributes over an operation \(\diamond\) on \(S\) if

\[a \star (b \diamond c) = (a \star b) \diamond (a \star c)\]

for all \(a, b, c \in S\).

ExmExample 6 (Addition does not distribute over multiplication)

Example 6 (Addition does not distribute over multiplication) \[ \begin{aligned} 2 + (3 \times 4) &= 2 + 12 \\ &= 14, \end{aligned} \]

but

\[ \begin{aligned} (2 + 3) \times (2 + 4) &= 5 \times 6 \\ &= 30. \end{aligned} \]

So Definition 5 fails with \(\star = +\) and \(\diamond = \times\).

LemTheorem 8 (Multiplication is distributive)

Theorem 8 (Multiplication is distributive) \[a(b+c) = ab + ac\]

DefDefinition 6 (Distributive law)

Definition 6 (Distributive law) The distributive law [4] is the statement that multiplication distributes over addition (Definition 5): \(a \times (b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)\) (Theorem 8). We then say multiplication is distributive.

ExmExample 7 (Multiplication distributes over addition)

Example 7 (Multiplication distributes over addition) \[ \begin{aligned} 3 \times (4 + 5) &= 3 \times 9 \\ &= 27, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} (3 \times 4) + (3 \times 5) &= 12 + 15 \\ &= 27. \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 7 (Like terms)

Definition 7 (Like terms) Two terms of a sum are like terms [5] if they are the same product of variables, each to the same power, possibly multiplied by different constants: \(c_1 m\) and \(c_2 m\), where \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are constants and \(m\) is that product.

DefDefinition 8 (Collecting like terms)

Definition 8 (Collecting like terms) To collect like terms (Definition 7) is to replace their sum by one term, using the distributive law (Theorem 8): \(c_1 m + c_2 m = (c_1 + c_2) m\).

ExmExample 8 (Like and unlike terms)

Example 8 (Like and unlike terms)  

  • \(3ab\) and \(-5ab\) are like terms; collecting them gives

    \[ \begin{aligned} 3ab + (-5ab) &= (3 - 5) ab \\ &= -2ab. \end{aligned} \]

  • \(2x\) and \(2x^2\) are not like terms: \(x\) appears to different powers.

ExrExercise 1 (Expand a squared sum)

Exercise 1 (Expand a squared sum) Is \((3 + 4)^2\) equal to \(3^2 + 4^2\)? Then expand \((a + b)^2\) for any numbers \(a\) and \(b\), using only the distributive law and the rules above.

SolutionSolution 1

Solution 1. No:

\[ \begin{aligned} (3 + 4)^2 &= 7^2 \\ &= 49, \end{aligned} \]

while

\[ \begin{aligned} 3^2 + 4^2 &= 9 + 16 \\ &= 25. \end{aligned} \]

The difference, \(49 - 25 = 24\), is \(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4\).

To see where that extra term comes from, write the square as a product and apply the distributive law (Theorem 8) twice:

\[ \begin{aligned} (a + b)^2 &= (a + b)(a + b) && \text{(definition of a square)} \\ &= (a + b)\,a + (a + b)\,b && \text{(distributive law)} \\ &= (a^2 + ba) + (ab + b^2) && \text{(distributive law, twice)} \\ &= a^2 + ab + ab + b^2 && \text{(commutative and associative laws)} \\ &= a^2 + 2ab + b^2 && \text{(collect like terms, }\href{#def-collect-like-terms}{\text{Definition~8}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

The step “commutative and associative laws” uses Theorem 5 to write \(ba\) as \(ab\), and Theorem 3 to drop the parentheses.

LemTheorem 9 (Product of two sums (FOIL))

Theorem 9 (Product of two sums (FOIL)) For any numbers \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) and \(d\),

\[ (a + b)(c + d) = ac + ad + bc + bd. \]

The four products pair the First terms (\(ac\)), the Outer terms (\(ad\)), the Inner terms (\(bc\)) and the Last terms (\(bd\)), which gives this rule its name, FOIL.

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} (a + b)(c + d) &= (a + b)\,c + (a + b)\,d && \text{(distributive law, }\href{#thm-mult-distr}{\text{Theorem~8}}\text{)} \\ &= (ac + bc) + (ad + bd) && \text{(distributive law, twice)} \\ &= ac + ad + bc + bd && \text{(commutative and associative laws)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 9 (Checking FOIL)

Example 9 (Checking FOIL) With \(a = 1\), \(b = 2\), \(c = 3\) and \(d = 4\),

\[ \begin{aligned} (1 + 2)(3 + 4) &= 3 \cdot 7 \\ &= 21, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} 1 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 4 &= 3 + 4 + 6 + 8 \\ &= 21. \end{aligned} \]

Setting \(c = a\) and \(d = b\) gives \((a + b)^2 = a^2 + ab + ba + b^2\), which is the square of a sum once \(ab\) and \(ba\) are combined.

LemTheorem 10 (Square of a sum)

Theorem 10 (Square of a sum) For any numbers \(a\) and \(b\),

\[ (a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \]

ProofProof

Proof. By Solution 1.

RemarkRemark 1 (Square of a difference)

Remark 1 (Square of a difference). Replacing \(b\) by \(-b\) in Theorem 10 gives \((a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2\). For example, the square \((y - \hat y)^2\) of the difference between an observed value \(y\) and a prediction \(\hat y\) of it expands this way. With \(y = 5\) and \(\hat y= 3\),

\[ \begin{aligned} (5 - 3)^2 &= 2^2 \\ &= 4, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} 5^2 - 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 3 + 3^2 &= 25 - 30 + 9 \\ &= 4. \end{aligned} \]

5 Summation notation

ExrExercise 2 (Expand a sum)

Exercise 2 (Expand a sum) The expression \(\sum_{i=1}^{4} i^2\) is shorthand for a sum of four terms.

  1. Guess which four terms, and add them up.
  2. Write \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\mathopen{}\left(y_i - \hat y_i\right)\mathclose{}^2\) for \(N = 3\) without the \(\sum\) symbol.
SolutionSolution 2

Solution 2.

  1. Replace \(i\) by each of \(1, 2, 3, 4\) in turn, and add the results:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{4} i^2 &= 1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 + 4^2 \\ &= 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 \\ &= 30 \end{aligned} \]

  2. Replace \(i\) by each of \(1, 2, 3\) in turn inside the parentheses, add the three squares, and multiply the total by \(\frac{1}{3}\):

    \[ \frac{1}{3} \sum_{i=1}^{3} \mathopen{}\left(y_i - \hat y_i\right)\mathclose{}^2 = \frac{1}{3} \mathopen{}\left[ \mathopen{}\left(y_1 - \hat y_1\right)\mathclose{}^2 + \mathopen{}\left(y_2 - \hat y_2\right)\mathclose{}^2 + \mathopen{}\left(y_3 - \hat y_3\right)\mathclose{}^2 \right]\mathclose{} \]

DefDefinition 9 (Summation notation)

Definition 9 (Summation notation) Let \(m\) and \(n\) be integers with \(m \le n\), and let \(a_m, a_{m+1}, \ldots, a_n\) be numbers. The sum of \(a_m\) through \(a_n\) is

\[ \sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}a_m + a_{m+1} + \cdots + a_n \]

The numbers \(m\) and \(n\) are its lower and upper limits: the first and last values of the index.

DefDefinition 10 (Index of a sum)

Definition 10 (Index of a sum) The variable \(i\) in \(\sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i\) (Definition 9) is the index of the sum.

RemarkRemark 2 (The index is a placeholder)

Remark 2 (The index is a placeholder). The name of the index does not change the sum: \(\sum_{i=1}^na_i\) and \(\sum_{j=1}^na_j\) are the same number. For example,

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{3} i &= 1 + 2 + 3 \\ &= 6 \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{j=1}^{3} j &= 1 + 2 + 3 \\ &= 6. \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 11 (Empty sum)

Definition 11 (Empty sum) When the upper limit is less than the lower limit (\(n < m\)), the sum \(\sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i\) has no terms. By convention, such an empty sum equals \(0\). For example, \(\sum_{i=1}^{0} a_i = 0\).

ExrExercise 3 (Rearrange a sum)

Exercise 3 (Rearrange a sum) Let \(c\) be a number, and let \(a_1, a_2, a_3\) and \(b_1, b_2, b_3\) be numbers. Using Definition 9 and the rules of algebra above, show that:

  1. \(\sum_{i=1}^{3} c\, a_i = c \sum_{i=1}^{3} a_i\);
  2. \(\sum_{i=1}^{3} \mathopen{}\left(a_i + b_i\right)\mathclose{} = \sum_{i=1}^{3} a_i + \sum_{i=1}^{3} b_i\).

Does either argument depend on there being exactly three terms?

SolutionSolution 3

Solution 3. Each step below applies Definition 9, the distributive law (Theorem 8), or the commutative and associative laws of addition (Theorem 2 and Theorem 3).

  1. Expand the sum, then factor out \(c\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{3} c\, a_i &= c\, a_1 + c\, a_2 + c\, a_3 && \text{(expand the sum)} \\ &= c \mathopen{}\left(a_1 + a_2 + a_3\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(distributive law)} \\ &= c \sum_{i=1}^{3} a_i && \text{(collect the sum)} \end{aligned} \]

  2. Expand the sum, then regroup the terms:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{3} \mathopen{}\left(a_i + b_i\right)\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left(a_1 + b_1\right)\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left(a_2 + b_2\right)\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left(a_3 + b_3\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(expand the sum)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(a_1 + a_2 + a_3\right)\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left(b_1 + b_2 + b_3\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(commutative and associative laws)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{3} a_i + \sum_{i=1}^{3} b_i && \text{(collect the sum)} \end{aligned} \]

Neither argument uses the number of terms: the same steps work for any lower and upper limits.

LemTheorem 11 (A constant factor comes out of a sum)

Theorem 11 (A constant factor comes out of a sum) For any number \(c\) and numbers \(a_m, \ldots, a_n\),

\[ \sum_{i=m}^{n} c\, a_i = c \sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i \]

ProofProof

Proof. By Solution 3, part 1.

LemTheorem 12 (A sum of sums splits)

Theorem 12 (A sum of sums splits) For any numbers \(a_m, \ldots, a_n\) and \(b_m, \ldots, b_n\),

\[ \sum_{i=m}^{n} \mathopen{}\left(a_i + b_i\right)\mathclose{} = \sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i + \sum_{i=m}^{n} b_i \]

ProofProof

Proof. By Solution 3, part 2.

ExrExercise 4 (Sum over a set)

Exercise 4 (Sum over a set) Let \(A = \mathopen{}\left\{-1, 2, 5\right\}\mathclose{}\) and let \(f(x) = x^2\). The expression \(\sum_{x \in A} f(x)\) means: add up \(f(x)\) for each element \(x\) of \(A\).

Write \(\sum_{x \in A} f(x)\) without the \(\sum\) symbol.

SolutionSolution 4

Solution 4. Replace \(x\) by each element of \(A\) in turn, and add:

\[\sum_{x \in A} f(x) = f(-1) + f(2) + f(5)\]

ExrExercise 5 (Value of a sum over a set)

Exercise 5 (Value of a sum over a set) In the setting of Exercise 4, find the value of \(\sum_{x \in A} f(x)\).

SolutionSolution 5

Solution 5. Evaluate each term and add:

\[ \begin{aligned} f(-1) + f(2) + f(5) &= (-1)^2 + 2^2 + 5^2 \\ &= 1 + 4 + 25 \\ &= 30 \end{aligned} \]

ExrExercise 6 (List the terms in a different order)

Exercise 6 (List the terms in a different order) In the setting of Exercise 4, list the elements of \(A\) in a different order, and write the term for each element in that order.

SolutionSolution 6

Solution 6. In the order \(5, -1, 2\), the terms are \(25\), \(1\) and \(4\).

ExrExercise 7 (Add the terms in the new order)

Exercise 7 (Add the terms in the new order) In the setting of Exercise 6, add up the terms.

SolutionSolution 7

Solution 7. The total is \(25 + 1 + 4 = 30\).

ExrExercise 8 (Does the order change the total?)

Exercise 8 (Does the order change the total?) Say whether the total in Exercise 7 is the same as the total in Exercise 5.

SolutionSolution 8

Solution 8. The total is the same, because addition does not depend on the order of the terms.

DefDefinition 12 (Sum over a finite set)

Definition 12 (Sum over a finite set) Let \(A = \mathopen{}\left\{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_k\right\}\mathclose{}\) be a finite set with \(k \ge 1\) different elements. Let \(f\) be a function that gives a number \(f(x)\) for each element \(x\) of \(A\). The sum of \(f\) over \(A\) is

\[ \sum_{x \in A} f(x) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}f(x_1) + f(x_2) + \cdots + f(x_k) \]

If \(A\) has no elements, the sum has no terms, and by convention it equals \(0\).

RemarkRemark 3 (The order of the terms does not matter)

Remark 3 (The order of the terms does not matter). The order in which we list the elements of \(A\) does not change the sum. The reason is that addition is commutative and associative: we can reorder and regroup the terms of a finite sum without changing the total. For example, if \(A = \mathopen{}\left\{1, 2, 3\right\}\mathclose{}\) and \(f(x) = x^2\), listing \(A\) as \(1, 2, 3\) gives \(1 + 4 + 9 = 14\), and listing \(A\) as \(3, 1, 2\) gives \(9 + 1 + 4 = 14\). Exercise 5 and Exercise 7 show another example.

When \(A = \mathopen{}\left\{m, m+1, \ldots, n\right\}\mathclose{}\), this sum is the same as \(\sum_{i=m}^{n} f(i)\) from Definition 9.

RemarkRemark 4 (Leaving the set out)

Remark 4 (Leaving the set out). Some authors leave the set out and write \(\sum_{x} f(x)\). This shorthand means the sum over every value \(x\) can take, \(\sum_{x \in \mathcal{R}(x)} f(x)\), where \(\mathcal{R}(x)\) is the range of \(x\). For example, if \(x\) is the outcome of one roll of a six-sided die, \(\sum_{x} f(x)\) means \(\sum_{x \in \mathopen{}\left\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\right\}\mathclose{}} f(x)\). In these notes, we write the set out in full (see Limits of sums and notational shorthands).

ExmExample 10 (A sum over the empty set)

Example 10 (A sum over the empty set) For example, \(\sum_{x \in \mathopen{}\left\{\right\}\mathclose{}} x^2 = 0\).

6 Quotients

DefDefinition 13 (Quotient)

Definition 13 (Quotient) For real numbers \(a\) and \(b\) with \(b \neq 0\), the quotient of \(a\) by \(b\) is the result of dividing \(a\) by \(b\):

\[\frac{a}{b}\]

DefDefinition 14 (Fraction)

Definition 14 (Fraction) A quotient \(\frac{a}{b}\) (Definition 13) is also called a fraction.

DefDefinition 15 (Numerator)

Definition 15 (Numerator) In a fraction \(\frac{a}{b}\) (Definition 14), \(a\) is its numerator.

DefDefinition 16 (Denominator)

Definition 16 (Denominator) In a fraction \(\frac{a}{b}\) (Definition 14), \(b\) is its denominator.

ExmExample 11 (Numerator and denominator of a fraction)

Example 11 (Numerator and denominator of a fraction) The fraction \(\frac{6}{4}\) has numerator \(6\) and denominator \(4\).

ExmExample 12 (A quotient)

Example 12 (A quotient) The quotient of \(6\) by \(4\) is \(\frac{6}{4} = 1.5\). The quotient of \(6\) by \(0\) is undefined, because Definition 13 requires a nonzero denominator.

DefDefinition 17 (Follow-up)

Definition 17 (Follow-up) In a study that observes participants over time, a participant’s follow-up is the length of time the study observes that participant.

DefDefinition 18 (Person-years)

Definition 18 (Person-years) In a study that observes participants over time, the study’s person-years are the sum of the follow-up times (Definition 17) of all participants, measured in years.

ExmExample 13 (Counting person-years)

Example 13 (Counting person-years) If \(10\) people are each followed for \(2\) years, the study has \(10 \cdot 2 = 20\) person-years. If one person is followed for \(3\) years and another for \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) year, the study has \(3 + \tfrac{1}{2} = 3.5\) person-years.

DefDefinition 19 (Rate)

Definition 19 (Rate) A rate is a quotient of two quantities, usually with a denominator that measures time, such as weeks or person-years of follow-up (Definition 18). For example, \(12\) new cases in \(4\) weeks is a rate of \(\frac{12}{4} = 3\) new cases per week, and \(30\) cases over \(10{,}000\) person-years of follow-up is a rate of \(\frac{30}{10{,}000} = 0.003\) cases per person-year.

cf. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_(mathematics)

DefDefinition 20 (Ratios)

Definition 20 (Ratios) A ratio is a quotient in which the numerator and denominator are measured using the same unit scales.

cf. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratio

ExmExample 14 (A ratio, and a quotient that is not one)

Example 14 (A ratio, and a quotient that is not one)  

  • A board \(150\) cm long and one \(75\) cm long have length ratio \(\tfrac{150 \text{ cm}}{75 \text{ cm}} = 2\): both lengths are in centimeters, so the units cancel and the ratio has none.
  • A sample of mass \(300\) g and volume \(150\) cm\(^3\) gives the quotient \(\tfrac{300 \text{ g}}{150 \text{ cm}^3} = 2\) g per cm\(^3\), its density. The numerator and denominator are in different units, so this quotient is not a ratio.
DefDefinition 21 (Proportion)

Definition 21 (Proportion) In statistics, a proportion typically means a ratio where the numerator represents a subset of the denominator.

See https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_proportion.

See also https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportion_(mathematics) for other meanings.

ExmExample 15 (A proportion, and a ratio that is not one)

Example 15 (A proportion, and a ratio that is not one) In a clinic with \(120\) patients, \(30\) of whom smoke:

  • the proportion of patients who smoke is \(\tfrac{30}{120} = 0.25\): the \(30\) smokers are a subset of the \(120\) patients;
  • the ratio of smokers to non-smokers is \(\tfrac{30}{90} = \tfrac{1}{3}\). Both counts are of patients, so this quotient is a ratio, but it is not a proportion: the \(30\) smokers are not part of the \(90\) non-smokers.
DefDefinition 22 (Proportional)

Definition 22 (Proportional) Two functions \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) are proportional if their ratio \(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) does not depend on \(x\). (cf. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportionality_(mathematics))

ExmExample 16 (Proportional and non-proportional functions)

Example 16 (Proportional and non-proportional functions)  

  • \(f(x) = 6x^2\) and \(g(x) = 2x^2\) are proportional: for \(x \ne 0\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} \tfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} &= \tfrac{6x^2}{2x^2} \\ &= 3, \end{aligned} \]

    which does not depend on \(x\).

  • \(f(x) = x + 1\) and \(g(x) = x\) are not proportional: for \(x \ne 0\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} \tfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} &= \tfrac{x + 1}{x} \\ &= \tfrac{x}{x} + \tfrac{1}{x} \\ &= 1 + \tfrac{1}{x}, \end{aligned} \]

    which is \(2\) at \(x = 1\) and \(\tfrac{3}{2}\) at \(x = 2\).

Additional reference for elementary algebra: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_proportion#Mathematical_definition

7 Limits of sums and notational shorthands

DefDefinition 23 (Lower limit of a sum or integral)

Definition 23 (Lower limit of a sum or integral) In a sum \(\sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i\), the lower limit \(m\) is the first value of the index \(i\). In an integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), the lower limit \(a\) is the left end of the interval of integration. In both, the lower limit is written below the symbol.

DefDefinition 24 (Upper limit of a sum or integral)

Definition 24 (Upper limit of a sum or integral) In a sum \(\sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i\), the upper limit \(n\) is the last value of the index \(i\). In an integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), the upper limit \(b\) is the right end of the interval of integration. In both, the upper limit is written above the symbol.

DefDefinition 25 (Limits of integration)

Definition 25 (Limits of integration) In an integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), the lower limit (Definition 23) \(a\) and the upper limit (Definition 24) \(b\) together are the limits of integration.

ExmExample 17 (Reading off limits)

Example 17 (Reading off limits)  

  • In \(\sum_{i=2}^{5} i^2\), the lower limit is \(2\) and the upper limit is \(5\), so the sum is \(2^2 + 3^2 + 4^2 + 5^2 = 54\).
  • In \(\int_0^3 x\,dx\), the lower limit is \(0\) and the upper limit is \(3\).
RemarkRemark 5 (Two meanings of “limit”)

Remark 5 (Two meanings of “limit”). The lower and upper limits of a sum or integral (Definition 23, Definition 24) are not limits in the calculus sense, such as the limit of a function or the limit of a sequence. They are the ends of the values the index or variable runs over. The two meanings meet in an integral such as \(\int_0^{\infty} f(x)\,dx\), which means \(\lim_{b \to \infty} \int_0^b f(x)\,dx\): the upper limit \(b\) is sent to infinity by a calculus limit.

DefDefinition 26 (Notational shorthand)

Definition 26 (Notational shorthand) A notational shorthand is a way of writing an expression that leaves part of the expression out, such as the set a sum runs over or the limits of an integral, and relies on the reader to supply the missing part from context.

DefDefinition 27 (Full form)

Definition 27 (Full form) The full form of a notational shorthand (Definition 26) is the expression with every part written out.

NoteThese notes write the full form

In these notes, we avoid notational shorthands in permanent writing and write the full form of each expression, even where a shorthand would be shorter. A reader who supplies the missing part of a shorthand from a different context than the writer intended reads a different expression than the one the writer meant. Handwritten board work is often less complete, but it should still write out ranges and limits where it can.

We make one exception: writing two factors side by side means multiplying them, as in \(2x\) for \(2 \cdot x\) and \(ab\) for \(a \cdot b\). We still write the multiplication sign when the side-by-side form could be misread:

  • \(2 \cdot 3\), not \(23\), which reads as twenty-three;
  • \(a \cdot(b + c)\) when \(a\) could be a function, since \(a(b + c)\) could mean \(a\) evaluated at \(b + c\).
DefDefinition 28 (Range of a variable)

Definition 28 (Range of a variable) The range of a variable \(x\), written \(\mathcal{R}(x)\), is the set of values that \(x\) can take.

For example, if \(x\) is the number of heads in two tosses of a coin, then \(\mathcal{R}(x) = \mathopen{}\left\{0, 1, 2\right\}\mathclose{}\). A random variable \(X\) (defined in the Morrison Lab’s probability notes) is a function, and the set of values \(X\) can take, \(\mathcal{R}(X)\), is the image of \(X\). These notes use “range” only for variables: for a function, “range” can mean either the image or the codomain (Range). In statistics, “the range” of a dataset can also mean its largest value minus its smallest value; \(\mathcal{R}(x)\) is a set, not that difference.

Table 1 lists shorthands you may encounter in other sources, the full form each one abbreviates, and the part each one leaves out. In the table, \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\) and \(a_1, \ldots, a_n\) are \(n\) numbers, \(b_{ij}\) is the entry in row \(i\) and column \(j\) of an \(m \times n\) array, \(k\) is an integer with \(1 \le k \le n\), \(f\) is a function from \(\mathbb{R}\) to \(\mathbb{R}\), and, in the first row, \(\mathcal{R}(x)\) is a finite set.

RemarkRemark 6 (\(\int f(x)\,dx\) has two readings)

Remark 6 (\(\int f(x)\,dx\) has two readings). In calculus, \(\int f(x)\,dx\) with no limits is not a shorthand: it is the indefinite integral of \(f\), a family of functions rather than a number. Some sources, especially in probability, also write \(\int f(x)\,dx\) for the number \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx\). Writing the limits out tells the reader which of the two is meant.

ExmExample 18 (Writing out an index shorthand)

Example 18 (Writing out an index shorthand) Let \(n = 3\) and \((x_1, x_2, x_3) = (2, 5, 1)\). A source that writes \(\sum_{i} x_i\) means \(\sum_{i=1}^{3} x_i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{3} x_i &= x_1 + x_2 + x_3 && \text{(definition of summation notation)} \\ &= 2 + 5 + 1 && \text{(substitute the values)} \\ &= 8 && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]
