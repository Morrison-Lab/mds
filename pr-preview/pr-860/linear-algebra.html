  • The Design Matrices and Projections page: design matrices, the hat matrix and orthogonal projection.

  • The Solving Linear Systems and Least Squares page: back substitution, the QR factorization, the LU factorization, least squares and the normal equations.

DefDefinition 1 (Matrix analysis)

Definition 1 (Matrix analysis) Matrix analysis is the study of matrices and their algebraic properties, with operations on matrices, functions of matrices and the eigenvalues of matrices as example topics [1].

ExmExample 1 (Eigenvalues of a small matrix)

Example 1 (Eigenvalues of a small matrix) The matrix \(\matr{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) has the eigenvalues \(3\) and \(1\). Finding these eigenvalues is a question about \(\matr{A}\) alone, so it is a topic of matrix analysis.

RemarkRemark 1 (Linear algebra and matrix analysis)

Remark 1 (Linear algebra and matrix analysis). Matrix analysis is usually placed within linear algebra and its applications [1], and these notes do not treat it as a separate subject: they use the name linear algebra for the material in the pages above. Banerjee and Roy [2] pairs the two names in its title. Its preface says that its last two chapters cover convergence of matrices and Hilbert spaces [2, p. xvi].

1 Further reading

  • Strang [3] is a widely used first course in linear algebra. It covers vectors, elimination, vector spaces, orthogonality, determinants, eigenvalues, and the singular value decomposition.
  • Nield [4] is a short, code-driven introduction to the linear algebra used in data science (Chapter 4).
  • Axler [5] is a proof-based treatment centered on vector spaces and linear maps. A free electronic version is available from the publisher.
  • Lange [6] covers computing eigenvalues and eigenvectors, including Jacobi’s method (Chapter 8), and the singular value decomposition, its properties, and its applications (Chapter 9).
  • Fieller [7]
  • Banerjee and Roy [2]
  • Searle and Khuri [8]
  • Petersen and Pedersen [9] is a free desk reference that collects matrix identities: it lists results on determinants, inverses, decompositions, and derivatives of vectors and matrices, so it is a quick place to look up a formula these pages prove.
  • Stanford University [10] (Stanford EE 278, taught by Tsachy Weissman) applies this page’s tools to probability and inference. Its “MMSE Geometry” lecture treats the best estimate as an orthogonal projection, and its lectures on spectral decomposition, the Karhunen-Loeve expansion and principal component analysis treat diagonalization of a covariance matrix.
  • Essence of Linear Algebra is a 16-video YouTube playlist by Grant Sanderson (3Blue1Brown) that develops visual and geometric intuition for vectors, linear combinations, spans, linear transformations, matrix multiplication, determinants, inverses, dot and cross products, change of basis, and eigenvectors and eigenvalues.
