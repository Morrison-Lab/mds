Second Partial Derivatives and the Hessian

Last modified: 2026-11-19 18:32:32 (PST)

1 Second partial derivatives and the Hessian

Definition 1 (Open ball) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) and \(r > 0\). The open ball [1] of radius \(r\) around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the set \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in \mathbb{R}^p : \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < r\right\}\mathclose{}\), of points whose distance from \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) (the Euclidean norm of the difference) is less than \(r\).

Definition 2 (Closed ball) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(r\) as in Definition 1, the closed ball of radius \(r\) around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in \mathbb{R}^p : \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \le r\right\}\mathclose{}\).

Definition 3 (Unit ball) The open and closed balls (Definition 1, Definition 2) of radius \(1\) around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) are the open and closed unit balls.

Example 1 (Points in the unit balls of \(\mathbb{R}^2\))  

  • \({(0.5, 0.5)}^{\top}\) has

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert{(0.5, 0.5)}^{\top}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 &= 0.25 + 0.25 \\ &= 0.5 \\ &< 1, \end{aligned} \]

    so it is in both the open and the closed unit ball.

  • \({(0.6, 0.8)}^{\top}\) has

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert{(0.6, 0.8)}^{\top}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 &= 0.36 + 0.64 \\ &= 1, \end{aligned} \]

    so it is in the closed unit ball but not the open one.

  • For \(p = 1\), the open ball of radius \(r\) around \(c\) is the open interval \((c - r, c + r)\).

Definition 4 (Second partial derivative) Let \(f\) be a scalar-valued function of a \(p \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), and let \(i, j \in \mathopen{}\left\{1, \ldots, p\right\}\mathclose{}\). Suppose the partial derivative \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f\) (Definition 1 in Partial Derivatives and Gradients) exists at every point of an open ball around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) (Definition 1). The second partial derivative of \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), first with respect to \(x_j\) and then with respect to \(x_i\), is

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}, \]

the partial derivative with respect to \(x_i\) of the function \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f\), when it exists.

Example 2 (Second partial derivatives of \(x_1^2 x_2 + 3 x_2\)) For \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 x_2 + 3 x_2\) of Example 1 in Partial Derivatives and Gradients, \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 2 x_1 x_2\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + 3\). Differentiating each of these again:

  • \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} (2 x_1 x_2) \\ &= 2 x_2; \end{aligned} \]

  • \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} (2 x_1 x_2) \\ &= 2 x_1; \end{aligned} \]

  • \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} (x_1^2 + 3) \\ &= 2 x_1; \end{aligned} \]

  • \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} (x_1^2 + 3) \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

The two mixed partial derivatives are both \(2 x_1\).

Definition 5 (Mixed partial derivative) When \(i \ne j\), the second partial derivative of Definition 4 is a mixed partial derivative.

Definition 6 (Hessian matrix) Let \(f\) be a scalar-valued function of a \(p \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) whose first partial derivatives exist on an open ball around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) (Definition 1) and whose second partial derivatives (Definition 4) exist at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\). The Hessian matrix [2] of \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the derivative (Definition 2 in Jacobians and Derivative Rules) of the transposed gradient \({\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}\) (Definition 2 in Partial Derivatives and Gradients):

\[ \underbrace{\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}_{p \times p} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}. \tag{1}\]

Example 3 (A Hessian matrix) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = e^{2x_1 + x_2} - x_1\) for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {(x_1, x_2)}^{\top}\), where \(e\) is Euler’s number, and write \(u = 2x_1 + x_2\). By the chain rule, \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} e^{u} = 2 e^{u}\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} e^{u} = e^{u}\), so

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 e^{u} - 1 \\ e^{u} \end{bmatrix}. \]

Differentiating each entry again,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} (2 e^{u} - 1) & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} e^{u} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} (2 e^{u} - 1) & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} e^{u} \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 e^{u} & 2 e^{u} \\ 2 e^{u} & e^{u} \end{bmatrix}, \end{aligned} \]

and at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), where \(u = 0\), \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\).

Example 4 (A function with a gradient but no Hessian at a point) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1 \mathopen{}\left|x_1\right|\mathclose{}\) for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {(x_1, x_2)}^{\top}\), where \(\mathopen{}\left|\cdot\right|\mathclose{}\) is the absolute value. For \(x_1 > 0\), \(f = x_1^2\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f = 2 x_1\); for \(x_1 < 0\), \(f = -x_1^2\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f = -2 x_1\); and at \(x_1 = 0\) the difference quotient is \(h \mathopen{}\left|h\right|\mathclose{} / h = \mathopen{}\left|h\right|\mathclose{} \to 0\). So the gradient exists everywhere: it is \({(2 \mathopen{}\left|x_1\right|\mathclose{},\ 0)}^{\top}\). But \(2 \mathopen{}\left|x_1\right|\mathclose{}\) has no derivative in \(x_1\) at \(x_1 = 0\) (its difference quotient \(2 \mathopen{}\left|h\right|\mathclose{} / h\) is \(2\) for \(h > 0\) and \(-2\) for \(h < 0\)), so \(\mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right]\mathclose{}_{11}\), and with it the Hessian, does not exist at any \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) with \(x_1 = 0\).

Theorem 1 (Other ways to write the Hessian, and its entries) Let \(f\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) be as in Definition 6.

  1. The Hessian can be written with the row-vector derivative (Definition 1 in Jacobians and Derivative Rules) of \(f\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}}\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= \frac{\partial^2 f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}}. \end{aligned} \tag{2}\]

  2. The \((i, j)\) entry of the Hessian is

    \[ \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}. \tag{3}\]

Proof. Part 1. By Theorem 1 in Jacobians and Derivative Rules, the transposed gradient \({\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}\) equals the row-vector derivative \(\frac{\partial f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}}\). Substituting it into Equation 1 gives the first line. The second line is the same expression written as a second derivative. It follows the shape rule of matrix multiplication (Remark 2 in Jacobians and Derivative Rules): the \(p \times 1\) column-vector operator \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\) stands on the left of a \(1 \times p\) row vector, giving a \(p \times p\) matrix.

Part 2. Let \(y_j \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) be entry \(j\) of the gradient. By Definition 2 in Jacobians and Derivative Rules, the \((i, j)\) entry of the derivative of the vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= (y_1, \ldots, y_p)\) with respect to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} &= \mathopen{}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}^{\top}\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} && \text{(}\href{#eq-hessian}{\text{Equation~1}}\text{, with } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\text{ the gradient)} \\ &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} y_j && \text{(}\href{vector-calculus-derivative-rules.qmd#def-vector-valued-derivative}{\text{Definition~2 in Jacobians and Derivative Rules}}\text{)} \\ &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}. && \text{(} y_j = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 5 (Computing the Hessian entry by entry, and checking it by finite differences) Take \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = e^{2x_1 + x_2} - x_1\) from Example 3. By Equation 3, entry \((i, j)\) of the Hessian is the derivative with respect to \(x_i\) of the derivative with respect to \(x_j\). The code differentiates f symbolically in that order with D(), evaluates the four entries at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (0.5, -1)\), and compares them with central differences of the gradient.

f_expr <- quote(exp(2 * x1 + x2) - x1)
vars <- c("x1", "x2")
point <- c(x1 = 0.5, x2 = -1)

grad_expr <- lapply(vars, function(v) D(f_expr, v))
hess_expr <- lapply(vars, function(i) {
  lapply(grad_expr, function(g_j) D(g_j, i))
})
H <- sapply(1:2, function(j) {
  sapply(1:2, function(i) eval(hess_expr[[i]][[j]], as.list(point)))
})

grad_at <- function(x) {
  sapply(grad_expr, function(g) eval(g, as.list(setNames(x, vars))))
}
h <- 1e-5
H_fd <- sapply(1:2, function(j) {
  sapply(1:2, function(i) {
    step <- replace(c(0, 0), i, h)
    (grad_at(point + step)[j] - grad_at(point - step)[j]) / (2 * h)
  })
})

H
#>      [,1] [,2]
#> [1,]    4    2
#> [2,]    2    1
H_fd
#>      [,1] [,2]
#> [1,]    4    2
#> [2,]    2    1

At this point \(u = 2x_1 + x_2 = 0\), so the closed form in Example 3 gives \(4 e^u = 4\) for entry \((1, 1)\), and the code gives 4. The off-diagonal entries are 2 and 2, which are equal. The finite differences agree with the symbolic entries to within 0.00000000026.

Definition 7 (Continuity at a point in several variables) A function \(f\) from \(\mathbb{R}^p\) to \(\mathbb{R}^q\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) if for every \(\varepsilon> 0\) there is a \(\delta> 0\) such that \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \varepsilon\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\). For \(q = 1\), \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{}\).

Definition 8 (Continuity on a set in several variables) A function \(f\) from \(\mathbb{R}^p\) to \(\mathbb{R}^q\) is continuous on a set if it is continuous at every point of the set (Definition 7).

Example 6 (A continuous function, and a discontinuous one)  

  • \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1 + x_2\) is continuous at every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\). Each \(\mathopen{}\left|y_i - x_i\right|\mathclose{} \le \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\), since the squared length is a sum of nonnegative squares, so

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left|(y_1 - x_1) + (y_2 - x_2)\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(subtract)} \\ &\le \mathopen{}\left|y_1 - x_1\right|\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left|y_2 - x_2\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(triangle inequality for numbers)} \\ &\le 2\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}, && \text{(each term is at most } \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

    which is less than \(\varepsilon\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta= \varepsilon/ 2\).

  • \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 1\) if \(x_1 > 0\) and \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 0\) otherwise is not continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\): for \(\varepsilon= \tfrac{1}{2}\) and any \(\delta> 0\), the point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= {(\delta/ 2, 0)}^{\top}\) has \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \delta/ 2 < \delta\) but \(\mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}})\right|\mathclose{} = 1 > \tfrac{1}{2}\).

Theorem 2 (Continuity in one variable, and sums, multiples and compositions)  

  1. For \(p = q = 1\), a function \(f\) is continuous at \(x\) in the sense of Definition 7 exactly when \(\lim_{y \to x} f(y) = f(x)\) (limit), which is the usual continuity at \(x\).
  2. If \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^q\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(g : \mathbb{R}^q \to \mathbb{R}^r\) is continuous at \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\), then the composition \(g \circ f\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).
  3. If \(f, g : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) are continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), then \(f + g\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).
  4. If \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(c \in \mathbb{R}\), then \(c f\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).

If the hypotheses of part 2, 3 or 4 hold at every point of a set, the conclusion holds at every point of that set.

Proof. Let \(\varepsilon> 0\) be given.

Part 1. For \(p = q = 1\), \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert f(y) - f(x)\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \mathopen{}\left|f(y) - f(x)\right|\mathclose{}\) and \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert y - x\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \mathopen{}\left|y - x\right|\mathclose{}\). Definition 7 then asks for a \(\delta> 0\) such that \(\mathopen{}\left|f(y) - f(x)\right|\mathclose{} < \varepsilon\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left|y - x\right|\mathclose{} < \delta\). The limit definition with \(L = f(x)\) asks for the same inequality, but only whenever \(0 < \mathopen{}\left|y - x\right|\mathclose{} < \delta\). The two agree, because at \(y = x\) the inequality \(\mathopen{}\left|f(x) - f(x)\right|\mathclose{} = 0 < \varepsilon\) holds for every \(\delta\).

Part 2. Because \(g\) is continuous at \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\), there is a \(\delta_g > 0\) such that \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}) - g(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}))\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \varepsilon\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta_g\). Because \(f\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), applied with \(\delta_g\) in the role of \(\varepsilon\), there is a \(\delta> 0\) such that \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta_g\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\). So if \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} = f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\) has \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta_g\), and therefore \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert g(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})) - g(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}))\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \varepsilon\).

Part 3. Because \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), there are \(\delta_f > 0\) and \(\delta_g > 0\) such that \(\mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} < \varepsilon/ 2\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta_f\), and \(\mathopen{}\left|g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} < \varepsilon/ 2\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta_g\). Let \(\delta\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\min\mathopen{}\left\{\delta_f, \delta_g\right\}\mathclose{}\). If \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\), then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left|(f + g)(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - (f + g)(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left|\mathopen{}\left(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left(g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(regroup)} \\ &\le \mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left|g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(triangle inequality for numbers)} \\ &< \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} && \text{(choice of } \delta\text{)} \\ &= \varepsilon. && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

Part 4. Because \(f\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), there is a \(\delta> 0\) such that \(\mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} < \varepsilon/ (\mathopen{}\left|c\right|\mathclose{} + 1)\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\). For such \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left|c f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - c f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left|c\right|\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(factor out } c \text{)} \\ &\le \mathopen{}\left|c\right|\mathclose{}\,\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathopen{}\left|c\right|\mathclose{} + 1} && \text{(choice of } \delta\text{)} \\ &< \varepsilon. && \text{(} \mathopen{}\left|c\right|\mathclose{} < \mathopen{}\left|c\right|\mathclose{} + 1 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 7 (Choosing \(\delta\) for a sum, a multiple and a composition) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1\) and \(g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_2\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\), and \(h(t) = 3t\) on \(\mathbb{R}\). Since \(\mathopen{}\left|y_i - x_i\right|\mathclose{} \le \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\), the functions \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous with \(\delta= \varepsilon\), and \(h\) is continuous with \(\delta= \varepsilon/ 3\), because \(\mathopen{}\left|h(s) - h(t)\right|\mathclose{} = 3\,\mathopen{}\left|s - t\right|\mathclose{}\). Take \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (1, 2)\) and \(\varepsilon= 0.1\). The proof of Theorem 2 gives these values of \(\delta\):

  • for \(f + g\) (part 3), \(\delta= \min\mathopen{}\left\{\varepsilon/ 2, \varepsilon/ 2\right\}\mathclose{}\);
  • for \(3 f\) (part 4), \(\delta= \varepsilon/ (3 + 1)\);
  • for \(h \circ (f + g)\) (part 2), the outer value \(\delta_h = \varepsilon/ 3\) plays the role of \(\varepsilon\) for \(f + g\), so \(\delta= \delta_h / 2\).

The code draws many points \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) at distance less than \(\delta\) from \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and finds the largest change in each function.

set.seed(571)
eps <- 0.1
x <- c(1, 2)
f <- function(x) x[1]
g <- function(x) x[2]
h <- function(t) 3 * t

fun_sum <- function(x) f(x) + g(x)
fun_multiple <- function(x) 3 * f(x)
fun_composition <- function(x) h(fun_sum(x))

deltas <- c(
  sum = eps / 2,
  multiple = eps / (3 + 1),
  composition = (eps / 3) / 2
)
funs <- list(
  sum = fun_sum, multiple = fun_multiple, composition = fun_composition
)

n <- 10000L
largest_change <- sapply(names(funs), function(nm) {
  radius <- deltas[[nm]] * sqrt(runif(n))
  angle <- runif(n, 0, 2 * pi)
  y <- cbind(x[1] + radius * cos(angle), x[2] + radius * sin(angle))
  max(abs(apply(y, 1, funs[[nm]]) - funs[[nm]](x)))
})
data.frame(delta = deltas, largest_change = largest_change, eps = eps)

For 3 of the 3 functions, the largest change among the 10000 points is below \(\varepsilon= 0.1\), as Theorem 2 guarantees.

Example 8 (Partial derivatives can exist where a function is not continuous) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\) be

\[ f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x_1 x_2}{x_1^2 + x_2^2} & \text{if } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}, \\ 0 & \text{if } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}. \end{cases} \]

Both partial derivatives exist at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). For \(h \ne 0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} f(h, 0) &= \frac{h \cdot 0}{h^2 + 0} \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

so

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{f(0 + h, 0) - f(0, 0)}{h} &= \frac{0 - 0}{h} && \text{(} f(h, 0) = 0 \text{ and } f(0, 0) = 0 \text{)} \\ &= 0, && \text{(} 0 / h = 0 \text{ for } h \ne 0 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

and the limit as \(h \to 0\) is \(0\): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) = 0\) (Definition 1 in Partial Derivatives and Gradients). The same steps with \(f(0, h) = 0\) give \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) = 0\).

But \(f\) is not continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) (Definition 7). Take \(\varepsilon= \tfrac{1}{4}\) and any \(\delta> 0\), and let \(t = \delta/ 2\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= {(t, t)}^{\top}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= \sqrt{t^2 + t^2} \\ &= t \sqrt{2} \\ &= \delta/ \sqrt{2} \\ &< \delta, \end{aligned} \]

but

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}})\right|\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left|\frac{t \cdot t}{t^2 + t^2} - 0\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\text{, since } t > 0 \text{)} \\ &= \frac{t^2}{2 t^2} && \text{(} t^2 + t^2 = 2 t^2 \text{)} \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2}, && \text{(cancel } t^2 > 0 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

which is more than \(\varepsilon= \tfrac{1}{4}\).

The partial derivatives only look along the two coordinate axes, where \(f\) is \(0\); along the line \(x_1 = x_2\), \(f\) is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) everywhere except at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). So partial derivatives existing at a point says little about how \(f\) behaves near it.

Theorem 3 (Symmetry of the Hessian) If the second partial derivatives of \(f\) exist and are continuous (Definition 7) on an open ball around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) (Definition 1), then \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}\) for all \(i, j\) [3], so \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) (Definition 6) is symmetric (symmetric matrix).

The proof applies the one-variable mean value theorem twice, which these notes do not develop; see [4], Theorem 9.41, which is stated for two variables: apply it to \(f\) as a function of \(x_i\) and \(x_j\), with the other coordinates held fixed.

Example 9 (Mixed partial derivatives agree) In Example 3, the \((1, 2)\) and \((2, 1)\) entries of \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) are both \(2 e^{2x_1 + x_2}\).

Example 10 (Without continuity, the mixed partials can differ) Let \(f(x_1, x_2) = \dfrac{x_1 x_2 (x_1^2 - x_2^2)}{x_1^2 + x_2^2}\) for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), and \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) = 0\). For \(x_2 \ne 0\), the partial derivative (Definition 1 in Partial Derivatives and Gradients) in \(x_1\) at \((0, x_2)\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(0, x_2) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(h, x_2) - f(0, x_2)}{h} && \text{(definition of the partial derivative, }\href{vector-calculus-gradients.qmd#def-partial-derivative}{\text{Definition~1 in Partial Derivatives and Gradients}}\text{)} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x_2 (h^2 - x_2^2)}{h^2 + x_2^2} && \text{(} f(0, x_2) = 0 \text{; cancel } h \text{)} \\ &= \frac{x_2 \cdot (-x_2^2)}{x_2^2} \\ &= -x_2, && \text{(the quotient is continuous at } h = 0 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) &= \lim_{h \to 0} (0 - 0)/h \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

so \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(0, x_2) = -x_2\) holds at \(x_2 = 0\) too. In the same way, with the roles of \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) swapped, \(f(x_1, k) / k = x_1 (x_1^2 - k^2) / (x_1^2 + k^2) \to x_1\), so \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} f(x_1, 0) = x_1\) for every \(x_1\). So at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) (Definition 6)

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}})\right]\mathclose{}_{21} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= \frac{d }{d x_2} (-x_2) \\ &= -1, \\ \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}})\right]\mathclose{}_{12} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} f\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= \frac{d }{d x_1} x_1 \\ &= 1: \end{aligned} \]

the Hessian at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is not symmetric. By Theorem 3, then, the second partial derivatives of this \(f\) cannot all be continuous near \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

Theorem 4 (Hessian of a quadratic form) If \(\mathbf{S}\) is a symmetric \(p \times p\) matrix that is constant with respect to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), then \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) has \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 2 \mathbf{S}\) for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} ({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} && \text{(}\href{#def-hessian}{\text{Definition~6}}\text{)} \\ &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} {\mathopen{}\left(2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} && \text{(}\href{vector-calculus-derivative-rules.qmd#cor-quadratic-form-symmetric}{\text{Corollary~2 in Jacobians and Derivative Rules}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{}\,{(2 \mathbf{S})}^{\top} && \text{(}\href{vector-calculus-derivative-rules.qmd#thm-deriv-matrix-vector}{\text{Theorem~5 in Jacobians and Derivative Rules}}\text{, with } \mathbf{A} = 2 \mathbf{S} \text{ and } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\text{; } \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} {(2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}^{\top} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} (2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \text{, }\href{vector-calculus-derivative-rules.qmd#def-vector-valued-derivative}{\text{Definition~2 in Jacobians and Derivative Rules}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p\,{(2 \mathbf{S})}^{\top} && \text{(} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathbf{I}_p \text{, }\href{vector-calculus-derivative-rules.qmd#rem-deriv-matrix-vector-special-case}{\text{Remark~4 in Jacobians and Derivative Rules}}\text{)} \\ &= 2 \mathbf{S}. && \text{(} \mathbf{S} \text{ is symmetric)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 11 (The Hessian of a \(2 \times 2\) quadratic form) For \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) as in Example 12 in Jacobians and Derivative Rules, Theorem 4 gives \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\). Directly, the gradient found there, with \(\beta_{i}\) renamed \(x_i\), is \({(6 x_1 + 2 x_2,\ 2 x_1 + 4 x_2)}^{\top}\), and differentiating its entries by \(x_1\) and by \(x_2\) (Definition 6) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} (6 x_1 + 2 x_2) & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} (2 x_1 + 4 x_2) \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} (6 x_1 + 2 x_2) & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} (2 x_1 + 4 x_2) \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]
