This section covers derivatives of functions of vectors and matrices. Its linear algebra prerequisites, such as vectors, matrices, transposes, dot products, and quadratic forms, are covered in Linear Algebra.

Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\) be column vectors of length \(p\) (see column vector and dot product).

DefDefinition 1 (Partial derivative)

Definition 1 (Partial derivative) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {(x_1, \ldots, x_p)}^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^p\). The partial derivative [2] of \(f\) with respect to \(x_j\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=} \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_1, \ldots, x_j + h, \ldots, x_p) - f(x_1, \ldots, x_p)}{h}, \]

when this limit exists. It is the derivative at \(x_j\) of the one-variable function \(t \mapsto f(x_1, \ldots, t, \ldots, x_p)\), which varies the \(j\)th coordinate and holds the other coordinates fixed.

ExmExample 1 (Partial derivatives of \(x_1^2 x_2 + 3 x_2\))

Example 1 (Partial derivatives of \(x_1^2 x_2 + 3 x_2\)) Let \(f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 x_2 + 3 x_2\). At \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {(1, 2)}^{\top}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} f(1, 2) &= 1 \cdot 2 + 6 \\ &= 8, \end{aligned} \]

and for \(h \ne 0\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{f(1 + h, 2) - f(1, 2)}{h} &= \frac{(1 + h)^2 \cdot 2 + 6 - 8}{h} && \text{(substitute into } f \text{)} \\ &= \frac{(1 + h)^2 \cdot 2 - 2}{h} && \text{(} 6 - 8 = -2 \text{)} \\ &= \frac{(1 + 2h + h^2) \cdot 2 - 2}{h} && \text{(expand } (1 + h)^2 \text{)} \\ &= \frac{2 + 4h + 2h^2 - 2}{h} && \text{(distributive law)} \\ &= \frac{4h + 2h^2 + 2 - 2}{h} && \text{(reorder the terms)} \\ &= \frac{4h + 2h^2}{h} && \text{(} 2 - 2 = 0 \text{)} \\ &= 4 + 2h, && \text{(divide by } h \ne 0 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

which tends to \(4\) as \(h \to 0\), so \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(1, 2) = 4\). Holding \(x_2\) fixed and differentiating in \(x_1\) gives the same answer: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 2 x_1 x_2\), which is \(2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 4\) at \({(1, 2)}^{\top}\). Holding \(x_1\) fixed instead gives \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + 3\), which is \(1 + 3 = 4\) at \({(1, 2)}^{\top}\).

ExrExercise 1 (First-order partial derivatives)

Exercise 1 (First-order partial derivatives) Adapted from Miller [3], Question 1.1.42.

Let \(f(x, y) = x^2 y + e^x + \sin(xy)\). Find \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\) and \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\).

SolutionSolution 1

Solution 1. To compute \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\), treat \(y\) as a constant:

\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy + e^x + y\cos(xy)\]

To compute \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\), treat \(x\) as a constant:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} &= x^2 + 0 + x\cos(xy) \\ &= x^2 + x\cos(xy) \end{aligned} \]

ExrExercise 2 (Partial derivatives of the normal density with respect to parameters)

Exercise 2 (Partial derivatives of the normal density with respect to parameters) Adapted from Miller [3], Question 1.1.43.

Let

\[f(x; \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}\mathclose{}}\]

Find \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mu}\) and \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma}\).

SolutionSolution 2

Solution 2. 1. Derivative with respect to \(\mu\): Treat \(\sigma\) as constant. Differentiating the exponential factor via the chain rule:

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mu} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}\mathclose{}} \cdot\mathopen{}\left[-\frac{2(x - \mu)(-1)}{2\sigma^2}\right]\mathclose{} \\ &= \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma^2} f(x; \mu, \sigma) \end{aligned}\]

2. Derivative with respect to \(\sigma\): Write \(f(x; \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma^{-1}{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}\mathclose{}}\) and apply the product rule:

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma} &= -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma^{-2}{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}\mathclose{}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma^{-1}{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}\mathclose{}} \cdot\mathopen{}\left[\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{\sigma^3}\right]\mathclose{} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[-\frac{1}{\sigma} + \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{\sigma^3}\right]\mathclose{} f(x; \mu, \sigma) \end{aligned}\]

Remark: Setting \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mu} = 0\) and \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma} = 0\) for a sample of independent observations leads directly to the values that make the sample most probable, which statistics calls the maximum likelihood estimates: \(\hat{\mu}= \bar{x}\) and \(\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i - \bar{x})^2\).

LemTheorem 1 (Partial derivatives of the normal density with respect to its parameters)

Theorem 1 (Partial derivatives of the normal density with respect to its parameters) Let \(\mu\in \mathbb{R}\) and \(\sigma> 0\), and let

\[f(x; \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}\mathclose{}}\]

Then for every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mu} &= \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma^2} f(x; \mu, \sigma) \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma} &= \mathopen{}\left[-\frac{1}{\sigma} + \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{\sigma^3}\right]\mathclose{} f(x; \mu, \sigma) \end{aligned} \]

ProofProof

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 2, worked in Solution 2. The argument has two steps.

  1. For \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mu}\), treat \(\sigma\) as constant. The prefactor is constant, so the chain rule on the exponential gives \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mu} = f(x; \mu, \sigma) \cdot\mathopen{}\left[-\frac{2(x - \mu)(-1)}{2\sigma^2}\right]\mathclose{} = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma^2} f(x; \mu, \sigma)\).
  2. For \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma}\), write \(f(x; \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma^{-1}{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}\mathclose{}}\). The product rule gives one term from differentiating \(\sigma^{-1}\), namely \(-\sigma^{-1} f(x; \mu, \sigma)\), and one from the chain rule on the exponential, namely \(\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{\sigma^3} f(x; \mu, \sigma)\). Adding them gives \(\mathopen{}\left[-\frac{1}{\sigma} + \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{\sigma^3}\right]\mathclose{} f(x; \mu, \sigma)\).
ExrExercise 3 (Partial derivatives with exponential of quadratic form)

Exercise 3 (Partial derivatives with exponential of quadratic form) Adapted from Miller [3], Question 1.1.44.

Find \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\) and \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\) for

\[f(x, y) = x{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{x^2 + y^2\right\}\mathclose{}}\]

SolutionSolution 3

Solution 3. With respect to \(x\), apply the product rule:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} &= (1){\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{x^2 + y^2\right\}\mathclose{}} + x \cdot\mathopen{}\left[2x{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{x^2 + y^2\right\}\mathclose{}}\right]\mathclose{} \\ &= (1 + 2x^2){\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{x^2 + y^2\right\}\mathclose{}} \end{aligned} \]

With respect to \(y\), treat \(x\) as constant:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} &= x \cdot\mathopen{}\left[2y{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{x^2 + y^2\right\}\mathclose{}}\right]\mathclose{} \\ &= 2xy{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{x^2 + y^2\right\}\mathclose{}} \end{aligned} \]

ExrExercise 4 (Using symmetry to compute partial derivatives)

Exercise 4 (Using symmetry to compute partial derivatives) Adapted from Miller [3], Question 1.1.45.

Find \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\) and \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\) for

\[f(x, y) = e^{xy} - \log(x^2 + y^2)\]

on \(\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(0, 0)\}\).

SolutionSolution 4

Solution 4. Holding \(y\) constant, differentiate with respect to \(x\):

\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = ye^{xy} - \frac{2x}{x^2 + y^2}\]

Notice that the function is symmetric in \(x\) and \(y\): \(f(x, y) = f(y, x)\). Interchanging \(x\) and \(y\) gives \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\) immediately:

\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = xe^{xy} - \frac{2y}{x^2 + y^2}\]

Remark: Recognizing symmetry in multi-variable functions eliminates redundant calculations.

DefDefinition 2 (Vector derivative, gradient)

Definition 2 (Vector derivative, gradient) If \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\) is a scalar-valued function of a \(p \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), such as \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), then its vector derivative is the column vector of its partial derivatives (Definition 1):

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{1}}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{2}}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) \\ \vdots \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{p}}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) \end{bmatrix} \]

The vector derivative is also called the gradient [4] of \(f\) with respect to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), written \(\nabla_{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\).

ExmExample 2 (A vector derivative)

Example 2 (A vector derivative) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= {(\beta_{1}, \beta_{2})}^{\top}\) and \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = 3 \beta_{1} + 5 \beta_{2}^2\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})}_{2 \times 1} &= \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{1}} (3 \beta_{1} + 5 \beta_{2}^2) \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{2}} (3 \beta_{1} + 5 \beta_{2}^2) \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 10 \beta_{2} \end{bmatrix}, \end{aligned} \]

and at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= {(1, 2)}^{\top}\) the vector derivative is \({(3, 20)}^{\top}\).

DefDefinition 3 (Direction)

Definition 3 (Direction) A direction in \(\mathbb{R}^p\) is given by a nonzero vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\): moving from a point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) in the direction \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}\) means moving to the points \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}\) with \(t > 0\). For any \(c > 0\), \(c \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}\) reaches the same points (with \(t / c\) in place of \(t\)), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}\) and \(c \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}\) give the same direction.

ExmExample 3 (Moving from \({(2, -1)}^{\top}\) in the direction \({(1, 1)}^{\top}\))

Example 3 (Moving from \({(2, -1)}^{\top}\) in the direction \({(1, 1)}^{\top}\)) From \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {(2, -1)}^{\top}\), moving in the direction \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} = {(1, 1)}^{\top}\) reaches the points \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} = {(2 + t,\ -1 + t)}^{\top}\) for \(t > 0\), such as \({(3, 0)}^{\top}\) at \(t = 1\). The vector \({(2, 2)}^{\top}\) gives the same direction: it reaches \({(3, 0)}^{\top}\) at \(t = \tfrac{1}{2}\). The vector \({(-1, -1)}^{\top}\) gives the opposite direction, reaching points such as \({(1, -2)}^{\top}\).

DefDefinition 4 (Level set)

Definition 4 (Level set) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) and let \(c\) be a real number. The level set [6] of \(f\) at \(c\) is

\[ \mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p : f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = c\right\}\mathclose{}, \]

the set of points where \(f\) takes the value \(c\). It is the preimage of \(\mathopen{}\left\{c\right\}\mathclose{}\) under \(f\), so we write it \({{{f}^{-1}\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left\{c\right\}\mathclose{}\right)\mathclose{}}}\). For \(p = 2\), a level set is often a curve in the plane.

ExmExample 4 (Level sets of \(x_1^2 + x_2^2\))

Example 4 (Level sets of \(x_1^2 + x_2^2\)) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2\) for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {(x_1, x_2)}^{\top}\).

  • The level set at \(1\) is the circle of radius \(1\) around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). For example, \({(0.6, 0.8)}^{\top}\) is on it, since \(0.36 + 0.64 = 1\), and \({(1, 1)}^{\top}\) is not, since \(1 + 1 = 2\).
  • The level set at \(0\) is the single point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).
  • The level set at \(-1\) is empty, since \(x_1^2 + x_2^2 \ge 0\).
DefDefinition 5 (Level curve)

Definition 5 (Level curve) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\) and let \(c\) be a real number. The level curve of \(f\) at \(c\) is the level set of \(f\) at \(c\) when it is a curve in the plane. It is the special case \(p = 2\) of a level set. A contour line is a level curve drawn on a plot of \(f\).

ExmExample 5 (A level curve of \(x_1^2 + x_2^2\))

Example 5 (A level curve of \(x_1^2 + x_2^2\)) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2\) for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {(x_1, x_2)}^{\top}\). The level curve at \(4\) is the circle of radius \(2\) around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

gradDeg = (rad) => ((rad * 180 / Math.PI) % 360 + 360) % 360
// The chosen function, and its gradient at the chosen point.

gradLen = Math.hypot(...gradG)

// The rate f changes at, per unit step in direction deg: the gradient dotted with that direction.
gradRate = (deg) => gradG[0] * Math.cos(deg * Math.PI / 180) + gradG[1] * Math.sin(deg * Math.PI / 180)

Exercise 5 (Take a gradient by hand) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\) be

\[f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) = 3 w_1 w_2 + w_2^3 - 5 w_1\]

  1. Compute \(\partial f / \partial w_1\) (Definition 1), treating \(w_2\) as a constant.
  2. Compute \(\partial f / \partial w_2\), treating \(w_1\) as a constant.
  3. Assemble \(\nabla_{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}} f\) and evaluate it at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}^{\top}\).
  4. Check the shape of your answer against Table 1.
SolutionSolution 5

Solution 5. 1. Differentiate with respect to \(w_1\), holding \(w_2\) fixed. The first term is the constant \(3w_2\) times \(w_1\), so it contributes \(3w_2\). The second term has no \(w_1\) in it, so it contributes \(0\). The third term is \(-5\) times \(w_1\), so it contributes \(-5\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial w_1} &= 3w_2 + 0 - 5 \\ &= 3w_2 - 5 \end{aligned} \]

2. Now with respect to \(w_2\), holding \(w_1\) fixed. The first term is the constant \(3w_1\) times \(w_2\), contributing \(3w_1\). The second term contributes \(3w_2^2\). The third has no \(w_2\) in it, contributing \(0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial w_2} &= 3w_1 + 3w_2^2 + 0 \\ &= 3w_1 + 3w_2^2 \end{aligned} \]

3. Stack the two, in the order the coordinates are numbered:

\[\nabla_{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) = \begin{bmatrix} 3w_2 - 5 \\ 3w_1 + 3w_2^2 \end{bmatrix}\]

The gradient is a vector-valued function of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\), not a single vector. At \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}^{\top}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}} f &= \begin{bmatrix} 3(-1) - 5 \\ 3(2) + 3(-1)^2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} -3 - 5 \\ 6 + 3 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} -8 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

4. The input was a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) and so is the answer, as Table 1 requires. Note the signs: from this point, increasing \(w_1\) decreases \(f\) while increasing \(w_2\) increases it, so the uphill direction is neither axis.

DefDefinition 6 (Row-vector derivative)

Definition 6 (Row-vector derivative) If \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\) is a scalar-valued function of a \(p \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), such as \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), then its row-vector derivative is the row vector of its partial derivatives (Definition 1):

\[ \frac{\partial f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top}} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{1}}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) & \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{2}}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) & \cdots & \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{p}}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) \end{bmatrix} \tag{1}\]

When the derivative of a scalar-valued function is written as a row vector, it is also called the total derivative [7] of \(f\).

RemarkRemark 1 (Other notation for the row-vector derivative)

Remark 1 (Other notation for the row-vector derivative). Some sources write the same row vector as \(\frac{\partial}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\), with the operator on the left, read as each operator \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{i}}\) applied to \(f\) rather than as a matrix product (see Remark 2).

RemarkRemark 2 (Which side the operator goes on)

Remark 2 (Which side the operator goes on). Read \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\) as a \(p \times 1\) column vector of operators with entries \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{1}}, \ldots, \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{p}}\), and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top}}\) as the \(1 \times p\) row vector of operators with the same entries. Writing either one next to \(f\) can be read in two ways:

  • As a matrix product, with \(f\) a \(1 \times 1\) matrix, under the shape rule of matrix multiplication. The column-vector operator works on the left: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} f\) is \((p \times 1)(1 \times 1)\), a \(p \times 1\) column vector (Definition 2). The row-vector operator does not: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top}} f\) is \((1 \times p)(1 \times 1)\), whose inner dimensions \(p\) and \(1\) do not match when \(p > 1\). For the shape rule to give the \(1 \times p\) row vector, the operator would have to go on the right of \(f\), as in \((1 \times 1)(1 \times p)\), which changes its meaning (see below).
  • As a scalar multiple, with \(f\) a scalar, the way \(c \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is read in scalar multiplication, which has no shape rule: each entry of the operator vector is applied to \(f\). Then both \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} f\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top}} f\) work with the operator on the left.

The fraction \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top}}\) of Equation 1 means the same thing under both readings: the transpose in the denominator says the result is a row vector, without making the derivative a product.

Treating the operators as vectors is sound for checking shapes, but “multiplying” an operator by a function means applying the operator to it, so two rules of ordinary matrix algebra do not carry over: scalars commute with matrices, and products can be regrouped.

  • Scalars do not commute with operators. \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{i}} f\) is a function, but \(f \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{i}}\) is still an unapplied operator, waiting for something to act on. So, unlike \(c \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}c\), the operator cannot move to the other side of \(f\) without changing the meaning. Putting the row-vector operator on the right of \(f\) gives the row-vector derivative only under the convention that it acts on the factor to its left.
  • Products cannot be regrouped. An operator acts on the whole product to its right, so by the product rule, \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{i}} (f g) \ne \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{i}} f\right)\mathclose{} g\) in general. For example, with \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = \beta_{1}\), \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{1}} (f f) = 2 \beta_{1}\) but \(\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{1}} f\right)\mathclose{} f = \beta_{1}\).

ProofProof

Proof. DefDefinition 7 (Derivative of a vector-valued function)

Definition 7 (Derivative of a vector-valued function) If

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}&= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) \\ &= {(y_1, \ldots, y_q)}^{\top} \end{aligned} \]

is a \(q \times 1\) vector-valued function of the \(p \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), its derivative with respect to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\) is the \(p \times q\) matrix whose \((i, j)\) entry is

\[ \mathopen{}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}^{\top}\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{i}} y_j, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, p, \quad j = 1, \ldots, q. \]

Both \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}^{\top}\) denote this \(p \times q\) matrix.

DefDefinition 8 (Denominator layout)

Definition 8 (Denominator layout) Writing derivatives as in Definition 7 is the denominator layout [8], which these notes use throughout: rows index the entries of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\) (the denominator) and columns index the entries of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) (the numerator), so column \(j\) is the vector derivative \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} y_j\) (Definition 2).

DefDefinition 9 (Jacobian matrix)

Definition 9 (Jacobian matrix) Let

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}&= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) \\ &= {(y_1, \ldots, y_q)}^{\top} \end{aligned} \]

be a \(q \times 1\) vector-valued function of the \(p \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\). DefDefinition 10 (Numerator layout)

Definition 10 (Numerator layout) Writing derivatives as in Definition 9, with rows indexing the entries of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) (the numerator) and columns indexing the entries of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\) (the denominator), is the numerator layout [8].

the transpose of the \(2 \times 3\) matrix found there.

LemTheorem 4 (Product rule for dot-products)

Theorem 4 (Product rule for dot-products) If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) are differentiable \(p \times 1\) vector functions of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), then:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}_{p \times 1}} \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}}_{p \times 1} \cdot \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}_{p \times 1} &= \mathopen{}\left( \frac{\partial}{\partial \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}_{p \times 1}} \underbrace{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}}^{\top}}_{1 \times p} \right)\mathclose{} \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}_{p \times 1} + \mathopen{}\left( \frac{\partial}{\partial \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}_{p \times 1}} \underbrace{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top}}_{1 \times p} \right)\mathclose{} \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}}_{p \times 1} \end{aligned} \]

ProofProof

Proof. Entry-wise, for \(i = 1, \ldots, p\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}})\right]\mathclose{}_i &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k b_k \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^{p} \mathopen{}\left(b_k \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} a_k + a_k \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} b_k\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right]\mathclose{}_i + \mathopen{}\left[\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}\right]\mathclose{}_i \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 12 (Example of the dot-product rule)

Example 12 (Example of the dot-product rule) Apply Theorem 4 with the vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= {(\beta_{1}, \beta_{2})}^{\top}\) in the role of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\). Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = {(\beta_{1}, \beta_{1}\beta_{2})}^{\top}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = {(\beta_{2}, \beta_{1})}^{\top}\). Then:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} &= \beta_{1} \cdot \beta_{2} + \beta_{1}\beta_{2} \cdot \beta_{1} \\ &= \beta_{1}\beta_{2} + \beta_{1}^2\beta_{2} \end{aligned} \]

By direct calculation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}})}_{2 \times 1} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}(\beta_{1}\beta_{2} + \beta_{1}^2\beta_{2}) \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} \beta_{2} + 2\beta_{1}\beta_{2} \\ \beta_{1} + \beta_{1}^2 \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

By the product rule (Theorem 4), using \(\underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}}^{\top}}_{2 \times 2} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & \beta_{2} \\ 0 & \beta_{1}\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top}}_{2 \times 2} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}})}_{2 \times 1} &= \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1 & \beta_{2} \\ 0 & \beta_{1}\end{pmatrix}}_{2 \times 2} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{2} \\ \beta_{1}\end{pmatrix}}_{2 \times 1} + \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}}_{2 \times 2} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1} \\ \beta_{1}\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix}}_{2 \times 1} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix}\beta_{2} + \beta_{1}\beta_{2} \\ \beta_{1}^2\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}\beta_{2} \\ \beta_{1}\end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix}\beta_{2} + 2\beta_{1}\beta_{2} \\ \beta_{1}^2 + \beta_{1}\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

Both methods agree.

LemTheorem 5 (Derivative of a linear map)

\[ \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} (\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})}_{p \times m} = \underbrace{{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}_{p \times m} \]

ProofProof

Proof. For entry \((i,j)\), where row \(i\) indexes the denominator \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\) (see Definition 7) and column \(j\) indexes the numerator \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} (\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{i}} (\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})_j \\ &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{i}} \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_{jk} \beta_{k} \\ &= a_{ji} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is constant with respect to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), \(\mathbf{A} = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\) is a constant \(1 \times p\) matrix. Applying Theorem 6 with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\) (so \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= \mathbf{I}\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) &= \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\right)\mathclose{} {({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top})}^{\top} \\ &= \mathbf{I} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 16 (Derivative of a transpose product)

Example 16 (Derivative of a transpose product) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {(3, 5)}^{\top}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= {(\beta_{1}, \beta_{2})}^{\top}\). Then \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= 3\beta_{1} + 5\beta_{2}\), and by Corollary 1:

\[ \begin{aligned} \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\mathopen{}\left(\underbrace{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}}_{1 \times 2}\underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}_{2 \times 1}\right)\mathclose{}}_{2 \times 1} &= \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}_{2 \times 1} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

RemarkRemark 5 (The coefficient gets transposed)

Remark 5 (The coefficient gets transposed). This vector derivative formula looks a lot like non-vector calculus, except that you have to transpose the coefficient: in scalar calculus \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(cx) = c\), but here the coefficient \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\) (a row vector) becomes \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) (a column vector) in the result. For example, with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {(2, -1)}^{\top}\), \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= 2\beta_{1} - \beta_{2}\), whose vector derivative is the column vector \({(2, -1)}^{\top} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), not the row vector \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} = (2, -1)\).

LemTheorem 8 (Derivative of a quadratic form)

Theorem 8 (Derivative of a quadratic form) For a quadratic form (see quadratic form), if \(\mathbf{S}\) is a \(p \times p\) matrix that is constant with respect to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), then:

\[ \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} ({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})}_{p \times 1} = \underbrace{(\mathbf{S} + {\mathbf{S}}^{\top}) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}_{p \times 1}. \]

ProofProof

Proof. Expanding entry-wise, \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} = \sum_{j=1}^p\sum_{k=1}^{p} s_{jk} \beta_{j} \beta_{k}\). Differentiating component-wise with respect to \(\beta_{i}\) for \(i = 1, \ldots, p\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\right]\mathclose{}_i &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{i}} \sum_{j=1}^p\sum_{k=1}^{p} s_{jk} \beta_{j} \beta_{k} && \text{(expand quadratic form)} \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^{p} s_{ik} \beta_{k} + \sum_{j=1}^ps_{ji} \beta_{j} && \text{(product rule for } \beta_{i} \beta_{k} \text{)} \\ &= [\mathbf{S}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}]_i + [{\mathbf{S}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}]_i && \text{(matrix-vector multiplication definition)} \\ &= [(\mathbf{S} + {\mathbf{S}}^{\top})\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}]_i && \text{(linearity of matrix multiplication)} \end{aligned} \]

CorCorollary 2 (Derivative of a quadratic form with a symmetric matrix)

Corollary 2 (Derivative of a quadratic form with a symmetric matrix) If, in addition, \(\mathbf{S}\) is symmetric (and, as in Theorem 8, \(p \times p\) and constant with respect to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\)), then

\[ \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} ({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})}_{p \times 1} = \underbrace{2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}_{p \times 1}. \]

ProofProof

Proof. If \(\mathbf{S} = {\mathbf{S}}^{\top}\), then \(\mathbf{S} + {\mathbf{S}}^{\top} = 2\mathbf{S}\), so Theorem 8 gives \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = 2\mathbf{S}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\).

RemarkRemark 6 (Like the derivative of \(cx^2\))

Remark 6 (Like the derivative of \(cx^2\)). This operation is like taking the derivative of \(cx^2\) with respect to \(x\) in non-vector calculus: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x} (cx^2) = 2cx\), and Corollary 2 says \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} ({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = 2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\). For example, with \(p = 1\), \(\mathbf{S} = (3)\), and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= (\beta_{1})\), \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} = 3\beta_{1}^2\), and its derivative is \(6\beta_{1} = 2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\).

ExmExample 17 (Derivative of a quadratic form)

Example 17 (Derivative of a quadratic form) Let \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) (\(2 \times 2\), symmetric and constant) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= {(\beta_{1}, \beta_{2})}^{\top}\). Then \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} = 3\beta_{1}^2 + 2\beta_{1}\beta_{2} + 2\beta_{2}^2\). By Corollary 2:

\[ \begin{aligned} \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})}_{2 \times 1} &= 2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\\ &= 2 \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \beta_{1} \\ \beta_{2} \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 6\beta_{1} + 2\beta_{2} \\ 2\beta_{1} + 4\beta_{2} \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

Differentiating component-wise directly:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{1}}(3\beta_{1}^2 + 2\beta_{1}\beta_{2} + 2\beta_{2}^2) \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_{2}}(3\beta_{1}^2 + 2\beta_{1}\beta_{2} + 2\beta_{2}^2) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6\beta_{1} + 2\beta_{2} \\ 2\beta_{1} + 4\beta_{2} \end{pmatrix} \]

Both methods agree.

For a matrix that is not symmetric, such as \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\), Theorem 8 gives

\[ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf{S} + {\mathbf{S}}^{\top}) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} &= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \beta_{1} \\ \beta_{2} \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 2\beta_{1} + 2\beta_{2} \\ 2\beta_{1} + 6\beta_{2} \end{pmatrix}, \end{aligned} \]

which matches differentiating \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} = \beta_{1}^2 + 2\beta_{1}\beta_{2} + 3\beta_{2}^2\) directly.

CorCorollary 3 (Derivative of a simple quadratic form)

Corollary 3 (Derivative of a simple quadratic form) \[ \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})}_{p \times 1} = \underbrace{2\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}_{p \times 1} \]

ProofProof

Proof. Applying Corollary 2 with \(\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{I}_{p \times p}\) (which is symmetric and constant with respect to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}^{\top} \mathbf{I}_{p \times p} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) && \text{(rewrite with identity matrix)} \\ &= 2\mathbf{I}_{p \times p}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} && \text{(derivative of a quadratic form, with } \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{I}_{p \times p} \text{)} \\ &= 2\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} && \text{(identity matrix property)} \end{aligned} \]

RemarkRemark 7 (Like the derivative of \(x^2\))

Remark 7 (Like the derivative of \(x^2\)). This vector derivative is like taking the derivative of \(x^2\): in scalar calculus \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x} x^2 = 2x\), and Corollary 3 says \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = 2\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\). For example, with \(p = 1\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= (\beta_{1})\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} = \beta_{1}^2\), and its derivative is \(2\beta_{1} = 2\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\).

ExmExample 18 (Derivative of a sum of squares)

Example 18 (Derivative of a sum of squares) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= {(\beta_{1}, \beta_{2})}^{\top}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} = \beta_{1}^2 + \beta_{2}^2\). By Corollary 3:

\[ \begin{aligned} \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})}_{2 \times 1} &= 2\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 2\beta_{1} \\ 2\beta_{2} \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

Direct partial differentiation yields the same column vector.

DefDefinition 12 (Residual vector and residuals)

Definition 12 (Residual vector and residuals) Let \(\mathbf{X}\) be an \(n \times p\) matrix, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in \mathbb{R}^n\), and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\). The residual vector of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\) is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), and its entries \(\varepsilon_i = y_i - (\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})_i\) are the residuals [10].

DefDefinition 13 (Squared error)

Definition 13 (Squared error) In the setting of Definition 12, the square \(\varepsilon_i^2\) is the \(i\)th squared error.

DefDefinition 14 (Residual sum of squares)

Definition 14 (Residual sum of squares) In the setting of Definition 12, the sum of the squared errors (Definition 13)

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}} &= \sum_{i=1}^n\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}_i^2 \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 \end{aligned} \]

is the residual sum of squares [11].

RemarkRemark 8 (Least squares solutions minimize the residual sum of squares)

Remark 8 (Least squares solutions minimize the residual sum of squares). A least squares solution of \(\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) makes \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\) as small as possible, and so makes the residual sum of squares (Definition 14) as small as possible too.

ExmExample 19 (Residuals of a constant fit)

Example 19 (Residuals of a constant fit) Let \(\mathbf{X}= {(1, 1)}^{\top}\) (\(n = 2\), \(p = 1\)) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= {(1, 3)}^{\top}\). For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= (2)\), \(\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= {(2, 2)}^{\top}\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) &= {(1, 3)}^{\top} - {(2, 2)}^{\top} && \text{(definition of the residual vector)} \\ &= {(-1, 1)}^{\top}, && \text{(subtract entry by entry)} \end{aligned} \]

the squared errors are \((-1)^2 = 1\) and \(1^2 = 1\), and the residual sum of squares is \(1 + 1 = 2\). For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= (1)\) the residuals are \(0\) and \(2\), and the residual sum of squares is \(0 + 4 = 4\).

LemTheorem 9 (Vector chain rule)

Theorem 9 (Vector chain rule) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) be a \(p \times 1\) vector, let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) be a \(q \times 1\) vector-valued function of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), and let \(z = f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\) be a scalar-valued function of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\), where \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\) and \(f\) have continuous partial derivatives. Then \(z = f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}))\), as a function of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), satisfies

\[ \underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}_{p \times 1} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}_{p \times q} \underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}}_{q \times 1} \]

where \(\frac{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\) is the derivative of Definition 7 and \(\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\) and \(\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}\) are vector derivatives (Definition 2).

RemarkRemark 9 (The order of the factors matters)

Remark 9 (The order of the factors matters). The vector chain rule (Theorem 9) is like the univariate chain rule, but the order matters now: \(\frac{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\) is \(p \times q\) and \(\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}\) is \(q \times 1\), so the product \(\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}} \frac{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\) in the other order is not even defined unless \(p = 1\).

The version presented here is for the gradient (Definition 2), a column vector. The total derivative (Definition 6), a row vector, is the transpose of the gradient (Theorem 2), and transposing both sides gives \({\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} = {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}\), with the factors in the reverse order; there \({\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}\) is the Jacobian matrix of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) as a function of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) (Definition 9).

ExmExample 20 (Applying the vector chain rule)

Example 20 (Applying the vector chain rule) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {(x_1, x_2)}^{\top}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}&= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \\ &= {(x_1 + x_2,\ x_1 x_2)}^{\top}, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} z &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) \\ &= y_1^2 + y_2. \end{aligned} \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}_{2 \times 1} &= \underbrace{\frac{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}_{2 \times 2} \underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}}_{2 \times 1} && \text{(vector chain rule)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_2 \\ 1 & x_1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2y_1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(differentiate } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \text{ and } f \text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 2(x_1 + x_2) + x_2 \\ 2(x_1 + x_2) + x_1 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply, and substitute } y_1 = x_1 + x_2 \text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 2x_1 + 3x_2 \\ 3x_1 + 2x_2 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(collect terms)} \end{aligned} \]

This matches differentiating \(z\) directly:

\[ \begin{aligned} z &= (x_1 + x_2)^2 + x_1 x_2 \\ &= x_1^2 + 3x_1 x_2 + x_2^2, \end{aligned} \]

so \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} z = 2x_1 + 3x_2\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} z = 3x_1 + 2x_2\). The product in the other order, a \(2 \times 1\) matrix times a \(2 \times 2\) matrix, is not defined. The total derivative is the transpose, the row vector \((2x_1 + 3x_2,\ 3x_1 + 2x_2)\).

See Felippa [12], and Wikipedia contributors [4], section “Relationship with Frechet derivative”.

CorCorollary 4 (Vector chain rule for quadratic forms)

Corollary 4 (Vector chain rule for quadratic forms) If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\) is an \(n \times 1\) vector-valued function of the \(p \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), with continuous partial derivatives, then

\[ \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}{\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\right)\mathclose{}}}_{p \times 1} = \underbrace{\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\right)\mathclose{}}_{p \times n} \underbrace{\mathopen{}\left(2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\right)\mathclose{}}_{n \times 1} \]

ProofProof

Proof. Apply Theorem 9 with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\), \(q = n\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} z &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}) \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}: \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\right)\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\right)\mathclose{} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}}\mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(vector chain rule)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\right)\mathclose{} \mathopen{}\left(2\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(derivative of a simple quadratic form, in } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

The second step is Corollary 3, applied to the \(n \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\) in place of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\).

ExmExample 21 (Derivative of the residual sum of squares)

Example 21 (Derivative of the residual sum of squares) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) (\(n \times 1\)) and \(\mathbf{X}\) (\(n \times p\)) be constant with respect to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\) be the residual vector (Definition 12). By Theorem 5, \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}(\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = {\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\), and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= \mathbf{0}_{p \times n}\) because \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) is constant, so \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}= -{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\) (\(p \times n\)). By Corollary 4:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\right)\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left(-{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{} \mathopen{}\left(2\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(vector chain rule for quadratic forms)} \\ &= -2\,{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(substitute } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\varepsilon}}\vphantom{\varepsilon}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Setting this \(p \times 1\) vector to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) gives the normal equations \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= {\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) of least squares.

DefDefinition 15 (Matrix derivative (gradient))

Definition 15 (Matrix derivative (gradient)) For a scalar-valued function \(f(\mathbf{X})\) of an \(m \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{X}\), the matrix derivative [8] is the \(m \times n\) matrix whose \((i,j)\) entry is the partial derivative of \(f\) with respect to the \((i,j)\) entry of \(\mathbf{X}\):

\[ \mathopen{}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{X}} f\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} = \frac{\partial}{\partial X_{ij}} f \]

Like the vector derivative (Definition 2), the matrix derivative is also called the gradient of \(f\) with respect to \(\mathbf{X}\), written \(\nabla_{\mathbf{X}} f\).

ExmExample 22 (The matrix derivative of a trace)

Example 22 (The matrix derivative of a trace) Let \(\mathbf{X}\) be a \(2 \times 2\) matrix and

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathbf{X}) &= \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{X}) \\ &= X_{11} + X_{22} \end{aligned} \]

(see trace). Then \(\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{ij}} f = 1\) if \(i = j\) and \(0\) otherwise, so:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{X}} f = \mathbf{I}_2 \]

LemTheorem 10 (Matrix derivative of the trace of a matrix product)

Theorem 10 (Matrix derivative of the trace of a matrix product) If \(\mathbf{A}\) (\(r \times m\)) and \(\mathbf{B}\) (\(n \times r\)) are constant with respect to the \(m \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{X}\), then:

\[ \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{X}} \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{X} \mathbf{B})}_{m \times n} = \underbrace{{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}_{m \times r} \underbrace{{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}}_{r \times n} \]

ProofProof

Proof. Write \(A_{kl}\), \(X_{kl}\), and \(B_{kl}\) for the entries of \(\mathbf{A}\), \(\mathbf{X}\), and \(\mathbf{B}\). For entry \((i,j)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{X}} \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{X} \mathbf{B})\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial X_{ij}} \sum_{a=1}^{r} \sum_{b=1}^{m} \sum_{c=1}^{n} A_{ab} X_{bc} B_{ca} && \text{(trace of the } r \times r \text{ product } \mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{B} \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{a=1}^{r} A_{ai} B_{ja} && \text{(only the terms with } b = i,\ c = j \text{ depend on } X_{ij} \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{a=1}^{r} \mathopen{}\left[{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\right]\mathclose{}_{ia} \mathopen{}\left[{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\right]\mathclose{}_{aj} && \text{(definition of the transpose)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} && \text{(definition of matrix multiplication)} \end{aligned} \]

RemarkRemark 10 (Why the theorem uses the trace)

Remark 10 (Why the theorem uses the trace). The trace makes \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{X} \mathbf{B})\) a scalar, so its matrix derivative (Definition 15) is again an \(m \times n\) matrix. The matrix product \(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{X} \mathbf{B}\) itself (without the trace) is an \(r \times r\) matrix, and each of its \(r^2\) entries has a partial derivative with respect to each of the \(m n\) entries of \(\mathbf{X}\). Those \(r^2 m n\) partial derivatives form a four-index array (a fourth-order tensor), not a matrix, which is why this result is stated for the scalar \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{X} \mathbf{B})\).

For example, with

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A} &= \mathbf{B} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_2, \end{aligned} \]

the product \(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{X} \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{X}\) has \(4\) entries, each with \(4\) partial derivatives, \(16\) in all, while its trace \(X_{11} + X_{22}\) has the \(4\) partial derivatives that form the \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(\mathbf{I}_2\) of Example 22.

ExmExample 23 (Differentiating a weighted trace)

Example 23 (Differentiating a weighted trace) Let \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{I}_2\) (\(2 \times 2\)) and \(\mathbf{B} = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix}\) (\(2 \times 2\)). Then \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{X} \mathbf{B}) = 2X_{11} + 3X_{22}\), and:

\[ \begin{aligned} \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{X}} \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{X} \mathbf{B})}_{2 \times 2} &= \underbrace{{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}_{2 \times 2} \underbrace{{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}}_{2 \times 2} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_2 \begin{pmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

ExrExercise 7 (A gradient that does not mention its variable)

Exercise 7 (A gradient that does not mention its variable) Take

\[A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\]

let \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3}\) vary, and define

\[g(W) = \sum_{i=1}^{2} \sum_{j=1}^{3} W_{ij} A_{ij}\]

which is the entry-by-entry version of the inner product for matrices. Compute \(\nabla_W\, g(W)\), and say what is unusual about the answer.

SolutionSolution 7

Solution 7. Written out over the six positions,

\[g(W) = 2W_{11} - W_{12} + 0\,W_{13} + 4W_{21} + 3W_{22} - 2W_{23}\]

Each \(W_{ij}\) appears in exactly one term, multiplied by \(A_{ij}\) and by nothing else, so differentiating with respect to it leaves \(A_{ij}\) behind:

\[\frac{\partial g}{\partial W_{ij}} = A_{ij}\]

Collecting those partial derivatives into a matrix of the same shape as \(W\), as Table 1 requires,

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_W\, g(W) &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= A \end{aligned} \]

What is unusual is that the gradient came out constant: it does not mention \(W\) at all. The constant answer is not a quirk of this particular \(A\). \(g\) is a linear function of \(W\), and the gradient of a linear function is constant everywhere, for the same reason the derivative of \(f(w) = cw\) is \(c\) no matter where it is evaluated.

A constant gradient is the easy case, and it is not the case we usually face. Most functions we minimize are curved — the residual sum of squares of a linear model (Definition 14) is the standard example — so their gradient changes from point to point and the downhill direction has to be worked out afresh at every step.

LemTheorem 11 (Gradient of an entrywise inner product with a fixed matrix)

Theorem 11 (Gradient of an entrywise inner product with a fixed matrix) Let \(A\) be a fixed \(m \times n\) matrix, and for \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) define

\[g(W) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n} W_{ij} A_{ij}\]

Then for every \(W\), \(\nabla_W\, g(W) = A\). In particular, the gradient is constant: it does not depend on \(W\).

ProofProof

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 7, worked in Solution 7, where \(m = 2\), \(n = 3\), and the entries of \(A\) are given. The same argument works for every \(A\): each \(W_{ij}\) appears in exactly one term, multiplied by \(A_{ij}\), so \(\frac{\partial g}{\partial W_{ij}} = A_{ij}\).

DefDefinition 16 (Open ball)

Definition 16 (Open ball) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) and \(r > 0\). The open ball [13] of radius \(r\) around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the set \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in \mathbb{R}^p : \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < r\right\}\mathclose{}\), of points whose distance from \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) (the Euclidean norm of the difference) is less than \(r\).

DefDefinition 17 (Closed ball)

Definition 17 (Closed ball) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(r\) as in Definition 16, the closed ball of radius \(r\) around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in \mathbb{R}^p : \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \le r\right\}\mathclose{}\).

DefDefinition 18 (Unit ball)

Definition 18 (Unit ball) The open and closed balls (Definition 16, Definition 17) of radius \(1\) around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) are the open and closed unit balls.

ExmExample 24 (Points in the unit balls of \(\mathbb{R}^2\))

Example 24 (Points in the unit balls of \(\mathbb{R}^2\))  

  • \({(0.5, 0.5)}^{\top}\) has

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert{(0.5, 0.5)}^{\top}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 &= 0.25 + 0.25 \\ &= 0.5 \\ &< 1, \end{aligned} \]

    so it is in both the open and the closed unit ball.

  • \({(0.6, 0.8)}^{\top}\) has

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert{(0.6, 0.8)}^{\top}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 &= 0.36 + 0.64 \\ &= 1, \end{aligned} \]

    so it is in the closed unit ball but not the open one.

  • For \(p = 1\), the open ball of radius \(r\) around \(c\) is the open interval \((c - r, c + r)\).

DefDefinition 19 (Second partial derivative)

Definition 19 (Second partial derivative) Let \(f\) be a scalar-valued function of a \(p \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), and let \(i, j \in \mathopen{}\left\{1, \ldots, p\right\}\mathclose{}\). Suppose the partial derivative \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f\) (Definition 1) exists at every point of an open ball around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) (Definition 16). The second partial derivative of \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), first with respect to \(x_j\) and then with respect to \(x_i\), is

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}, \]

the partial derivative with respect to \(x_i\) of the function \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f\), when it exists.

ExmExample 25 (Second partial derivatives of \(x_1^2 x_2 + 3 x_2\))

Example 25 (Second partial derivatives of \(x_1^2 x_2 + 3 x_2\)) For \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 x_2 + 3 x_2\) of Example 1, \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 2 x_1 x_2\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + 3\). Differentiating each of these again:

  • \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} (2 x_1 x_2) \\ &= 2 x_2; \end{aligned} \]

  • \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} (2 x_1 x_2) \\ &= 2 x_1; \end{aligned} \]

  • \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} (x_1^2 + 3) \\ &= 2 x_1; \end{aligned} \]

  • \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} (x_1^2 + 3) \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

The two mixed partial derivatives are both \(2 x_1\).

DefDefinition 21 (Hessian matrix)

Definition 21 (Hessian matrix) Let \(f\) be a scalar-valued function of a \(p \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) whose first partial derivatives exist on an open ball around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) (Definition 16) and whose second partial derivatives (Definition 19) exist at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\). The Hessian matrix [14] of \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the derivative (Definition 7) of the transposed gradient \({\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}\) (Definition 2):

\[ \underbrace{\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}_{p \times p} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}. \tag{4}\]

ExmExample 26 (A Hessian matrix)

Example 26 (A Hessian matrix) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = e^{2x_1 + x_2} - x_1\) for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {(x_1, x_2)}^{\top}\), where \(e\) is Euler’s number, and write \(u = 2x_1 + x_2\). By the chain rule, \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} e^{u} = 2 e^{u}\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} e^{u} = e^{u}\), so

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 e^{u} - 1 \\ e^{u} \end{bmatrix}. \]

Differentiating each entry again,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} (2 e^{u} - 1) & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} e^{u} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} (2 e^{u} - 1) & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} e^{u} \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 e^{u} & 2 e^{u} \\ 2 e^{u} & e^{u} \end{bmatrix}, \end{aligned} \]

and at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), where \(u = 0\), \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\).

ExmExample 27 (A function with a gradient but no Hessian at a point)

Example 27 (A function with a gradient but no Hessian at a point) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1 \mathopen{}\left|x_1\right|\mathclose{}\) for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {(x_1, x_2)}^{\top}\), where \(\mathopen{}\left|\cdot\right|\mathclose{}\) is the absolute value. For \(x_1 > 0\), \(f = x_1^2\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f = 2 x_1\); for \(x_1 < 0\), \(f = -x_1^2\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f = -2 x_1\); and at \(x_1 = 0\) the difference quotient is \(h \mathopen{}\left|h\right|\mathclose{} / h = \mathopen{}\left|h\right|\mathclose{} \to 0\). So the gradient exists everywhere: it is \({(2 \mathopen{}\left|x_1\right|\mathclose{},\ 0)}^{\top}\). But \(2 \mathopen{}\left|x_1\right|\mathclose{}\) has no derivative in \(x_1\) at \(x_1 = 0\) (its difference quotient \(2 \mathopen{}\left|h\right|\mathclose{} / h\) is \(2\) for \(h > 0\) and \(-2\) for \(h < 0\)), so \(\mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right]\mathclose{}_{11}\), and with it the Hessian, does not exist at any \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) with \(x_1 = 0\).

LemTheorem 12 (Other ways to write the Hessian, and its entries)

Theorem 12 (Other ways to write the Hessian, and its entries) Let \(f\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) be as in Definition 21.

  1. The Hessian can be written with the row-vector derivative (Definition 6) of \(f\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}}\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= \frac{\partial^2 f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}}. \end{aligned} \tag{5}\]

  2. The \((i, j)\) entry of the Hessian is

    \[ \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}. \tag{6}\]

ProofProof

Proof. Part 1. By Theorem 2, the transposed gradient \({\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}\) equals the row-vector derivative \(\frac{\partial f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}{\partial {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}}\). Substituting it into Equation 4 gives the first line. The second line is the same expression written as a second derivative. It follows the shape rule of matrix multiplication (Remark 2): the \(p \times 1\) column-vector operator \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}\) stands on the left of a \(1 \times p\) row vector, giving a \(p \times p\) matrix.

Part 2. Let \(y_j \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) be entry \(j\) of the gradient. By Definition 7, the \((i, j)\) entry of the derivative of the vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= (y_1, \ldots, y_p)\) with respect to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} &= \mathopen{}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}^{\top}\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} && \text{(}\href{#eq-hessian}{\text{Equation~4}}\text{, with } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\text{ the gradient)} \\ &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} y_j && \text{(}\href{#def-vector-valued-derivative}{\text{Definition~7}}\text{)} \\ &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}. && \text{(} y_j = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 28 (Computing the Hessian entry by entry, and checking it by finite differences)

DefDefinition 22 (Continuity at a point in several variables)

Definition 22 (Continuity at a point in several variables) A function \(f\) from \(\mathbb{R}^p\) to \(\mathbb{R}^q\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) if for every \(\varepsilon> 0\) there is a \(\delta> 0\) such that \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \varepsilon\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\). For \(q = 1\), \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{}\).

DefDefinition 23 (Continuity on a set in several variables)

Definition 23 (Continuity on a set in several variables) A function \(f\) from \(\mathbb{R}^p\) to \(\mathbb{R}^q\) is continuous on a set if it is continuous at every point of the set (Definition 22).

ExmExample 29 (A continuous function, and a discontinuous one)

Example 29 (A continuous function, and a discontinuous one)  

  • \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1 + x_2\) is continuous at every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\). Each \(\mathopen{}\left|y_i - x_i\right|\mathclose{} \le \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\), since the squared length is a sum of nonnegative squares, so

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left|(y_1 - x_1) + (y_2 - x_2)\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(subtract)} \\ &\le \mathopen{}\left|y_1 - x_1\right|\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left|y_2 - x_2\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(triangle inequality for numbers)} \\ &\le 2\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}, && \text{(each term is at most } \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

    which is less than \(\varepsilon\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta= \varepsilon/ 2\).

  • \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 1\) if \(x_1 > 0\) and \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 0\) otherwise is not continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\): for \(\varepsilon= \tfrac{1}{2}\) and any \(\delta> 0\), the point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= {(\delta/ 2, 0)}^{\top}\) has \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \delta/ 2 < \delta\) but \(\mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}})\right|\mathclose{} = 1 > \tfrac{1}{2}\).

LemTheorem 13 (Continuity in one variable, and sums, multiples and compositions)

Theorem 13 (Continuity in one variable, and sums, multiples and compositions)  

  1. For \(p = q = 1\), a function \(f\) is continuous at \(x\) in the sense of Definition 22 exactly when \(\lim_{y \to x} f(y) = f(x)\) (limit), which is the usual continuity at \(x\).
  2. If \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^q\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(g : \mathbb{R}^q \to \mathbb{R}^r\) is continuous at \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\), then the composition \(g \circ f\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).
  3. If \(f, g : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) are continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), then \(f + g\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).
  4. If \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(c \in \mathbb{R}\), then \(c f\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).

If the hypotheses of part 2, 3 or 4 hold at every point of a set, the conclusion holds at every point of that set.

ProofProof

Proof. Let \(\varepsilon> 0\) be given.

Part 1. For \(p = q = 1\), \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert f(y) - f(x)\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \mathopen{}\left|f(y) - f(x)\right|\mathclose{}\) and \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert y - x\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \mathopen{}\left|y - x\right|\mathclose{}\). Definition 22 then asks for a \(\delta> 0\) such that \(\mathopen{}\left|f(y) - f(x)\right|\mathclose{} < \varepsilon\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left|y - x\right|\mathclose{} < \delta\). The limit definition with \(L = f(x)\) asks for the same inequality, but only whenever \(0 < \mathopen{}\left|y - x\right|\mathclose{} < \delta\). The two agree, because at \(y = x\) the inequality \(\mathopen{}\left|f(x) - f(x)\right|\mathclose{} = 0 < \varepsilon\) holds for every \(\delta\).

Part 2. Because \(g\) is continuous at \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\), there is a \(\delta_g > 0\) such that \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}) - g(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}))\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \varepsilon\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta_g\). Because \(f\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), applied with \(\delta_g\) in the role of \(\varepsilon\), there is a \(\delta> 0\) such that \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta_g\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\). So if \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} = f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\) has \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta_g\), and therefore \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert g(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})) - g(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}))\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \varepsilon\).

Part 3. Because \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), there are \(\delta_f > 0\) and \(\delta_g > 0\) such that \(\mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} < \varepsilon/ 2\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta_f\), and \(\mathopen{}\left|g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} < \varepsilon/ 2\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta_g\). Let \(\delta\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\min\mathopen{}\left\{\delta_f, \delta_g\right\}\mathclose{}\). If \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\), then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left|(f + g)(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - (f + g)(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left|\mathopen{}\left(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left(g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(regroup)} \\ &\le \mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left|g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(triangle inequality for numbers)} \\ &< \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} && \text{(choice of } \delta\text{)} \\ &= \varepsilon. && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

Part 4. Because \(f\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), there is a \(\delta> 0\) such that \(\mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} < \varepsilon/ (\mathopen{}\left|c\right|\mathclose{} + 1)\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\). For such \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left|c f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - c f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left|c\right|\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(factor out } c \text{)} \\ &\le \mathopen{}\left|c\right|\mathclose{}\,\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathopen{}\left|c\right|\mathclose{} + 1} && \text{(choice of } \delta\text{)} \\ &< \varepsilon. && \text{(} \mathopen{}\left|c\right|\mathclose{} < \mathopen{}\left|c\right|\mathclose{} + 1 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 30 (Choosing \(\delta\) for a sum, a multiple and a composition)

Example 30 (Choosing \(\delta\) for a sum, a multiple and a composition) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1\) and \(g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_2\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\), and \(h(t) = 3t\) on \(\mathbb{R}\). Since \(\mathopen{}\left|y_i - x_i\right|\mathclose{} \le \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\), the functions \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous with \(\delta= \varepsilon\), and \(h\) is continuous with \(\delta= \varepsilon/ 3\), because \(\mathopen{}\left|h(s) - h(t)\right|\mathclose{} = 3\,\mathopen{}\left|s - t\right|\mathclose{}\). Take \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (1, 2)\) and \(\varepsilon= 0.1\). The proof of Theorem 13 gives these values of \(\delta\):

  • for \(f + g\) (part 3), \(\delta= \min\mathopen{}\left\{\varepsilon/ 2, \varepsilon/ 2\right\}\mathclose{}\);
  • for \(3 f\) (part 4), \(\delta= \varepsilon/ (3 + 1)\);
  • for \(h \circ (f + g)\) (part 2), the outer value \(\delta_h = \varepsilon/ 3\) plays the role of \(\varepsilon\) for \(f + g\), so \(\delta= \delta_h / 2\).

The code draws many points \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) at distance less than \(\delta\) from \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and finds the largest change in each function.

deltas <- c(
  sum = eps / 2,
  multiple = eps / (3 + 1),
  composition = (eps / 3) / 2
)
funs <- list(
  sum = fun_sum, multiple = fun_multiple, composition = fun_composition
)

n <- 10000L
largest_change <- sapply(names(funs), function(nm) {
  radius <- deltas[[nm]] * sqrt(runif(n))
  angle <- runif(n, 0, 2 * pi)
  y <- cbind(x[1] + radius * cos(angle), x[2] + radius * sin(angle))
  max(abs(apply(y, 1, funs[[nm]]) - funs[[nm]](x)))
})
data.frame(delta = deltas, largest_change = largest_change, eps = eps)

For 3 of the 3 functions, the largest change among the 10000 points is below \(\varepsilon= 0.1\), as Theorem 13 guarantees.

ExmExample 31 (Partial derivatives can exist where a function is not continuous)

Example 31 (Partial derivatives can exist where a function is not continuous) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\) be

\[ f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x_1 x_2}{x_1^2 + x_2^2} & \text{if } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}, \\ 0 & \text{if } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}. \end{cases} \]

Both partial derivatives exist at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). For \(h \ne 0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} f(h, 0) &= \frac{h \cdot 0}{h^2 + 0} \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

so

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{f(0 + h, 0) - f(0, 0)}{h} &= \frac{0 - 0}{h} && \text{(} f(h, 0) = 0 \text{ and } f(0, 0) = 0 \text{)} \\ &= 0, && \text{(} 0 / h = 0 \text{ for } h \ne 0 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

and the limit as \(h \to 0\) is \(0\): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) = 0\) (Definition 1). The same steps with \(f(0, h) = 0\) give \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) = 0\).

But \(f\) is not continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) (Definition 22). Take \(\varepsilon= \tfrac{1}{4}\) and any \(\delta> 0\), and let \(t = \delta/ 2\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= {(t, t)}^{\top}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= \sqrt{t^2 + t^2} \\ &= t \sqrt{2} \\ &= \delta/ \sqrt{2} \\ &< \delta, \end{aligned} \]

but

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left|f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}})\right|\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left|\frac{t \cdot t}{t^2 + t^2} - 0\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\text{, since } t > 0 \text{)} \\ &= \frac{t^2}{2 t^2} && \text{(} t^2 + t^2 = 2 t^2 \text{)} \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2}, && \text{(cancel } t^2 > 0 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

which is more than \(\varepsilon= \tfrac{1}{4}\).

The partial derivatives only look along the two coordinate axes, where \(f\) is \(0\); along the line \(x_1 = x_2\), \(f\) is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) everywhere except at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). So partial derivatives existing at a point says little about how \(f\) behaves near it.

LemTheorem 14 (Symmetry of the Hessian)

Theorem 14 (Symmetry of the Hessian) If the second partial derivatives of \(f\) exist and are continuous (Definition 22) on an open ball around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) (Definition 16), then \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}\) for all \(i, j\) [15], so \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) (Definition 21) is symmetric (symmetric matrix).

The proof applies the one-variable mean value theorem twice, which these notes do not develop; see [16], Theorem 9.41, which is stated for two variables: apply it to \(f\) as a function of \(x_i\) and \(x_j\), with the other coordinates held fixed.

ExmExample 32 (Mixed partial derivatives agree)

Example 32 (Mixed partial derivatives agree) In Example 26, the \((1, 2)\) and \((2, 1)\) entries of \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) are both \(2 e^{2x_1 + x_2}\).

ExmExample 33 (Without continuity, the mixed partials can differ)

Example 33 (Without continuity, the mixed partials can differ) Let \(f(x_1, x_2) = \dfrac{x_1 x_2 (x_1^2 - x_2^2)}{x_1^2 + x_2^2}\) for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), and \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) = 0\). For \(x_2 \ne 0\), the partial derivative (Definition 1) in \(x_1\) at \((0, x_2)\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(0, x_2) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(h, x_2) - f(0, x_2)}{h} && \text{(definition of the partial derivative, }\href{#def-partial-derivative}{\text{Definition~1}}\text{)} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x_2 (h^2 - x_2^2)}{h^2 + x_2^2} && \text{(} f(0, x_2) = 0 \text{; cancel } h \text{)} \\ &= \frac{x_2 \cdot (-x_2^2)}{x_2^2} \\ &= -x_2, && \text{(the quotient is continuous at } h = 0 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) &= \lim_{h \to 0} (0 - 0)/h \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

so \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f(0, x_2) = -x_2\) holds at \(x_2 = 0\) too. In the same way, with the roles of \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) swapped, \(f(x_1, k) / k = x_1 (x_1^2 - k^2) / (x_1^2 + k^2) \to x_1\), so \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} f(x_1, 0) = x_1\) for every \(x_1\). So at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) (Definition 21)

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}})\right]\mathclose{}_{21} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} f\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= \frac{d }{d x_2} (-x_2) \\ &= -1, \\ \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}})\right]\mathclose{}_{12} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} f\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= \frac{d }{d x_1} x_1 \\ &= 1: \end{aligned} \]

the Hessian at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is not symmetric. By Theorem 14, then, the second partial derivatives of this \(f\) cannot all be continuous near \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

LemTheorem 15 (Hessian of a quadratic form)

Theorem 15 (Hessian of a quadratic form) If \(\mathbf{S}\) is a symmetric \(p \times p\) matrix that is constant with respect to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), then \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) has \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 2 \mathbf{S}\) for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} ({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} && \text{(}\href{#def-hessian}{\text{Definition~21}}\text{)} \\ &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} {\mathopen{}\left(2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} && \text{(}\href{#cor-quadratic-form-symmetric}{\text{Corollary~2}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{}\,{(2 \mathbf{S})}^{\top} && \text{(}\href{#thm-deriv-matrix-vector}{\text{Theorem~6}}\text{, with } \mathbf{A} = 2 \mathbf{S} \text{ and } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\text{; } \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} {(2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}^{\top} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} (2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \text{, }\href{#def-vector-valued-derivative}{\text{Definition~7}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p\,{(2 \mathbf{S})}^{\top} && \text{(} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathbf{I}_p \text{, }\href{#rem-deriv-matrix-vector-special-case}{\text{Remark~4}}\text{)} \\ &= 2 \mathbf{S}. && \text{(} \mathbf{S} \text{ is symmetric)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 34 (The Hessian of a \(2 \times 2\) quadratic form)

Example 34 (The Hessian of a \(2 \times 2\) quadratic form) For \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) as in Example 17, Theorem 15 gives \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\). Directly, the gradient found there, with \(\beta_{i}\) renamed \(x_i\), is \({(6 x_1 + 2 x_2,\ 2 x_1 + 4 x_2)}^{\top}\), and differentiating its entries by \(x_1\) and by \(x_2\) (Definition 21) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} (6 x_1 + 2 x_2) & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1} (2 x_1 + 4 x_2) \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} (6 x_1 + 2 x_2) & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} (2 x_1 + 4 x_2) \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]
