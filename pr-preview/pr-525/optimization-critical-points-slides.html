1 Critical points and optimization

Definition 1 (Flat point (stationary point)) Let \(f\) be differentiable at \(c\). If \(f'(c) = 0\), then \(c\) is a flat point of \(f\) (also called a stationary point) [1].

Example 1 (Flat points, and a point that is not one)  

  • \(f(w) = w^3\) has \(f'(w) = 3w^2\), which is \(0\) at \(w = 0\), so \(0\) is a flat point. Yet \(f(0) = 0\) is neither the minimum nor the maximum of the values \(f\) takes on any open interval around \(0\): \(f(w) < 0\) for \(w < 0\), and \(f(w) > 0\) for \(w > 0\).
  • \(h(w) = w^2\) has \(h'(1) = 2 \ne 0\), so \(1\) is not a flat point.

Exercise 1 (Differentiate a quadratic) Let \(f(w) = w^2 - 4w + 7\).

Differentiate \(f\).

Solution 1. Differentiating each term:

\[\frac{df}{dw} = 2w - 4\]

Exercise 2 (Find the flat point) In the setting of Exercise 1, find the flat point \(w\) of \(f\).

Solution 2. The derivative is zero where \(2w - 4 = 0\), which gives \(w = 2\).

Exercise 3 (Classify the flat point) In the setting of Exercise 1, say whether \(f(w)\) at the flat point is the minimum or the maximum of the values of \(f\).

Solution 3. The flat point is \(w = 2\), from Solution 2. Evaluating \(f\) there gives

\[ \begin{aligned} f(2) &= 4 - 8 + 7 \\ &= 3. \end{aligned} \]

This is the minimum of the values of \(f\). Completing the square, \(f(w) = (w - 2)^2 + 3\), since

\[ \begin{aligned} (w - 2)^2 + 3 &= w^2 - 4w + 4 + 3 \\ &= w^2 - 4w + 7, \end{aligned} \]

and \((w - 2)^2 \ge 0\), so \(f(w) \ge 3 = f(2)\) for every \(w\). Equivalently, the derivative is negative below \(w = 2\) and positive above it, so the function falls into that point and rises out of it.

Exercise 4 (Slope at \(w = 1\)) In the setting of Exercise 1, evaluate the derivative at \(w = 1\).

Solution 4. At \(w = 1\) the derivative is \(2(1) - 4 = -2\), so \(f\) is falling there.

Exercise 5 (Predict a nearby value) In the setting of Exercise 1, use the linear approximation and the derivative at \(w = 1\) from Exercise 4 to predict \(f(1.01)\).

Solution 5. With \(\varepsilon= 0.01\), the linear approximation predicts a change of \((0.01)(-2) = -0.02\), from

\[ \begin{aligned} f(1) &= 1 - 4 + 7 \\ &= 4 \end{aligned} \]

to \(3.98\).

Exercise 6 (Exact value) In the setting of Exercise 1, find the exact value of \(f(1.01)\).

Solution 6. The exact value is

\[ \begin{aligned} f(1.01) &= (1.01)^2 - 4(1.01) + 7 \\ &= 1.0201 - 4.04 + 7 \\ &= 3.9801 \end{aligned} \]

a change of \(-0.0199\) from \(f(1) = 4\).

Exercise 7 (Check the prediction) Compare the prediction in Exercise 5 with the exact value of \(f(1.01)\) from Exercise 6.

Solution 7. The prediction \(3.98\) (Solution 5) and the exact value \(3.9801\) (Solution 6) differ by \(0.0001\), which is \(\varepsilon^2\). The linear approximation drops everything of that order and smaller, so halving the step quarters the error. That trade is the whole bargain of gradient descent, the step-by-step method of fitting models that the optimization page defines. We take a step in the direction the derivative recommends, and the recommendation is trustworthy only as far as the step is small.

Definition 2 (Critical point) Let \(f\) be a function defined on an open interval containing \(c\). The point \(c\) is a critical point of \(f\) if either \(f'(c) = 0\) or \(f\) is not differentiable at \(c\) (Definition 6 in Derivatives and Taylor Series).

Example 2 (Critical points that are and are not flat points)  

  • \(g(w) = \mathopen{}\left|w\right|\mathclose{}\) has no derivative at \(w = 0\): its one-sided derivatives there are \(1\) and \(-1\) (worked example). So the difference quotient \(\tfrac{\mathopen{}\left|h\right|\mathclose{} - 0}{h}\) has no limit as \(h \to 0\), because the limits from the left and from the right (one-sided limits) differ. So \(0\) is a critical point of \(g\) but not a flat point.

  • \(f(w) = w^3\) has

    \[ \begin{aligned} f'(0) &= 3 \cdot 0^2 \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

    so \(0\) is a flat point of \(f\), and hence a critical point.

  • \(h(w) = w^2\) is differentiable everywhere, with \(h'(w) = 2w\), which is \(0\) only at \(w = 0\). So \(0\) is the only critical point of \(h\). For example, \(h'(1) = 2 \ne 0\), so \(1\) is not one.

Theorem 1 (Every flat point is a critical point) If \(c\) is a flat point of \(f\) (Definition 1), then \(c\) is a critical point of \(f\) (Definition 2).

Example 3 (The flat points of a cubic, and a critical point that is not flat) For \(f(w) = w^3 - 3w\), the derivative is \(f'(w) = 3w^2 - 3\). The code differentiates \(f\) with D() and evaluates \(f'\) at several points. For \(g(w) = \mathopen{}\left|w\right|\mathclose{}\), it computes the difference quotients at \(0\) from both sides.

f_prime <- D(quote(w^3 - 3 * w), "w")
points <- c(-2, -1, 0, 1, 2)
derivs <- data.frame(w = points, f_prime = eval(f_prime, list(w = points)))
derivs
flat <- derivs$w[derivs$f_prime == 0]

h <- c(-1e-3, 1e-3)
quotients <- abs(h) / h
quotients
#> [1] -1  1

The derivative is \(0\) at 2 of the 5 points, namely \(w = -1\) and \(w = 1\). These are flat points of \(f\), so by Theorem 1 they are critical points. The difference quotients of \(g\) at \(0\) are -1 from the left and 1 from the right, so \(g\) has no derivative at \(0\). Thus \(0\) is a critical point of \(g\) (Definition 2), but not a flat point.

Exercise 8 (Maximizing a gamma-family kernel) Adapted from Miller [2], Question 1.1.10.

Find the maximum value of

\[f(x) = x^4 e^{-x}\]

on the interval \([0, \infty)\).

Solution 8. Differentiate using the product rule:

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 4x^3 e^{-x} - x^4 e^{-x} \\ &= x^3 e^{-x}(4 - x) \end{aligned} \]

On \([0, \infty)\), \(e^{-x} > 0\), so \(f'(x) = 0\) only at \(x = 0\) and \(x = 4\).

Evaluate \(f\) at the boundary and critical points:

  • At \(x = 0\): \(f(0) = 0\).

  • At \(x = 4\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} f(4) &= 4^4 e^{-4} \\ &= 256 e^{-4} \\ &\approx 4.6888. \end{aligned} \]

  • As \(x \to \infty\): exponential decay dominates polynomial growth, so \(\lim_{x \to \infty} x^4 e^{-x} = 0\).

Since \(f(x) \ge 0\) for all \(x \ge 0\) and \(f(4) > 0\), the global maximum occurs at \(x = 4\), with maximum value \(256/e^4\).

Exercise 9 (Classifying critical points with the second derivative test) Adapted from Miller [2], Question 1.1.11.

Find the critical points of

\[f(x) = 4x^3 - 3x^2\]

and decide whether each is a local maximum, a local minimum, or an inflection point.

Solution 9. Compute the first derivative:

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 12x^2 - 6x \\ &= 6x(2x - 1) \end{aligned} \]

Setting \(f'(x) = 0\) gives critical points at \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1/2\).

Compute the second derivative:

\[f''(x) = 24x - 6\]

Apply the second derivative test at each critical point:

  • At \(x = 0\): \(f''(0) = -6 < 0\), so \(x = 0\) is a strict local maximum.

  • At \(x = 1/2\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} f''(1/2) &= 24(1/2) - 6 \\ &= 6 \\ &> 0, \end{aligned} \]

    so \(x = 1/2\) is a strict local minimum.

Inflection points occur where the second derivative changes sign (\(f''(x) = 0\) at \(x = 1/4\)). Because \(f''\) is non-zero at both critical points, neither is an inflection point.

A local minimizer of a differentiable function on an open interval is a flat point (Definition 1), and, at a flat point, a positive second derivative guarantees a strict local minimum. This section extends both facts to a function \(f\) of a \(p \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\): the gradient \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) (gradient) takes the place of the first derivative, and a \(p \times p\) matrix of second partial derivatives takes the place of the second; both facts are proved in this section, for every \(p \ge 1\). Throughout, \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}})\) means the gradient evaluated at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}\).

Theorem 2 (A minimizer has derivative zero) Let \(g\) be a real-valued function on an open interval containing \(c\), and let \(g\) be differentiable at \(c\). If \(c\) is a local minimizer of \(g\), then \(g'(c) = 0\). [4]

Proof. Take \(\delta> 0\) as in the definition of a local minimizer, shrunk if needed so that \((c - \delta, c + \delta)\) lies inside the interval; then \(g(c) \le g(c + h)\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left|h\right|\mathclose{} < \delta\). For \(0 < h < \delta\), the difference quotient is

\[ \frac{g(c + h) - g(c)}{h} \ge 0, \]

because its numerator is at least \(0\) and its denominator is positive. As \(h \to 0\) from the right, these quotients tend to \(g'(c)\) (the two-sided limit exists, so the one-sided limit equals it), and a limit of numbers that are all at least \(0\) is at least \(0\); so \(g'(c) \ge 0\). For \(-\delta< h < 0\), the numerator is still at least \(0\) but the denominator is negative, so the quotient is at most \(0\), and in the same way, with \(h \to 0\) from the left, \(g'(c) \le 0\). Together, \(g'(c) = 0\).

Example 4 (Applying the test, and its limits)  

  • \(g(x) = (x - 2)^2\) has its minimizer at \(c = 2\), and

    \[ \begin{aligned} g'(2) &= 2 (2 - 2) \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

  • The converse fails: \(g(x) = x^3\) has

    \[ \begin{aligned} g'(0) &= 3 \cdot 0^2 \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

    but \(0\) is not a local minimizer, since

    \[ \begin{aligned} g(-h) &= -h^3 \\ &< 0 \\ &= g(0) \end{aligned} \]

    for every \(h > 0\).

  • Differentiability is needed: \(g(x) = \mathopen{}\left|x\right|\mathclose{}\) has its minimizer at \(0\), but the difference quotient \(\mathopen{}\left|h\right|\mathclose{} / h\) is \(1\) for \(h > 0\) and \(-1\) for \(h < 0\), so it has no limit and \(g'(0)\) does not exist.

Definition 3 (Stationary point) Let \(f\) be a scalar-valued function of a \(p \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\). A point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}\) at which the gradient exists and

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1} \]

is a stationary point of \(f\).

Example 5 (A stationary point, and a point that is not one) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 2 x_1\). Its gradient is \({(2 x_1 - 2,\ 2 x_2)}^{\top}\), which is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\) exactly when \(x_1 = 1\) and \(x_2 = 0\). So \({(1, 0)}^{\top}\) is its only stationary point, and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is not one: the gradient there is \({(-2, 0)}^{\top}\).

Remark 1 (Stationary points in one variable). For \(p = 1\), a stationary point is a flat point (Definition 1).

Theorem 3 (First-order necessary condition) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\). If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) is a local minimizer of \(f\) and the gradient of \(f\) exists at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) is a stationary point of \(f\) (Definition 3).

Proof. Take \(\delta> 0\) as in the definition of a local minimizer. Let \(i \in \mathopen{}\left\{1, \ldots, p\right\}\mathclose{}\), let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i\) be the vector with \(1\) in entry \(i\) and \(0\) elsewhere, and let \(g_i(t) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* + t\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i)\) for \(t \in \mathbb{R}\). For \(\mathopen{}\left|t\right|\mathclose{} < \delta\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* + t\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i) - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left|t\right|\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i\right\rVert\mathclose{} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left|t\right|\mathclose{} \\ &< \delta, \end{aligned} \]

so

\[ \begin{aligned} g_i(0) &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) \\ &\le f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* + t\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i) \\ &= g_i(t): \end{aligned} \]

\(0\) is a local minimizer of \(g_i\). Moving \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) from \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) along \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i\) changes only \(x_i\), so \(g_i'(0)\) is the partial derivative \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\), which exists because the gradient does. By Theorem 2,

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) &= g_i'(0) \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

The equation \(g_i'(0) = 0\) holds for every \(i\), so every entry of the gradient at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) is \(0\) (vector derivative).

Example 6 (Using the condition to locate, and to rule out, minimizers)  

  • For \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 2 x_1\) of Example 5, any local minimizer must be the stationary point \({(1, 0)}^{\top}\). It is one: completing the square,

    \[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= (x_1 - 1)^2 + x_2^2 - 1 \\ &\ge -1 \\ &= f(1, 0). \end{aligned} \]

  • For \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = e^{2x_1 + x_2} - x_1\) of Example, the second entry of the gradient is \(e^{2x_1 + x_2} > 0\), so \(f\) has no stationary point, and so no local minimizer.

  • The converse fails: \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 - x_2^2\) has gradient \({(2 x_1,\ -2 x_2)}^{\top}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is a stationary point, but

    \[ \begin{aligned} f(0, t) &= -t^2 \\ &< 0 \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) \end{aligned} \]

    for every \(t \ne 0\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is not a local minimizer.

Example 7 (A stationary point of the sum of squared errors, on real data) The Auto data record the horsepower and fuel economy (miles per gallon) of 392 car models sold between 1970 and 1982. They come from the StatLib library at Carnegie Mellon University and ship with the book An Introduction to Statistical Learning, in the Python package ISLP (ISLP.load_data("Auto")) and the R package ISLR2 (ISLR2::Auto). This site has a copy at data/auto.csv, and both code tabs below read that file.

Let \(y_i\) be the miles per gallon and \(x_i\) the horsepower of car \(i\). To predict miles per gallon from horsepower with a line, write \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= {(\beta_{0}, \beta_{1})}^{\top}\) and let \(\mathbf{X}\) be the matrix whose row \(i\) is \((1, x_i)\). The sum of squared errors is

\[ f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2, \]

a scalar-valued function of the \(2 \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\). Its gradient is \(-2\,{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\). By Theorem 3, a minimizer of \(f\) must make this gradient \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), which is the equation \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= {\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\). The code below solves that equation and evaluates the gradient at the solution and at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

auto = pd.read_csv("data/auto.csv")
y = auto["mpg"].to_numpy(dtype=float)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(auto)), auto["horsepower"].to_numpy(dtype=float)])

def grad(beta):
    return -2 * X.T @ (y - X @ beta)

beta_hat = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)
grad_hat = grad(beta_hat)
grad_zero = grad(np.zeros(2))

pd.DataFrame(
    {"beta": [beta_hat, np.zeros(2)], "gradient": [grad_hat, grad_zero]},

)

Theorem 4 (Taylor’s theorem with a second-order remainder) Let \(a < b\), and let \(g\) be a real-valued function on \([a, b]\) whose derivative \(g'\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) and whose second derivative \(g''\) exists at every point of \((a, b)\). Then there is a \(\tau\in (a, b)\) with [5]

\[ g(b) = g(a) + g'(a)\,(b - a) + \frac{1}{2}\,g''(\tau)\,(b - a)^2. \]

This theorem is the case \(n = 2\) of [6], Theorem 5.15; its proof uses the mean value theorem, which these notes do not develop.

Example 8 (The remainder point for \(e^x\)) Take \(g(x) = e^x\) on \([0, 1]\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} g' &= g'' \\ &= g. \end{aligned} \]

Theorem 4 says \(e = 1 + 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\,e^{\tau}\) for some \(\tau\in (0, 1)\). Solving,

\[ \begin{aligned} e^{\tau} &= 2 (e - 2) \\ &\approx 2 \times 0.71828 \\ &= 1.43656, \end{aligned} \]

so \(\tau= \log 1.43656 \approx 0.362\) (natural logarithm), which is in \((0, 1)\).

Theorem 5 (Second-order Taylor theorem in several variables) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) have first and second partial derivatives that are continuous on \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (continuity), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\). Then there is a \(\tau\in (0, 1)\) with [5]

\[ f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}) = f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}) + {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} + \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \tau\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}. \]

Proof. Let \(g(t) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\) for \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), the values of \(f\) along the line through \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}\) in the direction \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\). The inner function \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}(t) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\) has entries \(y_j = z_j + t h_j\), so \(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} y_j = h_j\), and its derivative (vector-valued derivative) is the \(1 \times p\) matrix \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\). By the vector chain rule (vector chain rule, with its input \(t\) of length \(1\), its inner function \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}(t) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\), and its outer function \(f\)),

\[ \begin{aligned} g'(t) &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}) && \text{(vector chain rule)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^ph_j\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}). && \text{(matrix product)} \end{aligned} \]

The second step writes out the matrix product. \(g'\) is continuous (continuity): \(t \mapsto \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\) is continuous, since \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + s \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}) - (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \mathopen{}\left|s - t\right|\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\), each \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f\) is continuous by assumption, and compositions, sums and constant multiples of continuous functions are continuous. Each \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f\) has continuous partial derivatives, the second partial derivatives of \(f\), so the same chain-rule computation applies to it: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \mathopen{}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\right]\mathclose{} = \sum_{i=1}^ph_i\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\right)\mathclose{}\). So

\[ \begin{aligned} g''(t) &= \sum_{j=1}^ph_j \sum_{i=1}^ph_i\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(differentiate each term of } g'(t) \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^p\sum_{j=1}^ph_i\,\mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\right]\mathclose{}_{ij}\,h_j && \text{(definition of the Hessian; reorder the finite sums)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}. && \text{(matrix product)} \end{aligned} \]

The last step is again the matrix product. Now apply Theorem 4 to \(g\) on \([0, 1]\): there is a \(\tau\in (0, 1)\) with

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}) &= g(1) && \text{(definition of } g \text{)} \\ &= g(0) + g'(0)\,(1 - 0) + \frac{1}{2}\,g''(\tau)\,(1 - 0)^2 && \text{(}\href{#thm-taylor-1d}{\text{Theorem~4}}\text{, with } a = 0, b = 1 \text{)} \\ &= g(0) + g'(0) + \frac{1}{2}\,g''(\tau) && \text{(} 1 - 0 = 1 \text{)} \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}) + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}) + \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \tau\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} && \text{(substitute } g(0), g'(0), g''(\tau) \text{)} \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}) + {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} + \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \tau\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}. && \text{(a } 1 \times 1 \text{ matrix equals its transpose)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 9 (For a quadratic form the expansion is exact) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) with \(\mathbf{S}\) symmetric and constant. Its gradient is \(2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) (derivative of a quadratic form) and its Hessian is \(2 \mathbf{S}\) at every point (Hessian of a quadratic form), so whatever \(\tau\) is, Theorem 5 reads

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}) &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + {(2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}})}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} + \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} (2 \mathbf{S}) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + 2\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}, \end{aligned} \]

using

\[ \begin{aligned} {(2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}})}^{\top} &= 2\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{S}}^{\top} \\ &= 2\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S}. \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying out directly gives the same:

\[ \begin{aligned} {(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})}^{\top} \mathbf{S} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}) &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} && \text{(distribute)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + 2\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}. && \text{(} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} = {({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}})}^{\top} \text{, which equals } \end{aligned} \]

Definition 4 (Strict local minimizer) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\). A point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) is a strict local minimizer of \(f\) if there is a number \(\delta> 0\) such that \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) < f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) with \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\).

Example 10 (Strict and non-strict local minimizers)  

  • \(f(x) = (x - 2)^2\) has

    \[ \begin{aligned} f(2) &= 0 \\ &< (x - 2)^2 \\ &= f(x) \end{aligned} \]

    for every \(x \ne 2\), so \(2\) is a strict local minimizer (any \(\delta> 0\) works).

  • A constant function \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 0\) has \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) \le f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) for all \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), so every point is a local minimizer, but no point is a strict one: \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) < f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) never holds.

Lemma 1 (A quadratic form is at least the smallest eigenvalue times the squared length) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be a symmetric \(p \times p\) matrix, and let \(\lambda_{\min}\) be the smallest of its eigenvalues (eigendecomposition). Then \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} \ge \lambda_{\min}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2\) for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\).

Proof. Write \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{\Lambda} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\) with \(\mathbf{Q}\) orthogonal and \(\mathbf{\Lambda} = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_p)\) (spectral theorem), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{\Lambda} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} && \text{(substitute the eigendecomposition)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}^{\top} \mathbf{\Lambda} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} && \text{(} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q} = {({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})}^{\top} \text{, transpose of a product)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^p\lambda_i\,y_i^2 && \text{(} \mathbf{\Lambda} \text{ is diagonal)} \\ &\ge \sum_{i=1}^p\lambda_{\min}\,y_i^2 && \text{(} \lambda_i \ge \lambda_{\min} \text{ and } y_i^2 \ge 0 \text{)} \\ &= \lambda_{\min}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(the squared length is the sum of squares)} \\ &= \lambda_{\min}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2. && \text{(orthogonal matrices preserve length)} \end{aligned} \]

The second step is the transpose of a product. The last step applies orthogonal matrices preserve length to \({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\), which is orthogonal because

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q} &= \mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p \end{aligned} \]

(orthogonal matrix).

Example 11 (Checking the bound) \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) has eigenvalues \(3\) and \(1\) (eigenvalue example), so \(\lambda_{\min} = 1\). At \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} = {(1, 0)}^{\top}\), \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} = 2 \ge 1 \cdot 1\). At \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} = {(1, -1)}^{\top}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} &= 2 - 1 - 1 + 2 \\ &= 2 \\ &= 1 \cdot 2: \end{aligned} \]

the bound holds with equality, since this \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\) is an eigenvector for \(\lambda_{\min}\).

Lemma 2 (A quadratic form with small entries is small) Let \(\mathbf{E}\) be a \(p \times p\) matrix whose entries all satisfy \(\mathopen{}\left|e_{ij}\right|\mathclose{} \le m\). Then \(\mathopen{}\left|{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{E} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right|\mathclose{} \le p\,m\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2\) for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\).

Proof. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{(\mathopen{}\left|h_1\right|\mathclose{}, \ldots, \mathopen{}\left|h_p\right|\mathclose{})}^{\top}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{(1, \ldots, 1)}^{\top}\), both in \(\mathbb{R}^p\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left|{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{E} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right|\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left|\sum_{i=1}^p\sum_{j=1}^ph_i\,e_{ij}\,h_j\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(matrix product)} \\ &\le \sum_{i=1}^p\sum_{j=1}^p\mathopen{}\left|h_i\right|\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left|e_{ij}\right|\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left|h_j\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(triangle inequality for numbers)} \\ &\le m \sum_{i=1}^p\sum_{j=1}^p\mathopen{}\left|h_i\right|\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left|h_j\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(} \mathopen{}\left|e_{ij}\right|\mathclose{} \le m \text{)} \\ &= m\,\mathopen{}\left(\sum_{i=1}^p\mathopen{}\left|h_i\right|\mathclose{}\right)\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(the double sum factors)} \\ &= m\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}})^2 && \text{(dot product)} \\ &\le m\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(Cauchy-Schwarz, squared)} \\ &= m\,p\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2. && \text{(} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 = p \text{ and } \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 = \textstyle\sum_i h_i^2 \text{, which equals } \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

The steps use the matrix product, the dot product and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Example 12 (Checking the bound) Let \(\mathbf{E} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 & -0.2 \\ -0.2 & 0.1 \end{bmatrix}\), so \(m = 0.2\) works, and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} = {(1, 1)}^{\top}\), so \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 = 2\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{E} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} &= 0.1 - 0.2 - 0.2 + 0.1 \\ &= -0.2, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left|-0.2\right|\mathclose{} &= 0.2 \\ &\le 2 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 2 \\ &= 0.8. \end{aligned} \]

Theorem 6 (Second-order sufficient condition) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) have first and second partial derivatives that are continuous on \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (continuity). If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) is a stationary point of \(f\) (Definition 3) and \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\) is positive definite, then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) is a strict local minimizer of \(f\) (Definition 4).

Proof. \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\) is positive definite, and so symmetric, so its smallest eigenvalue \(\lambda\) is positive (definiteness and eigenvalues). Each of the \(p^2\) entries of \(\mathbf{H}_f\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) (continuity): for \(\varepsilon= \lambda/ (2p)\) there is a \(\delta_{ij} > 0\) with \(\mathopen{}\left|\mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} - \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\right]\mathclose{}_{ij}\right|\mathclose{} < \lambda/ (2p)\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta_{ij}\). Let \(\delta\) be the smallest of these \(p^2\) numbers.

Now let \(0 < \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\). By Theorem 5 there is a \(\tau\in (0, 1)\) for which the first step of the next display holds; let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* + \tau\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\) and \(\mathbf{E} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\). Since \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \tau\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\), every entry of \(\mathbf{E}\) has absolute value less than \(\lambda/ (2p)\), so Lemma 2 applies with \(m = \lambda/ (2p)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) &= {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} + \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} && \text{(}\href{#thm-taylor-mv}{\text{Theorem~5}}\text{)} \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} && \text{(} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* \text{ is stationary)} \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} + \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{E}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} && \text{(} \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) = \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) + \mathbf{E} \text{; distribute)} \\ &\ge \frac{1}{2}\,\lambda\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 + \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{E}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} && \text{(}\href{#lem-qf-eigen-bound}{\text{Lemma~1}}\text{)} \\ &\ge \frac{1}{2}\,\lambda\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 - \frac{1}{2}\,\mathopen{}\left|{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{E}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(a number is at least minus its absolute value)} \\ &\ge \frac{1}{2}\,\lambda\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 - \frac{1}{2}\,p\,\frac{\lambda}{2p}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(}\href{#lem-qf-entry-bound}{\text{Lemma~2}}\text{, } m = \lambda/ (2p) \text{)} \\ &= \frac{\lambda}{4}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(arithmetic)} \\ &> 0. && \text{(} \lambda> 0 \text{ and } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

So \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) < f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) whenever \(0 < \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\) (take \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\)).

Example 13 (Classifying stationary points)  

  • \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 2 x_1\) has the stationary point \({(1, 0)}^{\top}\) (Example 5), and its Hessian is

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= 2 \mathbf{I}_2, \end{aligned} \]

    which is positive definite, so \({(1, 0)}^{\top}\) is a strict local minimizer.

  • \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 - x_2^2\) has the stationary point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) and Hessian \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\), which is not positive definite (\({(0, 1)}^{\top}\) gives \(-2\)), and indeed \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is not a local minimizer (Example 6).

Example 14 (The condition is sufficient, not necessary) \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^4\) and \(g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 - x_2^4\) both have gradient \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) and the same Hessian there, \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), since

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} (\pm x_2^4)\right)\mathclose{} &= \pm 12 x_2^2 \\ &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

at \(x_2 = 0\). That Hessian is not positive definite (\({(0, 1)}^{\top}\) gives \(0\)), so Theorem 6 says nothing about either function. In fact \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is a strict local minimizer of \(f\), since \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) > 0 = f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}})\) for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), but not a local minimizer of \(g\), since

\[ \begin{aligned} g(0, t) &= -t^4 \\ &< 0 \\ &= g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) \end{aligned} \]

for \(t \ne 0\).

Definition 5 (Local maximizer) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\). A point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* \in \mathbb{R}^p\) is a local maximizer of \(f\) if there is a number \(\delta> 0\) such that \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) \ge f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) for all \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) with \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\).

Example 15 (A local maximizer) Let \(f(x) = 1 - x^2\). For every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} f(0) - f(x) &= 1 - (1 - x^2) && \text{(substitute)} \\ &= x^2 && \text{(simplify)} \\ &\ge 0, && \text{(a square)} \end{aligned} \]

so \(f(0) \ge f(x)\) for all \(x\), and \(0\) is a local maximizer of \(f\) with any \(\delta> 0\).

Definition 6 (Saddle point) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\). A stationary point of \(f\) that is neither a local minimizer nor a local maximizer of \(f\) is a saddle point of \(f\) [7].

Example 16 (A saddle point) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 - x_2^2\). Its gradient is \({(2 x_1,\ -2 x_2)}^{\top}\), which is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\) only at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\) is a stationary point. For every \(t \ne 0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} f(t, 0) &= t^2 && \text{(substitute)} \\ &> 0 && \text{(} t \ne 0 \text{)} \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}), && \text{(substitute)} \\ f(0, t) &= -t^2 && \text{(substitute)} \\ &< 0 && \text{(} t \ne 0 \text{)} \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}). && \text{(substitute)} \end{aligned} \]

So every ball around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\) contains a point with a larger value of \(f\) and a point with a smaller one. Hence \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\) is neither a local minimizer nor a local maximizer, and it is a saddle point.
