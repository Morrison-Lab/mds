1 Vectors

ExrExercise 1 (Store a data point as one object)

Exercise 1 (Store a data point as one object) A house has a floor area of \(120\) square meters, \(3\) bedrooms, and an age of \(15\) years.

  1. Write these three numbers as a single vertical list \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), in the order given, and call its entries \(x_1\), \(x_2\) and \(x_3\). What is \(x_2\)?
  2. Does the list \((3, 120, 15)\) describe the same house?
SolutionSolution 1

Solution 1.

  1. Stacking the three numbers in order gives

    \[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \begin{bmatrix} 120 \\ 3 \\ 15 \end{bmatrix}, \]

    so \(x_2 = 3\), the number of bedrooms.

  2. No. The list \((3, 120, 15)\) would describe a house with \(3\) square meters of floor area and \(120\) bedrooms. An entry’s position says what it measures, so the order of the entries matters.

DefDefinition 1 (Column vector)

Definition 1 (Column vector) A column vector [1] of length \(p\) is an ordered list of \(p\) real numbers, written vertically as a \(p \times 1\) array:

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ \vdots \\ x_{p} \end{bmatrix} \]

RemarkRemark 1 (Vectors are columns by default)

Remark 1 (Vectors are columns by default). In these notes, as in most statistics textbooks, a vector is a column vector unless stated otherwise. A vector written inline as a list in parentheses is still a column vector: for example, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (2, -1, 5)\) means

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}. \]

RemarkRemark 2 (Why the vector mark sits under the symbol)

Remark 2 (Why the vector mark sits under the symbol). In these notes, the mark that shows a symbol is a vector is a tilde under the letter, as in \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\). Many books instead use a bold letter or an arrow over the letter. We chose a mark underneath because bold letters are hard to write by hand on a board, and because the space above a letter is already used by the hat that marks an estimated value, as in \(\hat{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}}\). The notation choices page gives the full reasons and an example of an estimated vector.

DefDefinition 2 (The set \(\mathbb{R}^p\))

Definition 2 (The set \(\mathbb{R}^p\)) For a positive integer \(p\), \(\mathbb{R}^p\) is the set of all column vectors of length \(p\) whose entries are real numbers.

RemarkRemark 3 (Reading \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\))

Remark 3 (Reading \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\)). Writing \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) says, in one symbol, that \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is a column vector with \(p\) real entries. For example, \((1, -2, 0.5) \in \mathbb{R}^3\). The vector \((1, 2)\) is not in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), because it has only \(2\) entries; it is in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). In Exercise 1, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^3\).

DefDefinition 3 (Scalar)

Definition 3 (Scalar) In linear algebra, a scalar [2] is a single real number, as opposed to a vector or a matrix, which holds several numbers.

ExmExample 1 (Scalars and a vector)

Example 1 (Scalars and a vector) The numbers \(5\) and \(-0.2\) are scalars. The column vector \((5, -0.2)\) is not a scalar: it has \(2\) entries, so it is in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) (Definition 2), and each of its \(2\) entries is a scalar.

DefDefinition 4 (Real-valued function, scalar-valued function)

Definition 4 (Real-valued function, scalar-valued function) A function \(f : A \to \mathbb{R}\), whose values are numbers, is real-valued (also called scalar-valued, since its values are scalars).

DefDefinition 5 (Vector-valued function)

Definition 5 (Vector-valued function) A function \(f : A \to \mathbb{R}^m\), whose values are vectors (Definition 2), is vector-valued.

ExmExample 2 (A real-valued and a vector-valued function)

Example 2 (A real-valued and a vector-valued function) On \(\mathbb{R}^2\), \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1 + x_2\) is real-valued and \(g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \begin{bmatrix}x_1 + x_2 \\ x_1 - x_2\end{bmatrix}\) is vector-valued:

\[ f\mathopen{}\left(\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}\right)\mathclose{} = 3 + 4 = 7, \qquad g\mathopen{}\left(\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}\right)\mathclose{} = \begin{bmatrix}3 + 4 \\ 3 - 4\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}7 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} \]

DefDefinition 6 (Transpose)

Definition 6 (Transpose) The transpose of a column vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the row vector with the same sequence of entries, written horizontally:

\[ {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \equiv \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}' \equiv [x_1,\; x_2,\; \ldots,\; x_p] \]

ExmExample 3 (Transposing a column vector)

Example 3 (Transposing a column vector) The transpose of the column vector with entries \(2\), \(-1\), \(5\) is the row vector with the same entries:

\[ {\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}}^{\top} = [2,\; -1,\; 5]. \]

DefDefinition 7 (Vector addition)

Definition 7 (Vector addition) The sum of two column vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) of the same length \(p\) is the column vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) of length \(p\) obtained by adding entry by entry:

\[(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})_i \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}x_i + y_i, \quad i = 1, \ldots, p\]

ExmExample 4 (Adding two vectors)

Example 4 (Adding two vectors) \[ \begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 + 4 \\ 2 + 5 \\ 3 + 6 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 7 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 8 (Dot product)

Definition 8 (Dot product) For any two real-valued vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (x_1, \ldots, x_p)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= (y_1, \ldots, y_p)\) of the same length \(p\), the dot product of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) is:

\[\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^px_i y_i \tag{1}\]

See also the definitions in:

  • Dobson and Barnett [3], Section 1.3 (equation 1.1, page 7)

  • Kaplan [4], chapter on vectors

  • wikipedia

The dot product has a different generalization for two matrices; see wikipedia for more.

ExmExample 5 (A dot product)

Example 5 (A dot product) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (1, 2, 3)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= (4, 5, 6)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} &= 1 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 5 + 3 \cdot 6 && \text{(definition of the dot product)} \\ &= 4 + 10 + 18 && \text{(multiply)} \\ &= 32 && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 9 (Linear combination)

Definition 9 (Linear combination) A linear combination of the numbers \(a_1, \ldots, a_k\) with coefficients \(c_1, \ldots, c_k\) is the sum

\[c_1 a_1 + \cdots + c_k a_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i a_i\]

A linear combination of vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\) of the same length is defined entry by entry: entry \(j\) of \(\sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_i\) is the linear combination of the \(j\)th entries of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\) with the same coefficients.

DefDefinition 10 (Coefficients of a linear combination)

Definition 10 (Coefficients of a linear combination) The numbers \(c_1, \ldots, c_k\) in a linear combination (Definition 9) \(\sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i a_i\) are its coefficients.

ExmExample 6 (Coefficients of a linear combination of numbers)

Example 6 (Coefficients of a linear combination of numbers) The linear combination of the numbers \(4\) and \(5\) with coefficients \(2\) and \(3\) is \(2 \cdot 4 + 3 \cdot 5 = 8 + 15 = 23\).

DefDefinition 11 (Weighted sum)

Definition 11 (Weighted sum) A weighted sum of the numbers \(a_1, \ldots, a_k\) is a linear combination of them, with coefficients \(w_1, \ldots, w_k\):

\[w_1 a_1 + \cdots + w_k a_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i a_i\]

Multiply each \(a_i\) by its coefficient \(w_i\), then add.

DefDefinition 12 (Weight)

Definition 12 (Weight) The coefficients \(w_1, \ldots, w_k\) of a weighted sum \(\sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i a_i\) are called its weights. The weight \(w_i\) says how much \(a_i\) counts.

ExmExample 7 (The total price of a shopping basket)

Example 7 (The total price of a shopping basket) A basket holds \(3\) items that cost \(\$2\) each and \(2\) items that cost \(\$5\) each. The total price is the weighted sum of the prices \(2\) and \(5\) with weights \(3\) and \(2\), the count of items at each price:

\[ \begin{aligned} 3 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 5 &= 6 + 10 && \text{(multiply each price by its weight)} \\ &= 16 && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 13 (Weighted average, weighted mean)

Definition 13 (Weighted average, weighted mean) A weighted average (or weighted mean) of the numbers \(a_1, \ldots, a_k\) is a weighted sum of them whose weights \(w_1, \ldots, w_k\) are non-negative and add to \(1\):

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i a_i \quad\text{with}\quad w_i \ge 0 \text{ for each } i \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i = 1 \]

An \(a_i\) with a larger weight counts for more.

ExmExample 8 (A course grade)

Example 8 (A course grade) A course grade counts the midterm for \(1/4\) and the final for \(3/4\). A student scores \(70\) on the midterm and \(90\) on the final. The weights \(1/4\) and \(3/4\) are non-negative and add to \(1\), so the grade is a weighted average of \(70\) and \(90\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{4} \cdot 70 + \frac{3}{4} \cdot 90 &= 17.5 + 67.5 && \text{(multiply each score by its weight)} \\ &= 85 && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

The grade \(85\) lies between \(70\) and \(90\), and nearer to \(90\), which has the larger weight.

ExmExample 9 (A linear combination of two vectors)

Example 9 (A linear combination of two vectors) The linear combination of \({(1, 0)}^{\top}\) and \({(0, 1)}^{\top}\) with coefficients \(2\) and \(3\) is \({(2 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 0,\ 2 \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot 1)}^{\top} = {(2, 3)}^{\top}\).

RemarkRemark 4 (The dot product is a linear combination)

Remark 4 (The dot product is a linear combination). The dot product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) is a linear combination (Definition 9) of the entries of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\), with coefficients \(x_1, \ldots, x_p\). In Example 5, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= 1 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 5 + 3 \cdot 6\) is the linear combination of \(4\), \(5\), and \(6\) with coefficients \(1\), \(2\), and \(3\).

The dot product is also the standard inner product on \(\mathbb{R}^p\); “inner product” is the general notion (Definition 1 in Inner Products and Orthogonality, later in these notes), of which the dot product is one example.

LemTheorem 1 (Dot product is symmetric)

Theorem 1 (Dot product is symmetric) The dot product is symmetric:

\[\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\]

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} &= \sum_{i=1}^px_i y_i && \text{(definition of the dot product)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^py_i x_i && \text{(products of numbers are symmetric)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(definition of the dot product)} \end{aligned} \]

1.1 Special vectors

DefDefinition 14 (Zero vector)

Definition 14 (Zero vector) The zero vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) of length \(p\) has all entries equal to zero:

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}= \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

RemarkRemark 5 (The zero vector is the additive identity)

Remark 5 (The zero vector is the additive identity). Adding the zero vector to a vector leaves it unchanged (Definition 7): \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) for any vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) of the same length, so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is the identity element for vector addition. For example,

\[ \begin{aligned} (2, -1) + (0, 0) &= (2 + 0, -1 + 0) \\ &= (2, -1). \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 15 (Mean, average)

Definition 15 (Mean, average) The mean [5] (or average) of \(n\) numbers \(y_1, \ldots, y_n\) is their sum divided by how many there are:

\[\bar{y} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^ny_i\]

ExmExample 10 (The mean of three numbers)

Example 10 (The mean of three numbers) For \(y_1 = 2\), \(y_2 = -1\) and \(y_3 = 5\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar{y} &= \frac{1}{3}\,\mathopen{}\left(2 + (-1) + 5\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(definition of the mean, with } n = 3 \text{)} \\ &= \frac{1}{3} \cdot 6 && \text{(add)} \\ &= 2 && \text{(multiply)} \end{aligned} \]

RemarkRemark 6 (The sample mean estimates the population mean)

Remark 6 (The sample mean estimates the population mean). When the numbers are observed data values, \(\bar{y}\) is also called the sample mean, an estimator of the population mean.

DefDefinition 16 (Ones vector)

Definition 16 (Ones vector) The ones vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}\) of length \(p\) has all entries equal to one:

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

DefDefinition 17 (Sum of the elements of a vector)

Definition 17 (Sum of the elements of a vector) The sum of the elements of a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) is the sum of its entries:

\[ {s_{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^px_i. \]

In words, \({s_{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}\) is the entries of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) added together.

ExmExample 11 (The sum of the elements of a vector with three entries)

Example 11 (The sum of the elements of a vector with three entries) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (2, -1, 5)\),

\[ \begin{aligned} {s_{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}} &= 2 + (-1) + 5 && \text{(definition of the sum of the elements, with } p = 3 \text{)} \\ &= 6. && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 2 (The ones vector sums the entries)

Theorem 2 (The ones vector sums the entries) For every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\), the sum of the elements of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the dot product of the ones vector with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\):

\[ {s_{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}. \]

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= \sum_{i=1}^p1 \cdot x_i && \text{(}\href{#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~8}}\text{, with every entry of } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}} \text{ equal to } 1 \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^px_i && \text{(multiply each entry by } 1 \text{)} \\ &= {s_{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}. && \text{(}\href{#def-sum-of-elements}{\text{Definition~17}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 12 (The ones vector applied to a vector with three entries)

Example 12 (The ones vector applied to a vector with three entries) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (2, -1, 5)\) and the ones vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}\) with three entries,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= 1 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot(-1) + 1 \cdot 5 && \text{(}\href{#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~8}}\text{)} \\ &= 6, && \text{(multiply and add)} \end{aligned} \]

which is \({s_{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}\), as in Example 11. Dividing by \(p = 3\) gives the mean of the entries, \(6 / 3 = 2\).

DefDefinition 18 (Indicator vector, standard basis vector)

Definition 18 (Indicator vector, standard basis vector) The \(j\)-th indicator vector (or standard basis vector [6] ) \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j\) of length \(p\) has a \(1\) in position \(j\) and \(0\)s elsewhere:

\[ (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j)_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } i = j \\ 0 & \text{if } i \neq j \end{cases} \qquad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \leftarrow \text{position } j \]

RemarkRemark 7 (Indicator vectors build every vector)

Remark 7 (Indicator vectors build every vector). For \(p = 3\), the indicator vectors are \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1 = (1, 0, 0)\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_2 = (0, 1, 0)\), and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_3 = (0, 0, 1)\). Every vector of length \(3\) is a weighted sum \(c_1\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1 + c_2\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_2 + c_3\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_3\) of them, with weights equal to its entries; for example, \((2, -1, 5) = 2\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1 - 1\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_2 + 5\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_3\). No other weights work: entry \(j\) of \(c_1\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1 + c_2\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_2 + c_3\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_3\) is \(c_j\), so each weight \(c_j\) must equal entry \(j\) of the vector.

LemTheorem 3 (Indicator vectors select entries)

Theorem 3 (Indicator vectors select entries) For any vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) of length \(p\) and any \(j \in \{1, \ldots, p\}\):

\[\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= x_j\]

ProofProof

Proof. Writing the dot product componentwise:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= \sum_{i=1}^p(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j)_i\, x_i && \text{(definition of the dot product)} \\&= \sum_{i=1}^p \begin{cases} 1 \cdot x_i & \text{if } i = j \\ 0 \cdot x_i & \text{if } i \neq j \end{cases} && \text{(definition of } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j \text{)} \\&= x_j && \text{(only the } i = j \text{ term is nonzero)} \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 4 (A vector is a linear combination of indicator vectors)

Theorem 4 (A vector is a linear combination of indicator vectors) Every vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) is the linear combination (Definition 9) of the indicator vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_p\) (Definition 18) with coefficients \(x_1, \ldots, x_p\):

\[\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \sum_{j=1}^px_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j\]

ProofProof

Proof. Compare the two sides entry by entry. For each \(i \in \{1, \ldots, p\}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{j=1}^px_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j\right)\mathclose{}_i &= \sum_{j=1}^px_j (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j)_i && \text{(}\href{#def-linear-combination}{\text{Definition~9}}\text{, entry by entry)} \\&= \sum_{j=1}^p \begin{cases} x_j \cdot 1 & \text{if } j = i \\ x_j \cdot 0 & \text{if } j \neq i \end{cases} && \text{(}\href{#def-indicator-vector}{\text{Definition~18}}\text{)} \\&= x_i \cdot 1 + \sum_{j \neq i} x_j \cdot 0 && \text{(split off the } j = i \text{ term)} \\&= x_i + 0 && \text{(times } 1 \text{ changes nothing; times } 0 \text{ gives } 0 \text{)} \\&= x_i && \text{(adding } 0 \text{ changes nothing)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 13 (Expanding a vector in indicator vectors)

Example 13 (Expanding a vector in indicator vectors) \[ \begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} &= 4 \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} + (-1) \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} + 2 \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \\ &= 4 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1 - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_2 + 2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_3 \end{aligned} \]

1.2 Orthogonality

DefDefinition 19 (Orthogonal vectors)

Definition 19 (Orthogonal vectors) Two vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) of the same length are orthogonal [7] (written \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\perp \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\)) if their dot product (Definition 8) is zero:

\[\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\perp \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\iff \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= 0\]

RemarkRemark 8 (Orthogonal means perpendicular)

Remark 8 (Orthogonal means perpendicular). Orthogonality extends the geometric idea of perpendicular lines to vectors with any number of entries. In the plane, \((1, 2)\) and \((-2, 1)\) are perpendicular, and their dot product is \(1 \cdot(-2) + 2 \cdot 1 = 0\). The same test works in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), where pictures are harder to draw:

\[ \begin{aligned} (1, 1, 0) \cdot (1, -1, 5) &= 1 - 1 + 0 \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

so \((1, 1, 0) \perp (1, -1, 5)\).

ExmExample 14 (Vectors that are not orthogonal)

Example 14 (Vectors that are not orthogonal) \[ \begin{aligned} (1, 2) \cdot (1, 1) &= 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 \\ &= 3 \\ &\ne 0, \end{aligned} \]

so \((1, 2)\) and \((1, 1)\) are not orthogonal.

DefDefinition 20 (Euclidean norm (length, \(L_2\) norm))

Definition 20 (Euclidean norm (length, \(L_2\) norm)) The Euclidean norm [8] (or length, also called the \(L_2\) norm and written \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}_2\)) of a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) of length \(p\) is

\[\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sqrt{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^px_i^2} \tag{2}\]

where \(\sqrt{\cdot}\) is the square root.

ExmExample 15 (The length of a vector)

Example 15 (The length of a vector) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (3, 4)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} && \text{(definition of the norm)} \\ &= \sqrt{25} && \text{(square and add)} \\ &= 5 \end{aligned} \]

The vector \((0.6, 0.8)\) has norm \(\sqrt{0.36 + 0.64} = 1\).

DefDefinition 21 (Unit vector)

Definition 21 (Unit vector) A unit vector [9] is a vector whose Euclidean norm (Definition 20) is \(1\). A unit vector is said to have unit length.

ExmExample 16 (A unit vector and a vector that is not one)

Example 16 (A unit vector and a vector that is not one) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (0.6, 0.8)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= \sqrt{0.6^2 + 0.8^2} && \text{(definition of the norm)} \\ &= \sqrt{0.36 + 0.64} && \text{(square)} \\ &= \sqrt{1} && \text{(add)} \\ &= 1 && \text{(take the square root)} \end{aligned} \]

so \((0.6, 0.8)\) is a unit vector. The vector \((1, 1)\) is not:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert(1, 1)\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} \\ &= \sqrt{2} \\ &\ne 1. \end{aligned} \]

ExrExercise 2 (How far apart are two points?)

Exercise 2 (How far apart are two points?) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (1, 2)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= (4, 6)\).

  1. Compute the vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\).
  2. Compute the length of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\), using the Euclidean norm.
  3. Is the result the same if you compute the length of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) instead?
SolutionSolution 2

Solution 2.

  1. \[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}&= (1 - 4, 2 - 6) \\ &= (-3, -4). \end{aligned} \]

  2. \[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= \sqrt{(-3)^2 + (-4)^2} \\ &= \sqrt{25} \\ &= 5. \end{aligned} \]

  3. \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (3, 4)\), and

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} \\ &= 5. \end{aligned} \]

    Yes, the result is the same.

DefDefinition 22 (Euclidean distance)

Definition 22 (Euclidean distance) The Euclidean distance [10] between two vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) of length \(p\) is the Euclidean norm of their difference:

\[d(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^p(x_i - y_i)^2} \tag{3}\]

RemarkRemark 9 (Distance from the origin)

Remark 9 (Distance from the origin). The distance from the origin \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) to a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the norm of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} d(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \end{aligned} \]

[11, p. 39].

For example, the distance from \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) to \((3, 4)\) is \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert(3, 4)\right\rVert\mathclose{} = 5\), as in Example 15.

DefDefinition 23 (Orthonormal vectors)

Definition 23 (Orthonormal vectors) A set of vectors \(\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_2, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_k\}\) is orthonormal if the vectors are mutually orthogonal (Definition 19) and each has norm \(1\) (Definition 20), that is, unit length:

\[\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_j = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } i = j \\ 0 & \text{if } i \neq j \end{cases}\]

ExmExample 17 (Indicator vectors are orthonormal)

Example 17 (Indicator vectors are orthonormal) The indicator vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_2, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_p\) (Definition 18) form an orthonormal set. By Theorem 3, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j\) is entry \(i\) of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_j\), which is \(1\) if \(i = j\) and \(0\) if \(i \neq j\). For \(p = 2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1 &= 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \\ &= 1, \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_2 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_2 &= 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 \\ &= 1, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_2 &= 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 18 (Two ways to fail to be orthonormal)

Example 18 (Two ways to fail to be orthonormal)  

  • \((1, 1)\) and \((1, -1)\) are orthogonal, since \(1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot(-1) = 0\), but not orthonormal: \((1, 1) \cdot (1, 1) = 2 \ne 1\).

  • \((1, 0)\) and \((0.6, 0.8)\) both have unit length, since \((1, 0) \cdot (1, 0) = 1\) and

    \[ \begin{aligned} (0.6, 0.8) \cdot (0.6, 0.8) &= 0.36 + 0.64 \\ &= 1, \end{aligned} \]

    but they are not orthonormal: \((1, 0) \cdot (0.6, 0.8) = 0.6 \ne 0\).

ExrExercise 3 (Compute an inner product and a norm)

Exercise 3 (Compute an inner product and a norm) Let

\[\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}= \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}\]

Compute \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) and \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}_2\).

SolutionSolution 3

Solution 3. Multiply entry by entry and add:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} &=(3)(0) + (-1)(4) + (2)(-2) \\ &= 0 - 4 - 4 \\ &= -8 \end{aligned} \]

For the norm, take the inner product of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}\) with itself first:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} &= 3^2 + (-1)^2 + 2^2 \\ &= 9 + 1 + 4 \\ &= 14 \end{aligned} \]

so

\[\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}_2 = \sqrt{14} \approx 3.742\]

The inner product came out negative while the norm cannot: a norm is a square root of a sum of squares, so it is never below zero.

Python has both operations built in, and R builds them from sum.

import numpy as np

a = np.array([3.0, -1.0, 2.0])
b = np.array([0.0, 4.0, -2.0])

inner = float(a @ b)
norm_a = round(float(np.linalg.norm(a)), 3)

print(inner, norm_a)
#> -8.0 3.742
a <- c(3, -1, 2)
b <- c(0, 4, -2)

print(c(sum(a * b), round(sqrt(sum(a^2)), 3)))
#> [1] -8.000  3.742

Both print -8 and 3.742.
