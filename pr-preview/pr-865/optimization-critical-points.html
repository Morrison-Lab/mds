1 Critical points and optimization

DefDefinition 1 (Flat point (stationary point))

Definition 1 (Flat point (stationary point)) Let \(f\) be differentiable at \(c\). If \(f'(c) = 0\), then \(c\) is a flat point of \(f\) (also called a stationary point) [1].

ExmExample 1 (Flat points, and a point that is not one)

Example 1 (Flat points, and a point that is not one)  

  • \(f(w) = w^3\) has \(f'(w) = 3w^2\), which is \(0\) at \(w = 0\), so \(0\) is a flat point. Yet \(f(0) = 0\) is neither the minimum nor the maximum of the values \(f\) takes on any open interval around \(0\): \(f(w) < 0\) for \(w < 0\), and \(f(w) > 0\) for \(w > 0\).
  • \(h(w) = w^2\) has \(h'(1) = 2 \ne 0\), so \(1\) is not a flat point.

ExrExercise 1 (Differentiate a quadratic)

Exercise 1 (Differentiate a quadratic) A flat point of a function is where its derivative is zero. The next exercises use the parabola \(f(w) = w^2 - 4w + 7\) to find where \(f\) is flat and to compare a linear approximation with the exact value.

Differentiate \(f\).

SolutionSolution 1

Solution 1. Differentiating each term:

\[\frac{df}{dw} = 2w - 4\]

ExrExercise 2 (Find the flat point)

Exercise 2 (Find the flat point) In the setting of Exercise 1, find the flat point \(w\) of \(f\).

SolutionSolution 2

Solution 2. The derivative is zero where \(2w - 4 = 0\), which gives \(w = 2\).

ExrExercise 3 (Classify the flat point)

Exercise 3 (Classify the flat point) In the setting of Exercise 1, say whether \(f(w)\) at the flat point is the minimum or the maximum of the values of \(f\).

SolutionSolution 3

Solution 3. The flat point is \(w = 2\), from Solution 2. Evaluating \(f\) there gives

\[ \begin{aligned} f(2) &= 4 - 8 + 7 \\ &= 3. \end{aligned} \]

This value is the minimum of the values of \(f\). Completing the square, \(f(w) = (w - 2)^2 + 3\), since

\[ \begin{aligned} (w - 2)^2 + 3 &= w^2 - 4w + 4 + 3 \\ &= w^2 - 4w + 7, \end{aligned} \]

and \((w - 2)^2 \ge 0\), so \(f(w) \ge 3 = f(2)\) for every \(w\). Equivalently, the derivative is negative below \(w = 2\) and positive above it, so the function falls into that point and rises out of it.

ExrExercise 4 (Slope at \(w = 1\))

Exercise 4 (Slope at \(w = 1\)) In the setting of Exercise 1, evaluate the derivative at \(w = 1\).

SolutionSolution 4

Solution 4. At \(w = 1\) the derivative is \(2(1) - 4 = -2\), so \(f\) is falling there.

ExrExercise 5 (Predict a nearby value)

Exercise 5 (Predict a nearby value) In the setting of Exercise 1, use the linear approximation and the derivative at \(w = 1\) from Exercise 4 to predict \(f(1.01)\).

SolutionSolution 5

Solution 5. With \(\varepsilon= 0.01\), the linear approximation predicts a change of \((0.01)(-2) = -0.02\), from

\[ \begin{aligned} f(1) &= 1 - 4 + 7 \\ &= 4 \end{aligned} \]

to \(3.98\).

ExrExercise 6 (Exact value)

Exercise 6 (Exact value) In the setting of Exercise 1, find the exact value of \(f(1.01)\).

SolutionSolution 6

Solution 6. The exact value is

\[ \begin{aligned} f(1.01) &= (1.01)^2 - 4(1.01) + 7 \\ &= 1.0201 - 4.04 + 7 \\ &= 3.9801 \end{aligned} \]

a change of \(-0.0199\) from \(f(1) = 4\).

ExrExercise 7 (Check the prediction)

Exercise 7 (Check the prediction) Compare the prediction in Exercise 5 with the exact value of \(f(1.01)\) from Exercise 6.

SolutionSolution 7

Solution 7. The prediction \(3.98\) (Solution 5) and the exact value \(3.9801\) (Solution 6) differ by \(0.0001\), which is \(\varepsilon^2\). For this \(f\), the difference between \(f(w + \varepsilon)\) and its linear approximation is, at any \(w\), exactly

\[ f(w + \varepsilon) - [f(w) + f'(w)\,\varepsilon] = \varepsilon^2, \]

so halving the step quarters that difference.

A step of gradient descent with step length \(s\) changes \(w\) by \(\varepsilon= -s f'(w)\). The linear approximation predicts the change \(-s\,f'(w)^2\) in \(f\), and the difference between \(f(w + \varepsilon)\) and its linear approximation is \(\varepsilon^2 = s^2 f'(w)^2\). The actual change in \(f\) is therefore \(-s\,f'(w)^2\,(1 - s)\). This change is negative exactly when \(0 < s < 1\), for \(f'(w) \ne 0\), so \(f\) decreases only for those steps. For this \(f\), the difference is smaller than the predicted decrease only when \(s < 1\): the step must be short enough.

DefDefinition 2 (Critical point)

Definition 2 (Critical point) Let \(f\) be a function defined on an open interval containing \(c\). The point \(c\) is a critical point of \(f\) if either \(f'(c) = 0\) or \(f\) is not differentiable at \(c\).

ExmExample 2 (Critical points that are and are not flat points)

Example 2 (Critical points that are and are not flat points)  

  • \(g(w) = \mathopen{}\left|w\right|\mathclose{}\) has no derivative at \(w = 0\): its one-sided derivatives there are \(1\) and \(-1\) (see the one-sided derivative example). So the difference quotient \(\tfrac{\mathopen{}\left|h\right|\mathclose{} - 0}{h}\) has no limit as \(h \to 0\), because the one-sided limits from the left and from the right differ. So \(0\) is a critical point of \(g\) but not a flat point.

  • \(f(w) = w^3\) has

    \[ \begin{aligned} f'(0) &= 3 \cdot 0^2 \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

    so \(0\) is a flat point of \(f\), and hence a critical point.

  • \(h(w) = w^2\) is differentiable everywhere, with \(h'(w) = 2w\), which is \(0\) only at \(w = 0\). So \(0\) is the only critical point of \(h\). For example, \(h'(1) = 2 \ne 0\), so \(1\) is not one.

LemTheorem 1 (Every flat point is a critical point)

Theorem 1 (Every flat point is a critical point) If \(c\) is a flat point of \(f\) (Definition 1), then \(c\) is a critical point of \(f\) (Definition 2).

ProofProof

Proof. A flat point \(c\) has \(f\) differentiable at \(c\) and \(f'(c) = 0\). The condition “\(f'(c) = 0\)” is the first alternative in Definition 2, so \(c\) is a critical point.

ExmExample 3 (The flat points of a cubic, and a critical point that is not flat)

Example 3 (The flat points of a cubic, and a critical point that is not flat) For \(f(w) = w^3 - 3w\), the derivative is \(f'(w) = 3w^2 - 3\). The code differentiates \(f\) with D() and evaluates \(f'\) at several points. For \(g(w) = \mathopen{}\left|w\right|\mathclose{}\), it computes the difference quotients at \(0\) from both sides.

f_prime <- D(quote(w^3 - 3 * w), "w")
points <- c(-2, -1, 0, 1, 2)
derivs <- data.frame(w = points, f_prime = eval(f_prime, list(w = points)))
derivs
flat <- derivs$w[derivs$f_prime == 0]

h <- c(-1e-3, 1e-3)
quotients <- abs(h) / h
quotients
#> [1] -1  1

The derivative is \(0\) at 2 of the 5 points, namely \(w = -1\) and \(w = 1\). Both points are flat points of \(f\), since the derivative is \(0\) there, so by Theorem 1 they are critical points. The difference quotients of \(g\) at \(0\) are -1 from the left and 1 from the right, so \(g\) has no derivative at \(0\). Thus \(0\) is a critical point of \(g\) (Definition 2), but not a flat point.

LemTheorem 2 (Second derivative test)

Theorem 2 (Second derivative test) Let \(c\) be a flat point of \(f\), where the second derivative \(f''(c)\) is defined.

  • If \(f''(c) > 0\), then \(f(x) > f(c)\) for every \(x \ne c\) in some open interval around \(c\).
  • If \(f''(c) < 0\), then \(f(x) < f(c)\) for every \(x \ne c\) in some open interval around \(c\).
ProofProof

Proof. Suppose \(f''(c) > 0\). Because \(f'(c) = 0\), the derivative of \(f'\) at \(c\) is

\[f''(c) = \lim_{x \to c} \frac{f'(x)}{x - c}.\]

A limit that is positive forces the quotient \(f'(x)/(x - c)\) to be positive for \(x\) near \(c\). So \(f'(x)\) has the same sign as \(x - c\) on some open interval around \(c\). Hence \(f' < 0\) to the left of \(c\) and \(f' > 0\) to the right of \(c\), so \(f\) is decreasing to the left of \(c\) and increasing to the right of \(c\). Therefore \(f(x) > f(c)\) for every \(x \ne c\) in that open interval.

For \(f''(c) < 0\), apply the same reasoning to \(-f\), since \((-f)''(c) = -f''(c) > 0\). Then \(-f(x) > -f(c)\) near \(c\), which says \(f(x) < f(c)\) near \(c\).

ExmExample 4 (Second derivative test for powers of \(x\))

Example 4 (Second derivative test for powers of \(x\)) The function \(f(x) = x^2\) has the flat point \(c = 0\) and second derivative \(f''(0) = 2 > 0\), so \(0\) is a local minimizer by Theorem 2.

RemarkRemark 1 (The second derivative test can fail to decide)

Remark 1 (The second derivative test can fail to decide). If \(f''(c) = 0\) at a flat point \(c\), the second derivative test does not decide: \(f\) may be smallest at \(c\) among nearby points, largest there, or neither (Example 5).

ExmExample 5 (Three functions with second derivative \(0\) at a flat point)

Example 5 (Three functions with second derivative \(0\) at a flat point) Take \(f(x) = x^4\), \(g(x) = -x^4\) and \(h(x) = x^3\). Each has the derivative \(0\) at \(x = 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 4x^3, & f'(0) &= 0, \\ g'(x) &= -4x^3, & g'(0) &= 0, \\ h'(x) &= 3x^2, & h'(0) &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

So \(0\) is a flat point of all three. Each has the second derivative \(0\) there:

\[ \begin{aligned} f''(x) &= 12x^2, & f''(0) &= 0, \\ g''(x) &= -12x^2, & g''(0) &= 0, \\ h''(x) &= 6x, & h''(0) &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

Because the second derivative is \(0\) in all three cases, Theorem 2 does not decide any of them. Yet the three behave differently near \(0\) (Figure 4):

  • \(f(x) = x^4 > 0 = f(0)\) for every \(x \ne 0\), so \(0\) is a local minimizer.
  • \(g(x) = -x^4 < 0 = g(0)\) for every \(x \ne 0\), so \(0\) is a local maximizer.
  • \(h(x) = x^3\) is negative for \(x < 0\) and positive for \(x > 0\), so \(h(x) < h(0)\) to the left of \(0\) and \(h(x) > h(0)\) to the right of \(0\). Thus \(0\) is neither a local minimizer nor a local maximizer.

DefDefinition 3 (Inflection point)

Definition 3 (Inflection point) An inflection point of a function \(f\) is a point \(c\) such that, on some open interval around \(c\), the second derivative \(f''\) is positive on one side of \(c\) and negative on the other.

RemarkRemark 2 (Inflection points change the curvature)

Remark 2 (Inflection points change the curvature). At an inflection point, the graph changes between curving up and curving down.

ExmExample 6 (An inflection point of \(x^3\))

Example 6 (An inflection point of \(x^3\)) For \(f(x) = x^3\), the second derivative is \(f''(x) = 6x\), which is negative for \(x < 0\) and positive for \(x > 0\). So \(0\) is an inflection point of \(f\).

ExrExercise 8 (Maximizing a gamma-family kernel)

Exercise 8 (Maximizing a gamma-family kernel) Adapted from Miller [2], Question 1.1.10.

The gamma density with shape \(a > 0\) has the kernel \(x^{a-1} e^{-x}\) on \([0, \infty)\). A kernel has the same mode as its density, so maximizing the kernel finds the mode of the gamma density without computing the normalizing constant.

For shape \(a = 5\), the kernel is

\[f(x) = x^4 e^{-x}.\]

Find the maximum of \(f\) on \([0, \infty)\).

SolutionSolution 8

Solution 8. Differentiate using the product rule:

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 4x^3 e^{-x} - x^4 e^{-x} \\ &= x^3 e^{-x}(4 - x) \end{aligned} \]

On \([0, \infty)\), \(e^{-x} > 0\), so \(f'(x) = 0\) only at \(x = 0\) and \(x = 4\).

Evaluate \(f\) at the boundary and critical points:

  • At \(x = 0\): \(f(0) = 0\).

  • At \(x = 4\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} f(4) &= 4^4 e^{-4} \\ &= 256 e^{-4} \\ &\approx 4.6888. \end{aligned} \]

  • As \(x \to \infty\): exponential decay dominates polynomial growth, so \(\lim_{x \to \infty} x^4 e^{-x} = 0\).

Since \(f(x) \ge 0\) for all \(x \ge 0\) and \(f(4) > 0\), the global maximum occurs at \(x = 4\), with maximum value \(256/e^4\).

ExrExercise 9 (Classifying critical points with the second derivative test)

Exercise 9 (Classifying critical points with the second derivative test) Adapted from Miller [2], Question 1.1.11.

An optimization algorithm such as Newton’s method or gradient descent can stop at a critical point, which may be a local maximum, a local minimum, or neither. The second derivative test tells these apart.

Consider the cubic

\[f(x) = 4x^3 - 3x^2.\]

Find its critical points, and decide whether each is a local maximum, a local minimum, or an inflection point.

SolutionSolution 9

Solution 9. Compute the first derivative:

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 12x^2 - 6x \\ &= 6x(2x - 1) \end{aligned} \]

Setting \(f'(x) = 0\) gives critical points at \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1/2\).

Compute the second derivative:

\[f''(x) = 24x - 6\]

Apply the second derivative test at each critical point:

  • At \(x = 0\): \(f''(0) = -6 < 0\), so \(x = 0\) is a strict local maximum.

  • At \(x = 1/2\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} f''(1/2) &= 24(1/2) - 6 \\ &= 6 \\ &> 0, \end{aligned} \]

    so \(x = 1/2\) is a strict local minimum.

Inflection points occur where the second derivative changes sign (\(f''(x) = 0\) at \(x = 1/4\)). Because \(f''\) is non-zero at both critical points, neither is an inflection point.
