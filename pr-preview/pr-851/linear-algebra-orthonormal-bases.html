Orthonormal Bases, Outer Products and Hilbert Spaces

1 Orthonormal bases and Gram-Schmidt

NoteSource

Like Section  in Inner Products and the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality, this section is adapted from Zhou [1], used under the MIT License (see the license text in Section  in Subspaces and Rank). The source leaves the proof that orthonormal vectors are linearly independent to class; it is written out here.

LemTheorem 1 (The dot product of a vector with a linear combination)

Theorem 1 (The dot product of a vector with a linear combination) For vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_k \in \mathbb{R}^p\) and numbers \(c_1, \ldots, c_k\), with \(k \ge 1\),

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k} c_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_j\right)\mathclose{} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} c_j\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_j). \]

ProofProof

Proof. Write \(u_{ji}\) for entry \(i\) of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_j\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k} c_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_j\right)\mathclose{} &= \sum_{i=1}^px_i \sum_{j=1}^{k} c_j u_{ji} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~10 in Vectors}}\text{, }\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-linear-combination}{\text{Definition~11 in Vectors}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^p\sum_{j=1}^{k} x_i c_j u_{ji} && \text{(distribute each } x_i \text{ over the inner sum)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{i=1}^px_i c_j u_{ji} && \text{(swap the order of the finite sums)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{i=1}^pc_j x_i u_{ji} && \text{(commute the factors in each product)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{k} c_j \sum_{i=1}^px_i u_{ji} && \text{(factor } c_j \text{ out of the inner sum)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{k} c_j\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_j). && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~10 in Vectors}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 1 (Dotting with a combination of three vectors)

Example 1 (Dotting with a combination of three vectors) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = (1, 2, 3)\) and take the combination \(2\,(1, 0, 0) - (0, 1, 0) + 4\,(0, 0, 1) = (2, -1, 4)\). Directly,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \cdot (2, -1, 4) &= 2 - 2 + 12 \\ &= 12. \end{aligned} \]

By Theorem 1, \(2 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 3 = 12\), the same number.

LemTheorem 2 (Orthonormal vectors are linearly independent)

Theorem 2 (Orthonormal vectors are linearly independent) If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_k\) are orthonormal (Definition 28 in Vectors), then they are linearly independent (Definition 1 in Subspaces and Rank).

ProofProof

Proof. Suppose \(\sum_{j=1}^{k} c_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). Take any \(i \in \mathopen{}\left\{1, \ldots, k\right\}\mathclose{}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} 0 &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}} && \text{(every term of the dot product is } 0 \text{)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \cdot \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k} c_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(substitute the supposed equation)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{k} c_j\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j) && \text{(}\href{#thm-dot-linear-sum}{\text{Theorem~1}}\text{)} \\ &= c_i. && \text{(} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \text{ is } 1 \text{ if } j = i \text{ and } 0 \text{ otherwise)} \end{aligned} \]

So every \(c_i = 0\).

ExmExample 2 (Three orthonormal vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\))

Example 2 (Three orthonormal vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)) Let

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 = (0, 0, 1), \quad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1, 1, 0), \quad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1, -1, 0). \]

Each has norm \(1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 &= \tfrac{1}{2}\,(1 + 1 + 0) \\ &= 1, \end{aligned} \]

and likewise for the others. They are mutually orthogonal: \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 = 0\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_3 = 0\), because \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1\) has its only nonzero entry where the others have \(0\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_3 &= \tfrac{1}{2}\,(1 - 1 + 0) \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

So by Theorem 2 they are linearly independent, with no need to solve \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 + c_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 + c_3 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_3 = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

The converse fails: \((1, 0)\) and \((1, 1)\) are linearly independent, since \(c_1 (1, 0) + c_2 (1, 1) = (c_1 + c_2, c_2)\) is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) only if \(c_2 = 0\) and then \(c_1 = 0\), but they are not orthonormal, since \((1, 0) \cdot (1, 1) = 1 \ne 0\).

DefDefinition 1 (Orthonormal basis)

Definition 1 (Orthonormal basis) An orthonormal basis [2] of a subspace \(\mathcal{S}\) of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) is a basis of \(\mathcal{S}\) (Definition 6 in Subspaces and Rank) whose vectors are orthonormal (Definition 28 in Vectors).

ExmExample 3 (Orthonormal bases of \(\mathbb{R}^3\))

Example 3 (Orthonormal bases of \(\mathbb{R}^3\))  

  • The indicator vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_2, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_3\) are orthonormal
    1. and a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) (Example 16 in Subspaces and Rank).
  • The vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_3\) of Example 2 are orthonormal and linearly independent, so they are a basis of their span (Definition 6 in Subspaces and Rank), which therefore has dimension \(3\) (Definition 9 in Subspaces and Rank) and is all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) (Theorem 11 in Subspaces and Rank, Example 16 in Subspaces and Rank). So they are an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) too.
ExmExample 4 (Bases that are not orthonormal, and orthonormal lists that are not bases)

Example 4 (Bases that are not orthonormal, and orthonormal lists that are not bases)  

  • \((1, 0), (1, 1)\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) (Example 11 in Subspaces and Rank). It is not an orthonormal basis, since \((1, 0) \cdot (1, 1) = 1\).
  • \((1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0)\) is orthonormal but not a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\): its span is the plane \(z = 0\) (Example 6 in Subspaces and Rank), which does not contain \((0, 0, 1)\).
LemTheorem 3 (Coordinates in an orthonormal basis are dot products)

Theorem 3 (Coordinates in an orthonormal basis are dot products) If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_k\) is an orthonormal basis of a subspace \(\mathcal{S}\) (Definition 1), then every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathcal{S}\) satisfies

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i. \]

ProofProof

Proof. The basis spans \(\mathcal{S}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} c_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j\) for some numbers \(c_j\). For each \(i\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \cdot \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k} c_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(substitute)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{k} c_j\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j) && \text{(}\href{#thm-dot-linear-sum}{\text{Theorem~1}}\text{)} \\ &= c_i, && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-orthonormal-vectors}{\text{Definition~28 in Vectors}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

so each coefficient \(c_i\) is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).

ExmExample 5 (Expanding a vector in an orthonormal basis)

Example 5 (Expanding a vector in an orthonormal basis) With the orthonormal basis \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_3\) of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) from Example 3 and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = (1, 2, 3)\): \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = 3\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1 + 2) \\ &= \tfrac{3}{\sqrt{2}} \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_3 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1 - 2) \\ &= -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}. \end{aligned} \]

Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{3} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i &= 3\,(0, 0, 1) + \tfrac{3}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1, 1, 0) - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1, -1, 0) && \text{(substitute)} \\ &= (0, 0, 3) + \mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{3}{2}, \tfrac{3}{2}, 0\right)\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}, 0\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(multiply out each term)} \\ &= (1, 2, 3), && \text{(add entrywise)} \end{aligned} \]

which is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), as Theorem 3 says.

DefDefinition 2 (Gram-Schmidt process)

Definition 2 (Gram-Schmidt process) The Gram-Schmidt process [3] takes vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k \in \mathbb{R}^p\) and, for \(i = 1, 2, \ldots, k\) in turn:

  1. Orthogonalize: \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i - \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j\) (for \(i = 1\) the sum is empty, so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_1 = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1\));
  2. Test: if \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), stop;
  3. Normalize: \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i / \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i\right\rVert\mathclose{}\).

The output is the list \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2, \ldots\) produced before the process stops, or all \(k\) of them if it never stops.

ExmExample 6 (Two steps of Gram-Schmidt in \(\mathbb{R}^3\))

Example 6 (Two steps of Gram-Schmidt in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 = (1, 1, 0)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 = (1, 0, 1)\).

  • Step 1. \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_1 = (1, 1, 0)\), with norm \(\sqrt{2}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1, 1, 0)\).

  • Step 2.

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1 + 0 + 0) \\ &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \end{aligned} \]

    so

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_2 &= (1, 0, 1) - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1, 1, 0) && \text{(orthogonalize)} \\ &= (1, 0, 1) - \mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}, 0\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(multiply out)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}, 1\right)\mathclose{}, && \text{(subtract entrywise)} \end{aligned} \]

    with norm \(\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{4} + 1} = \sqrt{\tfrac{3}{2}}\), so

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 &= \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}\,\mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}, 1\right)\mathclose{} \\ &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\,(1, -1, 2). \end{aligned} \]

As a check,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{12}}\,(1 - 1 + 0) \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 7 (Gram-Schmidt stops on dependent vectors)

Example 7 (Gram-Schmidt stops on dependent vectors) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 = (1, 2)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 = (2, 4)\). Step 1 gives \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\,(1, 2)\). In step 2,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\,(2 + 8) \\ &= \tfrac{10}{\sqrt{5}}, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_2 &= (2, 4) - \tfrac{10}{\sqrt{5}} \cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\,(1, 2) \\ &= (2, 4) - 2\,(1, 2) \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}, \end{aligned} \]

so the process stops: \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2\) is a multiple of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1\).

LemTheorem 4 (What Gram-Schmidt produces)

Theorem 4 (What Gram-Schmidt produces) Run the Gram-Schmidt process (Definition 2) on \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k \in \mathbb{R}^p\).

  1. If it has produced \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i\) without stopping, then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i\) are orthonormal and \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i\right\}\mathclose{} = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i\right\}\mathclose{}\).
  2. If it reaches step \(i\) (that is, it did not stop at steps \(1, \ldots, i - 1\)), it stops there exactly when \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i \in \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_{i-1}\right\}\mathclose{}\).
  3. It completes all \(k\) steps exactly when \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k\) are linearly independent; then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_k\) is an orthonormal basis of \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k\right\}\mathclose{}\) (Definition 1).
ProofProof

Proof. Parts 1 and 2, by induction on \(i\). Before step \(1\), the list of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}\)’s is empty, which is orthonormal, and both spans are \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\) (Definition 5 in Subspaces and Rank). Suppose part 1 holds after step \(i - 1\), and write

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{S}_{i-1} &= \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_{i-1}\right\}\mathclose{} \\ &= \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_{i-1}\right\}\mathclose{}. \end{aligned} \]

The orthonormal vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_{i-1}\) are linearly independent (Theorem 2) and span \(\mathcal{S}_{i-1}\), so they are an orthonormal basis of it.

\(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i\) is orthogonal to the earlier \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}\)’s. For each \(l < i\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_l \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_l \cdot \mathopen{}\left(1\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i + \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \mathopen{}\left(-\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i\right)\mathclose{}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(}\href{#def-gram-schmidt}{\text{Definition~2}}\text{, as a linear combination)} \\ &= 1\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_l \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i) + \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \mathopen{}\left(-\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i\right)\mathclose{}\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_l \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j) && \text{(}\href{#thm-dot-linear-sum}{\text{Theorem~1}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_l \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i - \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i)\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_l \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j) && \text{(} 1\,z = z \text{, and pull the minus signs out of the sum)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_l \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_l \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i && \text{(only the } j = l \text{ term survives, }\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-orthonormal-vectors}{\text{Definition~28 in Vectors}}\text{)} \\ &= 0. && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

Part 2 at step \(i\). If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i = \sum_{j<i} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \in \mathcal{S}_{i-1}\). Conversely, if \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i \in \mathcal{S}_{i-1}\), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i = \sum_{j<i} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j\) (Theorem 3), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

Part 1 at step \(i\), if the process does not stop. Then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), so \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i\right\rVert\mathclose{} > 0\), and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i / \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i\right\rVert\mathclose{}\) has norm \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i\right\rVert\mathclose{} / \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i\right\rVert\mathclose{} = 1\) (Theorem 1 in Inner Products and the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality) and for \(l < i\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_l \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i &= \frac{1}{\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i\right\rVert\mathclose{}}\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_l \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-direct-sums.qmd#thm-dot-linear}{\text{Theorem~5 in Direct Sums and Orthogonal Complements}}\text{, special case } b = 0 \text{)} \\ &= 0, && \text{(} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i \text{ is orthogonal to } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_l \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_l = 0\) too (Theorem 1 in Vectors). So \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i\) are orthonormal. For the spans:

  • \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i\right\rVert\mathclose{}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i + \sum_{j<i} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j\) is in \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i\right\}\mathclose{}\), and so is each of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_{i-1}\), which lie in \(\mathcal{S}_{i-1} = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_{i-1}\right\}\mathclose{} \subseteq \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i\right\}\mathclose{}\).
  • \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i = \mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i - \sum_{j<i} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j\right)\mathclose{} / \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i\right\rVert\mathclose{}\) is in \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i\right\}\mathclose{}\), because each \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_j\) with \(j < i\) lies in \(\mathcal{S}_{i-1}\); and so is each of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_{i-1}\).

A span is a subspace (Theorem 2 in Subspaces and Rank), so it contains every linear combination of vectors in it; each span therefore contains the other, and they are equal.

Part 3. If the process completes, then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 \notin \operatorname{span}\) of the empty list, and for each \(i \ge 2\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i \notin \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_{i-1}\right\}\mathclose{}\) (part 2). Applying Theorem 8 in Subspaces and Rank \(k\) times, starting from the empty list, shows that \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k\) are linearly independent. Conversely, if they are linearly independent, no \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i\) is a linear combination \(\sum_{j<i} c_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j\) of the earlier ones, because then \(\sum_{j<i} c_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) would be a combination with a nonzero coefficient; so by part 2 the process never stops. When it completes, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_k\) are orthonormal, hence linearly independent (Theorem 2), and span \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k\right\}\mathclose{}\) (part 1), so they are an orthonormal basis of it.

ExmExample 8 (Finishing an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\))

Example 8 (Finishing an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)) Continue Example 6 with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_3 = (0, 1, 1)\). \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\) and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_3 &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\,(0 - 1 + 2) \\ &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}, \end{aligned} \]

so

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_3 &= (0, 1, 1) - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1, 1, 0) - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}} \cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\,(1, -1, 2) && \text{(orthogonalize)} \\ &= (0, 1, 1) - \tfrac{1}{2}\,(1, 1, 0) - \tfrac{1}{6}\,(1, -1, 2) && \text{(multiply the scalars)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(0 - \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{6},\; 1 - \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{6},\; 1 - 0 - \tfrac{2}{6}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(subtract entrywise)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(-\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3}\right)\mathclose{}, && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

with norm \(\sqrt{3 \cdot\tfrac{4}{9}} = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\,(-1, 1, 1)\). The process did not stop, so by Theorem 4 \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_3\) are linearly independent and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_3\) is an orthonormal basis of their span. That span therefore has dimension \(3\) (Definition 9 in Subspaces and Rank), so it is \(\mathbb{R}^3\) (Theorem 11 in Subspaces and Rank, Example 16 in Subspaces and Rank). As a check,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_3 &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\,(-1 + 1 + 0) \\ &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_3 &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{18}}\,(-1 - 1 + 2) \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

CorCorollary 1 (Every subspace has an orthonormal basis)

Corollary 1 (Every subspace has an orthonormal basis) Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^p\). Every orthonormal list of vectors in \(\mathcal{S}\) extends to an orthonormal basis of \(\mathcal{S}\) (Definition 1). Starting from the empty list shows that \(\mathcal{S}\) has an orthonormal basis.

ProofProof

Proof. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_r \in \mathcal{S}\) be orthonormal. They are linearly independent (Theorem 2), so they extend to a basis \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_r, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_{r+1}, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_d\) of \(\mathcal{S}\) (Theorem 9 in Subspaces and Rank). Run the Gram-Schmidt process on this basis. It is linearly independent, so the process completes, and the output is an orthonormal basis of its span, which is \(\mathcal{S}\) (Theorem 4, part 3).

The first \(r\) outputs are \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_r\) themselves, by induction on \(i \le r\). Suppose the outputs before step \(i\) are \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_{i-1}\) (for \(i = 1\) there are none, and the sum below is empty). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_i - \sum_{j<i} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_i)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_j \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_i, \end{aligned} \]

because each \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_j \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_i = 0\); \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_i = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_i \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), because \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_i\right\rVert\mathclose{} = 1\), so the process does not stop; and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_i / \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_i\right\rVert\mathclose{} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_i. \end{aligned} \]

So the orthonormal basis contains the starting list.

ExmExample 9 (Extending one unit vector to an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\))

Example 9 (Extending one unit vector to an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)) Start from the unit vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1, 1, 0)\). Extend it to a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) as in Example 18 in Subspaces and Rank, starting from the empty list: \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1\) is not in the span of the empty list, \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\), because \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1\right\rVert\mathclose{} = 1\); \((1, 0, 0)\) is not in \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1\right\}\mathclose{}\), because every multiple of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1\) has equal first and second entries, and \((0, 0, 1)\) is not in \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1, (1, 0, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\), because every combination of those two vectors has third entry \(0\). So \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1, (1, 0, 0), (0, 0, 1)\) are linearly independent (Theorem 8 in Subspaces and Rank), and three linearly independent vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) are a basis of it (they are a basis of their span, which has dimension \(3\) (Definition 9 in Subspaces and Rank) and so is \(\mathbb{R}^3\) (Theorem 11 in Subspaces and Rank, Example 16 in Subspaces and Rank)). Gram-Schmidt on \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1, (1, 0, 0), (0, 0, 1)\):

  1. \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1\).

  2. \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 \cdot (1, 0, 0) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\), so

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_2 &= (1, 0, 0) - \tfrac{1}{2}\,(1, 1, 0) \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}, 0\right)\mathclose{}, \end{aligned} \]

    with norm \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\), and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,(1, -1, 0)\).

  3. \((0, 0, 1)\) is orthogonal to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}'_3 = (0, 0, 1)\), which already has norm \(1\), and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_3 = (0, 0, 1)\).

The result is the orthonormal basis of Example 3, in a different order.

2 Outer product

DefDefinition 3 (Outer product)

Definition 3 (Outer product) The outer product [4] of a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) of length \(m\) and a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) of length \(n\) is the \(m \times n\) matrix product (Definition 1 in Matrix Multiplication) \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\) of the column vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) with the row vector \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\). Its entries are

\[ \mathopen{}\left(\underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}_{m \times 1}\,\underbrace{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}}_{1 \times n}\right)\mathclose{}_{ij} = u_i v_j \qquad \text{(definition of matrix multiplication; the sum has one term)} \]

ExmExample 10 (An outer product)

Example 10 (An outer product) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = (1, 2, 3)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = (4, 5)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of the outer product)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 4 & 1 \cdot 5 \\ 2 \cdot 4 & 2 \cdot 5 \\ 3 \cdot 4 & 3 \cdot 5 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(entry } (i, j) \text{ is } u_i v_j \text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 5 \\ 8 & 10 \\ 12 & 15 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply)} \end{aligned} \]

RemarkRemark 1 (Outer product and dot product)

Remark 1 (Outer product and dot product). The dot product \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) (Example 3 in Matrix Multiplication) needs two vectors of the same length and gives a number. The outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\) takes vectors of any two lengths and gives a matrix [5, Ch. 1], p. 11.

For example, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = (1, 2, 3)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = (4, 5)\) in Example 10 have different lengths, so they have no dot product, but their outer product is a \(3 \times 2\) matrix. For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} = (1, 2)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = (3, 4)\), which have the same length, both products exist:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} &= 1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 4 \\ &= 11 \\ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 3 & 1 \cdot 4 \\ 2 \cdot 3 & 2 \cdot 4 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 4 (Self outer product)

Definition 4 (Self outer product) The self outer product of a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) with \(n\) entries is the outer product of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) with itself, the \(n \times n\) matrix

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \]

ExmExample 11 (A self outer product)

Example 11 (A self outer product) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (1, 2)\), the self outer product is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of the self outer product)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 2 \\ 2 \cdot 1 & 2 \cdot 2 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply each entry of the column by each entry of the row)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply)} \end{aligned} \]

RemarkRemark 2 (Self outer products in statistics)

Remark 2 (Self outer products in statistics). Statistics meets the self outer product mostly through sums of them. The Gram matrix \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top} \mathbf{X}\) of a data matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) is the sum of the self outer products of its rows, as the Gram matrix example shows. For observation vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_n\), the sum \(\sum_{i=1}^n\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i - {\bar{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}\right)\mathclose{} {\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i - {\bar{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}\) of the self outer products of their deviations from the vector \({\bar{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}\) of entrywise means is what other sources call the scatter matrix [6]. Each term of either sum is one observation’s contribution to the total.

LemTheorem 5 (An outer product has rank one)

Theorem 5 (An outer product has rank one) If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) is a nonzero vector of length \(m\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) is a nonzero vector of length \(n\), then \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}) = 1\) (Definition 2 in Subspaces and Rank).

ProofProof

Proof. Column \(j\) of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\) has entries \(u_1 v_j, \ldots, u_m v_j\), so it is the vector \(v_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\).

The rank is at least one. Because \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \neq \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), some entry \(v_j\) is nonzero. Then column \(j\), \(v_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\), is a nonzero vector, and a single nonzero vector is linearly independent (Definition 1 in Subspaces and Rank): \(c\,(v_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) forces \(c = 0\).

The rank is at most one. Take any two columns \(j \neq k\). If

\[ \begin{aligned} v_j &= v_k \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

both columns are \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), and \(1 \cdot(v_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) + 1 \cdot(v_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). Otherwise, use the coefficients \(c_j = v_k\) and \(c_k = -v_j\), which are not both zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} v_k\,(v_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) - v_j\,(v_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}) &= (v_k v_j - v_j v_k)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} && \text{(collect the scalar multiples of } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \text{)} \\ &= 0 \cdot\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} && \text{(multiplication of numbers is commutative)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}} && \text{(a zero multiple of a vector is } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Either way, every pair of columns has a combination with coefficients that are not all zero and that equals \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). Now take any set of two or more columns. It contains a pair \(j \neq k\). Use that pair’s coefficients for columns \(j\) and \(k\), and the coefficient \(0\) for every other column in the set. This combination equals \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), and its coefficients are not all zero, so the set is not linearly independent (Definition 1 in Subspaces and Rank). The largest linearly independent set of columns therefore has one column.

ExmExample 12 (The rank of an outer product)

Example 12 (The rank of an outer product) In Example 10, the second column \((5, 10, 15)\) of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\) is \(\frac{5}{4}\) times the first column \((4, 8, 12)\), because the columns are \(v_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = 4\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) and \(v_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = 5\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\). So the two columns are not linearly independent, and the \(3 \times 2\) matrix has rank \(1\).

CorCorollary 2 (A self outer product has rank one)

Corollary 2 (A self outer product has rank one) For a nonzero vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), the self outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\) has rank \(1\):

\[ {\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}} = 1 \]

ProofProof

Proof. The self outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\) is the outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\). Both of its factors are the nonzero vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), so Theorem 5, with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) in place of both \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\), gives rank \(1\).

ExmExample 13 (The rank of a self outer product)

Example 13 (The rank of a self outer product) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= (1, 2)\), the example of a self outer product found the matrix \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\). Its second column \((2, 4)\) is \(2\) times its first column \((1, 2)\), so the two columns are not linearly independent, and the matrix has rank \(1\), as Corollary 2 says.

LemTheorem 6 (The transpose of an outer product)

Theorem 6 (The transpose of an outer product) For a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) with \(m\) entries and a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) with \(n\) entries, the transpose of the outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\) equals the outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}^{\top}\):

\[ \underbrace{{\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}}_{n \times m} = \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}^{\top}}_{n \times m} \]

ProofProof

Proof. Entry \((i, j)\) of each side, for \(i\) from \(1\) to \(n\) and \(j\) from \(1\) to \(m\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left[{\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} &= \mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}_{ji} && \text{(definition of the transpose)} \\ &= u_j v_i && \text{(entries of an outer product)} \\ &= v_i u_j && \text{(multiplication of numbers is commutative)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}_{ij} && \text{(entries of an outer product)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 14 (Transposing an outer product)

Example 14 (Transposing an outer product) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = (1, 2, 3)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = (4, 5)\), the example of an outer product found the \(3 \times 2\) matrix \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\). Swapping the two vectors gives its transpose:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}^{\top} &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of the outer product)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 \cdot 1 & 4 \cdot 2 & 4 \cdot 3 \\ 5 \cdot 1 & 5 \cdot 2 & 5 \cdot 3 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(entry } (i, j) \text{ is } v_i u_j \text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 8 & 12 \\ 5 & 10 & 15 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply)} \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 7 (An outer product times a vector)

Theorem 7 (An outer product times a vector) For a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) with \(m\) entries and two vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\) with \(n\) entries, the product of the outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\) with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\) equals the scalar multiple of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) by the dot product \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\):

\[ \underbrace{\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}}_{m \times 1} = \mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\right)\mathclose{} \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}_{m \times 1} \]

ProofProof

Proof. Entry \(i\) of each side, for \(i\) from \(1\) to \(m\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left[\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\right]\mathclose{}_{i} &= \sum_{j=1}^{n} \mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}_{ij} w_j && \text{(definition of matrix-vector multiplication)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{n} u_i v_j w_j && \text{(entries of an outer product)} \\ &= u_i \sum_{j=1}^{n} v_j w_j && \text{(factor } u_i \text{ out of the sum)} \\ &= u_i \mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(definition of the dot product)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\right)\mathclose{} u_i && \text{(multiplication of numbers is commutative)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[\mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\right)\mathclose{} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\right]\mathclose{}_{i} && \text{(definition of scalar multiplication)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 15 (Multiplying an outer product by a vector)

Example 15 (Multiplying an outer product by a vector) Take \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = (1, 2, 3)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = (4, 5)\) from the example of an outer product, and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = (1, -1)\). Multiplying the outer product by \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\) directly gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 5 \\ 8 & 10 \\ 12 & 15 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(the outer product found in that example)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 - 5 \\ 8 - 10 \\ 12 - 15 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of matrix-vector multiplication)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(subtract)} \end{aligned} \]

The dot product of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} &= 4 \cdot 1 + 5 \cdot(-1) && \text{(definition of the dot product)} \\ &= -1 && \text{(multiply and add)} \end{aligned} \]

and \((-1)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = (-1, -2, -3)\) is the same vector, as Theorem 7 says.

3 Kronecker product

DefDefinition 5 (Kronecker product)

Definition 5 (Kronecker product) The Kronecker product of an \(m \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) and a \(p \times q\) matrix \(\mathbf{B}\) is the \(mp \times nq\) block matrix whose block in row \(i\) and column \(j\) is \(a_{ij} \mathbf{B}\), where \(a_{ij}\) is the \((i, j)\) entry of \(\mathbf{A}\):

\[ \mathbf{A} \otimes \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} \mathbf{B} & \cdots & a_{1n} \mathbf{B} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{m1} \mathbf{B} & \cdots & a_{mn} \mathbf{B} \end{bmatrix}. \]

ExmExample 16 (A Kronecker product)

Example 16 (A Kronecker product) For \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A} \otimes \mathbf{B} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot\mathbf{B} & 2 \cdot\mathbf{B} \\ 3 \cdot\mathbf{B} & 4 \cdot\mathbf{B} \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of the Kronecker product)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply each entry of } \mathbf{A} \text{ by } \mathbf{B} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

The result is \(2 \times 4\): \(\mathbf{A}\) is \(2 \times 2\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) is \(1 \times 2\), so \(\mathbf{A} \otimes \mathbf{B}\) is \((2 \cdot 1) \times (2 \cdot 2)\).

LemTheorem 8 (An outer product is a Kronecker product)

Theorem 8 (An outer product is a Kronecker product) For a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) with \(m\) entries and a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) with \(n\) entries, the Kronecker product of the column vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) with the transpose \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\) of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) equals the outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\):

\[ \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}_{m \times 1} \otimes \underbrace{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}}_{1 \times n} = \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}}_{m \times n} \]

ProofProof

Proof. The Kronecker product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \otimes {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\) stacks \(u_1 {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}, \ldots, u_m {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\), so its \(i\)th row is \(u_i {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\). So entry \((i, j)\) of each side, for \(i\) from \(1\) to \(m\) and \(j\) from \(1\) to \(n\), is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \otimes {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}_{ij} &= \mathopen{}\left(u_i {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}_{j} && \text{(definition of the Kronecker product)} \\ &= u_i v_j && \text{(entry } j \text{ of the scalar multiple } u_i {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} \text{)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}_{ij} && \text{(entries of an outer product)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 17 (An outer product as a Kronecker product)

Example 17 (An outer product as a Kronecker product) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = (1, 2, 3)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = (4, 5)\), the Kronecker product of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) with \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \otimes {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \\ 2 \cdot\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \\ 3 \cdot\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of the Kronecker product)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 5 \\ 8 & 10 \\ 12 & 15 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(multiply)} \end{aligned} \]

This matrix is the outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\) that the example of an outer product found, as Theorem 8 says.

DefDefinition 6 (Vectorization)

Definition 6 (Vectorization) The vectorization [7] of an \(m \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) is the column vector with \(mn\) entries that stacks the columns \(\mathbf{A}_{:,1}, \ldots, \mathbf{A}_{:,n}\) of \(\mathbf{A}\) in order:

\[ {\operatorname{vec}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A}_{:,1} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{A}_{:,n} \end{bmatrix} \]

ExmExample 18 (Vectorizing a \(2 \times 2\) matrix)

Example 18 (Vectorizing a \(2 \times 2\) matrix) For \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\), the columns are \((1, 3)\) and \((2, 4)\), so the vectorization stacks them:

\[ {\operatorname{vec}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \]

LemTheorem 9 (The vectorization of an outer product is a Kronecker product)

Theorem 9 (The vectorization of an outer product is a Kronecker product) For a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) with \(m\) entries and a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}\) with \(n\) entries, the vectorization of the outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\) equals the Kronecker product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \otimes \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\):

\[ \underbrace{{\operatorname{vec}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}}}_{mn \times 1} = \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}_{n \times 1} \otimes \underbrace{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}_{m \times 1} \]

ProofProof

Proof. The \(j\)th column of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\) has entries \(u_1 v_j, \ldots, u_m v_j\), so it is the vector \(v_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{vec}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}} &= \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \\ \vdots \\ v_n \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \end{bmatrix} && \text{(definition of vectorization)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \otimes \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} && \text{(definition of the Kronecker product, with one column of blocks)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 19 (Vectorizing an outer product)

Example 19 (Vectorizing an outer product) For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = (1, 2, 3)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = (4, 5)\), the example of an outer product found the outer product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\), whose columns are \((4, 8, 12)\) and \((5, 10, 15)\). Its vectorization and the Kronecker product \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \otimes \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\) agree:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{vec}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}} &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 8 \\ 12 \\ 5 \\ 10 \\ 15 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(stack the columns)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 \cdot\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \\ 5 \cdot\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \end{bmatrix} && \text{(each column is a multiple of } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \text{)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} \otimes \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} && \text{(definition of the Kronecker product)} \end{aligned} \]

as Theorem 9 says.

4 Hilbert spaces

DefDefinition 7 (Cauchy sequence)

Definition 7 (Cauchy sequence) A sequence \((\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_n)\) of vectors in a real inner product space \(V\) is a Cauchy sequence [8] if for every \(\varepsilon> 0\) there is a natural number \(N\) such that

\[\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_m - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_n\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \varepsilon\quad \text{for all } m, n \ge N,\]

where \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \sqrt{{\mathopen{}\left\langle \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rangle\mathclose{}}}\) for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in V\).

ExmExample 20 (A Cauchy sequence of real numbers)

Example 20 (A Cauchy sequence of real numbers) The real numbers \(\mathbb{R}\) with the inner product \({\mathopen{}\left\langle x, y\right\rangle\mathclose{}} = x\,y\) form an inner product space, and \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert x\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \mathopen{}\left|x\right|\mathclose{}\) in it. The sequence \(x_n = \frac{1}{n}\) is a Cauchy sequence. Given \(\varepsilon> 0\), take \(N\) to be any natural number larger than \(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\). For all \(m, n \ge N\), both \(\frac{1}{m}\) and \(\frac{1}{n}\) lie in \((0, \frac{1}{N}]\), so \(\mathopen{}\left|\frac{1}{m} - \frac{1}{n}\right|\mathclose{} < \frac{1}{N} < \varepsilon\). For example, with \(\varepsilon= 0.01\), take \(N = 101\): all \(m, n \ge 101\) have \(\mathopen{}\left|\frac{1}{m} - \frac{1}{n}\right|\mathclose{} < \frac{1}{101} < 0.01\).

DefDefinition 8 (Complete inner product space)

Definition 8 (Complete inner product space) A real inner product space \(V\) is complete [9] if for every Cauchy sequence \((\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_n)\) in \(V\) there is a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in V\) such that for every \(\varepsilon> 0\) there is a natural number \(N\) with

\[\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_n - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \varepsilon\quad \text{for every } n \ge N.\]

ExmExample 21 (Polynomials are not complete)

Example 21 (Polynomials are not complete) Let \(V\) be the set of polynomials on \([0, 1]\), with the inner product \({\mathopen{}\left\langle f, g\right\rangle\mathclose{}} = \int_0^1 f(t)\,g(t)\,dt\). The partial sums \(p_n(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{t^k}{k!}\) are a Cauchy sequence in \(V\), because they converge uniformly on \([0, 1]\) to \(\text{e}^{t}\). The only candidate for a limit is \(\text{e}^{t}\), which is not a polynomial, so no polynomial \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in V\) satisfies the condition of Definition 8, and \(V\) is not complete.

DefDefinition 9 (Hilbert space)

Definition 9 (Hilbert space) A Hilbert space [10] is a complete real inner product space [5, Sec. 16.8], Definition 16.8, p. 547.

ExmExample 22 (Real coordinate space is a Hilbert space)

Example 22 (Real coordinate space is a Hilbert space) The Euclidean space \(\mathbb{R}^2\) with the dot product (Example 2 in Inner Products and the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality) is a Hilbert space. Let \((\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_n)\) with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_n = (x_{n,1}, x_{n,2})\) be a Cauchy sequence in it. Each coordinate sequence is a Cauchy sequence of real numbers, because \(\mathopen{}\left|x_{m,j} - x_{n,j}\right|\mathclose{} \le \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_m - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_n\right\rVert\mathclose{}\) for \(j = 1, 2\). Each coordinate sequence then converges to a real number \(y_j\), because every Cauchy sequence of real numbers converges [9], and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= (y_1, y_2)\) satisfies the condition of Definition 8. For example, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_n = (\frac{1}{n}, 1 + \frac{1}{n})\) is a Cauchy sequence with limit \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= (0, 1)\).
