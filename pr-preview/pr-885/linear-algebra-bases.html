1 Bases and dimension

DefDefinition 1 (Basis)

Definition 1 (Basis) A basis [1] of a subspace \(\mathcal{S}\) (Definition 4 in Subspaces and Rank) is a list of vectors that are linearly independent (Definition 1 in Subspaces and Rank) and span \(\mathcal{S}\) (Definition 5 in Subspaces and Rank).

ExmExample 1 (Two bases of \(\mathbb{R}^2\))

Example 1 (Two bases of \(\mathbb{R}^2\))  

  • \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\) form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\): they are linearly independent, and any \((x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2\) equals \(x\,(1, 0) + y\,(0, 1)\).
  • \((1, 0)\) and \((1, 1)\) also form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\): Example 1 in Subspaces and Rank shows they are linearly independent, and any \((x, y)\) equals \((x - y)\,(1, 0) + y\,(1, 1)\), because \((x - y) + y = x\) in the first entry and \(0 + y = y\) in the second.
ExmExample 2 (Lists that are not bases of \(\mathbb{R}^2\))

Example 2 (Lists that are not bases of \(\mathbb{R}^2\))  

  • \((1, 0)\) and \((2, 0)\) are not a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\). They are not linearly independent, since \(2\,(1, 0) - (2, 0) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), and they do not span \(\mathbb{R}^2\): every combination \(c_1 (1, 0) + c_2 (2, 0)\) has second entry \(0\), so \((0, 1)\) is not in their span.
  • \((1, 0)\), \((0, 1)\) and \((1, 1)\) span \(\mathbb{R}^2\), but they are not a basis: \((1, 0) + (0, 1) - (1, 1) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), so they are not linearly independent.
ExrExercise 1 (Testing whether \((1, 1)\) and \((1, -1)\) form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\))

Exercise 1 (Testing whether \((1, 1)\) and \((1, -1)\) form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)) Describing every vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) through \((1, 1)\) and \((1, -1)\) instead of the axes works only if those two vectors form a basis. Let

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2\) a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)?

TipHint

Check both conditions of Definition 1. For spanning, look for \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) with \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + c_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 = {(x, y)}^{\top}\).

SolutionSolution 1

Solution 1. Yes. They span \(\mathbb{R}^2\): for every \({(x, y)}^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^2\),

\[ \frac{x + y}{2} \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} + \frac{x - y}{2} \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}. \]

They are linearly independent: if \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + c_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), then \(c_1 + c_2 = 0\) and \(c_1 - c_2 = 0\), and adding the two equations gives \(c_1 = 0\) and then \(c_2 = 0\). By Definition 1, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\).

LemTheorem 1 (Coefficients in a basis are unique)

Theorem 1 (Coefficients in a basis are unique) If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k\) is a basis of a subspace \(\mathcal{S}\) (Definition 1), then every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathcal{S}\) is a linear combination \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + \cdots + c_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k\) for exactly one list of numbers \(c_1, \ldots, c_k\).

ProofProof

Proof. At least one such list exists, because the basis spans \(\mathcal{S}\) (Definition 5 in Subspaces and Rank).

Suppose two lists both work:

\[ \begin{aligned} c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + \cdots + c_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \\ &= d_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + \cdots + d_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k. \end{aligned} \]

Subtracting the right side from the left side gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}} &= (c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + \cdots + c_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k) - (d_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + \cdots + d_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k) && \text{(both combinations equal } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \text{)} \\ &= (c_1 - d_1)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + \cdots + (c_k - d_k)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k && \text{(regroup, and distribute each } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k\) are linearly independent (Definition 1 in Subspaces and Rank), every coefficient \(c_i - d_i\) is \(0\), so \(c_i = d_i\) for each \(i\).

ExmExample 3 (Coefficients of \((3, 5)\) in two bases)

Example 3 (Coefficients of \((3, 5)\) in two bases) In the basis \((1, 0), (0, 1)\) of Example 1, \((3, 5) = 3\,(1, 0) + 5\,(0, 1)\), and no other coefficients work. In the basis \((1, 0), (1, 1)\), the formula in Example 1 gives

\[ \begin{aligned} (3, 5) &= (3 - 5)\,(1, 0) + 5\,(1, 1) \\ &= -2\,(1, 0) + 5\,(1, 1). \end{aligned} \]

The same vector has different coefficients in different bases, but within one basis its coefficients are unique.

The list \((1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 1)\) in Example 2 spans \(\mathbb{R}^2\) but is not linearly independent, and the coefficients of \((3, 5)\) are not unique:

\[ \begin{aligned} (3, 5) &= 3\,(1, 0) + 5\,(0, 1) + 0\,(1, 1) \\ &= 0\,(1, 0) + 2\,(0, 1) + 3\,(1, 1). \end{aligned} \]

ExrExercise 2 (Counting the ways to write a vector in a basis)

Exercise 2 (Counting the ways to write a vector in a basis) The vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 = {(1, 1)}^{\top}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 = {(1, -1)}^{\top}\) form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\), and

\[ \begin{bmatrix}5 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = 3 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + 2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2. \]

How many lists of numbers \(c_1, c_2\) satisfy \({(5, 1)}^{\top} = c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + c_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2\)?

TipHint

Use Theorem 1.

SolutionSolution 2

Solution 2. Exactly one, namely \(c_1 = 3\) and \(c_2 = 2\). By Theorem 1, a vector in the span of a basis has only one list of numbers, and \(3, 2\) is a list that works.

DefDefinition 2 (Coordinates in a basis)

Definition 2 (Coordinates in a basis) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k\) be a basis of a subspace \(\mathcal{S}\) (Definition 1), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathcal{S}\). The coordinates [2] of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) in this basis are the numbers \(c_1, \ldots, c_k\) with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + \cdots + c_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k\). By Theorem 1 there is exactly one such list of numbers.

DefDefinition 3 (Coordinate vector)

Definition 3 (Coordinate vector) With \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and its coordinates \(c_1, \ldots, c_k\) as in Definition 2, \((c_1, \ldots, c_k) \in \mathbb{R}^k\) is the coordinate vector of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).

ExmExample 4 (Coordinates of \((4, 1)\) in two bases of \(\mathbb{R}^2\))

Example 4 (Coordinates of \((4, 1)\) in two bases of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)) In the basis \((1, 0), (1, 1)\) of Example 1, the coordinates of \((4, 1)\) are \(3\) and \(1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} 3\,(1, 0) + 1\,(1, 1) &= (3 + 1,\; 0 + 1) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-linear-combination}{\text{Definition~11 in Vectors}}\text{, entry by entry)} \\ &= (4, 1). && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]

In the basis \((1, 0), (0, 1)\), its coordinates are its entries, \(4\) and \(1\).

ExrExercise 3 (Finding coordinates in a basis other than the indicator vectors)

Exercise 3 (Finding coordinates in a basis other than the indicator vectors) The coordinates of a vector depend on the basis, and they match its entries only in the basis of indicator vectors. The vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 = {(2, 0)}^{\top}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 = {(1, 1)}^{\top}\) form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Let

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \begin{bmatrix}7 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}. \]

Find the coordinates of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) in the basis \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2\).

TipHint

Write \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + c_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2\) entry by entry, and start from the second entry.

SolutionSolution 3

Solution 3. The coordinates of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) are \(2\) and \(3\). Entry by entry, \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + c_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 = {(2 c_1 + c_2,\ c_2)}^{\top}\). The second entry gives \(c_2 = 3\), and then the first gives \(2 c_1 + 3 = 7\), so \(c_1 = 2\). As a check, \(2 {(2, 0)}^{\top} + 3 {(1, 1)}^{\top} = {(7, 3)}^{\top}\).

ExrExercise 4 (Describing a point on a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) with two numbers)

Exercise 4 (Describing a point on a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) with two numbers) A point on a plane through the origin in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) needs only two numbers, its coordinate vector in a basis of the plane. The vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 = {(1, 1, 0)}^{\top}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 = {(0, 1, 1)}^{\top}\) form a basis of a plane \(\mathcal{S}\), which contains

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 5 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}. \]

Find the coordinate vector of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) in the basis \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2\).

TipHint

Write \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + c_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2\) entry by entry, then use Definition 3.

SolutionSolution 4

Solution 4. Entry by entry, \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + c_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_2 = {(c_1,\ c_1 + c_2,\ c_2)}^{\top}\). Matching \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) gives \(c_1 = 2\) and \(c_2 = 3\), and the second entry checks: \(2 + 3 = 5\). By Definition 3, the coordinate vector of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is \({(2, 3)}^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^2\).

LemTheorem 2 (All bases of a subspace have the same number of vectors)

Theorem 2 (All bases of a subspace have the same number of vectors) If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}_l\) are both bases of a subspace \(\mathcal{S}\) of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (Definition 1), then \(k = l\).

ProofProof

Proof. Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be the \(p \times k\) matrix with columns \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k\), and \(\mathbf{B}\) the \(p \times l\) matrix with columns \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}_l\).

Write \(\mathbf{A}\) in terms of \(\mathbf{B}\). Each \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j\) is in \(\mathcal{S}\), and the \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\)’s span \(\mathcal{S}\) (Definition 5 in Subspaces and Rank), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j = \mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_j\) for some \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_j \in \mathbb{R}^l\) (Definition 8 in Matrix Multiplication). Let \(\mathbf{C}\) be the \(l \times k\) matrix with columns \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_k\). Then \(\underbrace{\mathbf{A}}_{p \times k} = \underbrace{\mathbf{B}}_{p \times l}\,\underbrace{\mathbf{C}}_{l \times k}\), because column \(j\) of \(\mathbf{B} \mathbf{C}\) is \(\mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_j\).

The columns of \(\mathbf{C}\) are linearly independent. Suppose \(\mathbf{C} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) for some \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^k\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= (\mathbf{B} \mathbf{C})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(substitute } \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{B} \mathbf{C} \text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{B}\,(\mathbf{C} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{B}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}} && \text{(substitute } \mathbf{C} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}} && \text{(a matrix times the zero vector is } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

\(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the combination \(x_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + \cdots + x_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_k\) (Theorem 3 in Subspaces and Rank), and the \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}\)’s are linearly independent (Definition 1 in Subspaces and Rank), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). So the only combination of the columns of \(\mathbf{C}\) that equals \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) has all coefficients \(0\).

Count. \(\mathbf{C}\) has \(k\) linearly independent columns of length \(l\). More than \(l\) vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^l\) are never linearly independent (Theorem 5 in Subspaces and Rank), so \(k \le l\). Exchanging the roles of the two bases gives \(l \le k\), so \(k = l\).

DefDefinition 4 (Dimension)

Definition 4 (Dimension) The dimension of a subspace \(\mathcal{S}\), written \(\dim(\mathcal{S})\), is the number of vectors in any basis of \(\mathcal{S}\) (Definition 1). Theorem 2 makes this number well defined. By convention, the empty list is the basis of \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\), so \(\dim(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}) = 0\).

Example 6 (The dimensions of some subspaces)

  • \(\dim(\mathbb{R}^2) = 2\), by Example 1. More generally, \(\dim(\mathbb{R}^p) = p\): the indicator vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_p\) (Definition 21 in Vectors) are linearly independent, since \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1 + \cdots + c_p \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_p = (c_1, \ldots, c_p)\), which is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p\) only if every \(c_i = 0\); and they span \(\mathbb{R}^p\), since any \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = x_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1 + \cdots + x_p \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_p\).

LemTheorem 3 (Adding a vector outside the span keeps a list independent)

Theorem 3 (Adding a vector outside the span keeps a list independent) If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\) are linearly independent (Definition 1 in Subspaces and Rank) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\) is not in \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\right\}\mathclose{}\) (Definition 5 in Subspaces and Rank), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\) are linearly independent.

ProofProof

Proof. Suppose \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + c_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k + d\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

The coefficient \(d\) is \(0\). If \(d \neq 0\), then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} &= \frac{1}{d}\,\mathopen{}\left(d\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(} d \neq 0 \text{)} \\ &= \frac{1}{d}\,\mathopen{}\left(-c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 - \cdots - c_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(solve the supposed equation for } d\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \text{)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(-\frac{c_1}{d}\right)\mathclose{}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + \mathopen{}\left(-\frac{c_k}{d}\right)\mathclose{}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k, && \text{(distribute } \tfrac{1}{d} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

which puts \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\) in \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\right\}\mathclose{}\), contrary to the assumption.

The other coefficients are \(0\). With \(d = 0\), the equation reads \(c_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1 + \cdots + c_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), so every \(c_i = 0\), because \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k\) are linearly independent.

Example 7 (Adding \((0, 0, 1)\) to two independent vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)) The vectors \((1, 0, 0)\) and \((0, 1, 0)\) are linearly independent, and their span is the plane \(z = 0\) (Example 6 in Subspaces and Rank). The vector \((0, 0, 1)\) has third entry \(1\), so it is not in that plane, and by Theorem 3 the three vectors \((1, 0, 0)\), \((0, 1, 0)\), \((0, 0, 1)\) are linearly independent. Adding \((1, 1, 0)\) instead would not work: \((1, 1, 0)\) is in the span, and \((1, 0, 0) + (0, 1, 0) - (1, 1, 0) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

LemTheorem 4 (An independent list extends to a basis)

Theorem 4 (An independent list extends to a basis) If \(\mathcal{S}\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (Definition 4 in Subspaces and Rank) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}_k \in \mathcal{S}\) are linearly independent (Definition 1 in Subspaces and Rank), then adding vectors of \(\mathcal{S}\) to the list gives a basis of \(\mathcal{S}\) (Definition 1). Starting from the empty list shows that every subspace has a basis.

ProofProof

Proof. Repeat the following step. If the current list spans \(\mathcal{S}\), stop: it is linearly independent and spans \(\mathcal{S}\), so it is a basis. Otherwise, some \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \in \mathcal{S}\) is not in the span of the current list; add \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\) to the list, which stays linearly independent by Theorem 3.

Every list produced is a linearly independent list of vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^p\), so it has at most \(p\) vectors (Theorem 5 in Subspaces and Rank). Each step adds one vector, so the process stops after at most \(p - k\) steps, and when it stops the list is a basis of \(\mathcal{S}\).

For the empty list, which is linearly independent and spans \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\) by the conventions in Definition 5 in Subspaces and Rank, the first step asks whether \(\mathcal{S} = \mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\): if so, the empty list is already a basis; if not, the process adds vectors as described.

ExmExample 8 (Extending \((1, 1, 0)\) to a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\))

Example 8 (Extending \((1, 1, 0)\) to a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)) Start with the single vector \((1, 1, 0)\).

  1. \((1, 0, 0)\) is not in \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 1, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\), because every multiple \(c\,(1, 1, 0)\) has equal first and second entries. Add it.
  2. \((0, 0, 1)\) is not in \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 1, 0), (1, 0, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\), because every combination of those two vectors has third entry \(0\). Add it.
  3. The list \((1, 1, 0), (1, 0, 0), (0, 0, 1)\) spans \(\mathbb{R}^3\): \((x, y, z) = y\,(1, 1, 0) + (x - y)\,(1, 0, 0) + z\,(0, 0, 1)\). Stop.

The result is a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) that contains the starting vector.

LemTheorem 5 (An independent list in a subspace has at most \(\dim\) vectors)

Theorem 5 (An independent list in a subspace has at most \(\dim\) vectors) Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) with \(\dim(\mathcal{S}) = d\) (Definition 4).

  1. Any linearly independent list of vectors in \(\mathcal{S}\) has at most \(d\) vectors.
  2. If \(\mathcal{T}\) is a subspace with \(\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{S}\), then \(\dim(\mathcal{T}) \le \dim(\mathcal{S})\).
ProofProof

Proof. Part 1. If \(d = 0\), then \(\mathcal{S} = \mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\), so every vector in a nonempty list from \(\mathcal{S}\) is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), and \(1 \cdot\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) shows the list is not linearly independent; only the empty list, with \(0 = d\) vectors, is. Now let \(d \ge 1\), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_d\) be a basis of \(\mathcal{S}\), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_k \in \mathcal{S}\) be linearly independent. Each \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_j\) is in \(\mathcal{S}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_j = c_{1j} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_1 + \cdots + c_{dj} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_d\) for some coordinate vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_j = (c_{1j}, \ldots, c_{dj}) \in \mathbb{R}^d\). Suppose \(k > d\). Then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_k\) are \(k > d\) vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), so some \(x_1, \ldots, x_k\), not all zero, satisfy \(x_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_1 + \cdots + x_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_k = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_d\) (Theorem 5 in Subspaces and Rank); entry \(i\) of that equation says \(\sum_{j=1}^{k} x_j c_{ij} = 0\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{j=1}^{k} x_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_j &= \sum_{j=1}^{k} x_j \sum_{i=1}^{d} c_{ij} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i && \text{(substitute each } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_j \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{i=1}^{d} x_j c_{ij}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i && \text{(distribute each } x_j \text{ over the inner sum)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{d} \sum_{j=1}^{k} x_j c_{ij}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i && \text{(swap the order of the finite sums)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{d} \mathopen{}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k} x_j c_{ij}\right)\mathclose{}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i && \text{(factor } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i \text{ out of the inner sum)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{d} 0 \cdot\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_i && \text{(entry } i \text{ of } \textstyle\sum_j x_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_j = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_d \text{)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p, && \text{(a zero multiple of a vector is } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

a combination of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}_k\) with coefficients not all zero that equals \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p\). That contradicts their linear independence, so \(k \le d\).

Part 2. \(\mathcal{T}\) has a basis (Theorem 4) with \(\dim(\mathcal{T})\) vectors. Those vectors are linearly independent and lie in \(\mathcal{S}\), so by Part 1 there are at most \(\dim(\mathcal{S})\) of them.

ExmExample 9 (Subspaces of a plane have dimension at most two)

Example 9 (Subspaces of a plane have dimension at most two) The plane \(\mathcal{S} = \mathopen{}\left\{(x, y, 0) : x, y \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) has dimension \(2\) (Example 6). By Theorem 5, no three vectors with third entry \(0\) are linearly independent; for instance, \((1, 0, 0) + (0, 1, 0) - (1, 1, 0) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\). The line \(\mathcal{T} = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 1, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\) lies in \(\mathcal{S}\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \dim(\mathcal{T}) &= 1 \\ &\le 2 \\ &= \dim(\mathcal{S}). \end{aligned} \]

ExrExercise 9 (Testing whether four independent vectors fit in a three-dimensional subspace)

Exercise 9 (Testing whether four independent vectors fit in a three-dimensional subspace) Theorem 5 caps how many linearly independent vectors a subspace can hold. Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^5\) with dimension \(3\).

Can \(\mathcal{S}\) contain \(4\) linearly independent vectors?

TipHint

Use part 1 of Theorem 5 with \(d = 3\).

SolutionSolution 9

Solution 9. No. By part 1 of Theorem 5, a linearly independent list of vectors in \(\mathcal{S}\) has at most \(\dim(\mathcal{S}) = 3\) vectors, so it cannot have \(4\).

LemTheorem 6 (A subspace inside another of the same dimension equals it)

Theorem 6 (A subspace inside another of the same dimension equals it) If \(\mathcal{T}\) and \(\mathcal{S}\) are subspaces of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) with \(\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{S}\) and \(\dim(\mathcal{T}) = \dim(\mathcal{S})\) (Definition 4), then \(\mathcal{T} = \mathcal{S}\).

ProofProof

Proof. Let \(d = \dim(\mathcal{S})\), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{t}}\vphantom{t}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{t}}\vphantom{t}}_d\) be a basis of \(\mathcal{T}\) (Theorem 4); it has \(d\) vectors because \(\dim(\mathcal{T}) = d\). (When \(d = 0\) it is the empty list, whose span is \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\) by the convention in Definition 5 in Subspaces and Rank, and the argument is unchanged.) Suppose some \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{s}}\vphantom{s}} \in \mathcal{S}\) is not in \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{t}}\vphantom{t}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{t}}\vphantom{t}}_d\right\}\mathclose{} = \mathcal{T}\). Then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{t}}\vphantom{t}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{t}}\vphantom{t}}_d, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{s}}\vphantom{s}}\) are linearly independent (Theorem 3), and they are \(d + 1\) vectors of \(\mathcal{S}\), which contradicts part 1 of Theorem 5. So every vector of \(\mathcal{S}\) is in \(\mathcal{T}\), that is, \(\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathcal{T}\). Together with \(\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{S}\), this inclusion gives \(\mathcal{T} = \mathcal{S}\).

ExmExample 10 (Two independent vectors in a plane span it)

Example 10 (Two independent vectors in a plane span it) Let \(\mathcal{S} = \mathopen{}\left\{(x, y, 0) : x, y \in \mathbb{R}\right\}\mathclose{}\), the plane \(z = 0\), which has dimension \(2\) (Example 6), and let \(\mathcal{T} = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 0, 0), (1, 1, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\). Both vectors have third entry \(0\), so \(\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{S}\). They are linearly independent: \(c_1 (1, 0, 0) + c_2 (1, 1, 0) = (c_1 + c_2, c_2, 0)\), which is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) only if \(c_2 = 0\) and then \(c_1 = 0\). So \(\dim(\mathcal{T}) = 2\), and Theorem 6 gives \(\mathcal{T} = \mathcal{S}\) without solving for any coefficients. As a check, \((x, y, 0) = (x - y)\,(1, 0, 0) + y\,(1, 1, 0)\).

ExmExample 11 (Equal dimensions without containment)

Example 11 (Equal dimensions without containment) The lines \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 0, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\) and \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(0, 1, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) both have dimension \(1\), but they are not equal: \((1, 0, 0)\) is in the first and not the second. Theorem 6 does not apply, because neither line contains the other.

ExrExercise 10 (Showing that a span of two vectors is all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\))

Exercise 10 (Showing that a span of two vectors is all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)) Theorem 6 shows that two subspaces coincide by comparing dimensions instead of checking every vector. Let

\[ \mathcal{T} = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{{(1, 2)}^{\top},\ {(3, 1)}^{\top}\right\}\mathclose{}. \]

Is \(\mathcal{T}\) all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)?

TipHint

Find \(\dim(\mathcal{T})\) and \(\dim(\mathbb{R}^2)\), then use Theorem 6 with \(\mathcal{S} = \mathbb{R}^2\).

SolutionSolution 10

Solution 10. Yes. The two vectors are linearly independent: if \(c_1 {(1, 2)}^{\top} + c_2 {(3, 1)}^{\top} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), then \(c_1 + 3 c_2 = 0\) and \(2 c_1 + c_2 = 0\). Subtracting twice the first equation from the second gives \(-5 c_2 = 0\), so \(c_2 = 0\) and then \(c_1 = 0\). So they form a basis of \(\mathcal{T}\), and \(\dim(\mathcal{T}) = 2\). The indicator vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_2\) form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\), so \(\dim(\mathbb{R}^2) = 2\). Since \(\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2\) and the dimensions agree, Theorem 6 gives \(\mathcal{T} = \mathbb{R}^2\).
