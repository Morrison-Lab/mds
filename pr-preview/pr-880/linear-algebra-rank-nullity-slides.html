This section is adapted from Zhou [1], used under the MIT License (see the license text in Section  in Subspaces and Rank). Theorem 5 and Corollary 2 restore the source’s statements that a product has rank at most the rank of either factor, and that multiplying by a matrix of full column rank or by a matrix whose rank is its number of rows does not change the rank. These parts of the source are not part of this section:

  • the names “full row rank”, “full rank” and “singular”
  • the statement that \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{C}) \subseteq \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) exactly when \(\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\) for some \(\mathbf{B}\), and its row space version, and the containment \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{B}) \subseteq \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\)
  • the dimension of a subspace as the largest number of linearly independent vectors in it, or the smallest number of vectors that span it
  • the properties of a rank factorization \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{R}\) (\(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{C}) = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{R}) = r\), \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{C})\), \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{R})\)), the fact that it is not unique, and the second proof of \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\) that it gives
  • calling \(\mathbf{A}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\) a Gram matrix, and the equation \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) = \mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\) (Theorem 6 in Column Space and Null Space applied to \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\) gives it)
  • the statement that \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) and \(\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\) share only the zero vector (it follows from Theorem 6 in Orthogonal Complements and the Fundamental Theorem)

Theorem 1 (The rank is the dimension of the column space) For any \(m \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\),

\[ \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = \dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\right)\mathclose{}. \]

Proof. Let \(r = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\) (Definition 2 in Subspaces and Rank), and choose \(r\) linearly independent columns of \(\mathbf{A}\); by Definition 2 in Subspaces and Rank, no larger set of columns is linearly independent. We show the chosen columns are a basis of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) (Definition 1 in Bases and Dimension).

Every column is in the span of the chosen columns. A chosen column is in that span trivially. If some unchosen column \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j\) were not in the span, then adding it to the chosen columns would give \(r + 1\) linearly independent columns (Theorem 3 in Bases and Dimension), more than \(r\). So every column of \(\mathbf{A}\) is in the span of the chosen columns.

The chosen columns span \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\). The span of the chosen columns is a subspace (Theorem 2 in Subspaces and Rank), so it is closed under addition and scalar multiplication, and it contains every linear combination of the columns of \(\mathbf{A}\). By Theorem 1 in Column Space and Null Space, those combinations make up \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\), so \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) is contained in the span of the chosen columns. The chosen columns are themselves columns of \(\mathbf{A}\), so their span is contained in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\), and the two sets are equal.

The chosen columns are linearly independent and span \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\), so they are a basis of it, and \(\dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\right)\mathclose{} = r\) (Definition 4 in Bases and Dimension). If \(r = 0\), every column is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_m\), \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_m\right\}\mathclose{}\), and both sides are \(0\).

Example 1 (The rank of the matrix in Example 1 in Column Space and Null Space) For \(\mathbf{A}\) in Example 1 in Column Space and Null Space, \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 3)\right\}\mathclose{}\) (Example 2 in Column Space and Null Space), and \((1, 3)\) is linearly independent because it is nonzero, so it is a basis and \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) has dimension \(1\). So \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = 1\), by Theorem 1. Directly: the first column \((1, 3)\) is linearly independent on its own, and any two columns are dependent, because each column is a multiple of \((1, 3)\).

Exercise 1 (Finding a rank from the column space) Theorem 1 reads the rank of a matrix off the dimension of its column space. Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 6\end{bmatrix}. \]

Find \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}}\) by finding \(\dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\right)\mathclose{}\).

Hint

In \(\mathbf{A}\), the second column is a multiple of the first.

Solution 1. In \(\mathbf{A}\), the second column is \((2, 4, 6) = 2\,(1, 2, 3)\), so by Theorem 1 in Column Space and Null Space

\[ \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 2, 3), (2, 4, 6)\right\}\mathclose{} = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 2, 3)\right\}\mathclose{}. \]

The single nonzero vector \((1, 2, 3)\) is a basis of that span, so \(\dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\right)\mathclose{} = 1\), and by Theorem 1, \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}} = 1\).

Definition 1 (Nullity) The nullity [2] of a matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) is the dimension of its null space (Definition 3 in Column Space and Null Space):

\[ \operatorname{nullity}(\mathbf{A}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\right)\mathclose{}. \]

Example 2 (The nullity of the matrix in Example 1 in Column Space and Null Space) For \(\mathbf{A}\) in Example 1 in Column Space and Null Space, \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(2, 1, 0), (2, 0, 1)\right\}\mathclose{}\) (Example 3 in Column Space and Null Space). Those two vectors are linearly independent: \(c_1 (2, 1, 0) + c_2 (2, 0, 1) = (2c_1 + 2c_2, c_1, c_2)\), which is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_3\) only if

\[ \begin{aligned} c_1 &= c_2 \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

So they are a basis of \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\), and \(\operatorname{nullity}(\mathbf{A}) = 2\). The \(2 \times 2\) identity matrix has nullity \(0\), because \(\mathbf{I} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_2\) only when \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_2\).

Theorem 2 (Rank-nullity theorem) For any \(m \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\),

\[ \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) + \operatorname{nullity}(\mathbf{A}) = n. \]

Proof. Let \(\nu= \operatorname{nullity}(\mathbf{A})\). Choose a basis \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_\nu\) of \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\), and extend it to a basis \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_\nu, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_{n - \nu}\) of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (Theorem 4 in Bases and Dimension); it has \(n\) vectors, because every basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) has \(\dim(\mathbb{R}^n) = n\) vectors (Theorem 2 in Bases and Dimension, Example 6 in Bases and Dimension). We show that \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_{n - \nu}\) are a basis of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\), using Theorem 4 in Subspaces and Rank to apply \(\mathbf{A}\) to linear combinations.

They are linearly independent. Suppose \(\sum_{i=1}^{n-\nu} v_i\,\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_i = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_m\). Then \(\mathbf{A}\,\mathopen{}\left(\sum_i v_i \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_i\right)\mathclose{} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_m\) (Theorem 4 in Subspaces and Rank), so \(\sum_i v_i \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_i \in \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\), and it equals \(\sum_{j=1}^{\nu} u_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_j\) for some numbers \(u_j\), since the \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_j\) span \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\). Moving everything to one side,

\[ \sum_{j=1}^{\nu} (-u_j)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_j + \sum_{i=1}^{n-\nu} v_i \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_i = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_n, \]

and because the \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\)’s and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\)’s together form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\), which is linearly independent, every coefficient is \(0\); in particular every \(v_i = 0\).

They span \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\). Take any \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}\) for some \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) (Definition 1 in Column Space and Null Space). Write \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} = \sum_j a_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_j + \sum_i b_i \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_i\) in the basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} &= \mathbf{A}\,\mathopen{}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\nu} a_j \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_j + \sum_{i=1}^{n-\nu} b_i \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_i\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(substitute } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{\nu} a_j\,\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_j + \sum_{i=1}^{n-\nu} b_i\,\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_i && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-subspaces.qmd#thm-matvec-linear}{\text{Theorem~4 in Subspaces and Rank}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{\nu} a_j\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_m + \sum_{i=1}^{n-\nu} b_i\,\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_i && \text{(each } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_j \in \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}) \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{n-\nu} b_i\,\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_i, && \text{(drop the zero terms)} \end{aligned} \]

a linear combination of \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}_{n-\nu}\). These vectors are in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\), so their span is exactly \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\).

So \(\dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\right)\mathclose{} = n - \nu\), and \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = n - \nu\) by Theorem 1.

Write out \(\mathbf{A}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), find the null space, and then find a basis of it.

Theorem 3 (Multiplying on the right cannot increase the rank) If \(\mathbf{A}\) is \(m \times n\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) is \(n \times k\), then

\[ \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B}) \le \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}). \]

Proof. \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B}) \subseteq \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\). Any vector in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B})\) is \((\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) for some \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^k\) (Definition 1 in Column Space and Null Space), and

\[ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= \mathbf{A}\,(\mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

is \(\mathbf{A}\) times the vector \(\mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^n\), so it is in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\).

Compare dimensions. Both column spaces are subspaces (Theorem 1 in Column Space and Null Space), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B}) &= \dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B})\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(}\href{#thm-rank-dim}{\text{Theorem~1}}\text{)} \\ &\le \dim\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-bases.qmd#thm-dim-bound}{\text{Theorem~5 in Bases and Dimension}}\text{, part 2)} \\ &= \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}). && \text{(}\href{#thm-rank-dim}{\text{Theorem~1}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 4 (A product can lose rank) Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{A} \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 \\ 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 & 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

\(\mathbf{A}\) has rank \(1\), but \(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B}\) has rank \(0\), so the inequality in Theorem 3 can be strict. With the \(2 \times 2\) identity in place of \(\mathbf{B}\), \(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{I} = \mathbf{A}\) keeps rank \(1\), and the inequality is an equality.

Exercise 4 (Finding how large the rank of a matrix product can be) Theorem 3 shows how large the rank of a matrix product can be. Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5\end{bmatrix}. \]

Find the number that, by Theorem 3, \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right)\mathclose{}}\) cannot exceed.

Hint

Find \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}}\) first.

Solution 4. Columns of \(\mathbf{A}\) are \((1, 0)\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_2\), so its column space is \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 0)\right\}\mathclose{}\), and Theorem 1 gives \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}} = 1\). By Theorem 3, \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right)\mathclose{}} \le 1\). Indeed, \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\), which has rank \(1\).

Theorem 4 (Fundamental theorem of ranks) For any \(m \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) &= \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) \\ &= \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}) \\ &= \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A} {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}). \end{aligned} \]

In particular, the largest number of linearly independent rows of \(\mathbf{A}\) equals the largest number of linearly independent columns.

Proof. \(\operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\). Both matrices have \(n\) columns, and \(\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\) (Theorem 6 in Column Space and Null Space), so they have the same nullity (Definition 1). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}) &= n - \operatorname{nullity}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}) && \text{(}\href{#thm-rank-nullity}{\text{Theorem~2}}\text{)} \\ &= n - \operatorname{nullity}(\mathbf{A}) && \text{(equal null spaces)} \\ &= \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}). && \text{(}\href{#thm-rank-nullity}{\text{Theorem~2}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

\(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A} {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) = \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\). Apply the previous step to the \(n \times m\) matrix \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\), using \({({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})}^{\top} = \mathbf{A}\).

Chain the inequalities.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) &= \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}) && \text{(first step)} \\ &\le \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) && \text{(}\href{#thm-rank-product}{\text{Theorem~3}}\text{, with } {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \text{ on the left)} \\ &= \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A} {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) && \text{(second step)} \\ &\le \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}). && \text{(}\href{#thm-rank-product}{\text{Theorem~3}}\text{, with } \mathbf{A} \text{ on the left)} \end{aligned} \]

The chain starts and ends at \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\), so every quantity in it equals \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\). The columns of \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\) are the rows of \(\mathbf{A}\), which gives the statement about rows.

Example 5 (Four equal ranks) For \(\mathbf{A}\) in Example 1 in Column Space and Null Space, \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = 1\) (Example 1). Its transpose \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ -2 & -6 \\ -2 & -6 \end{bmatrix}\) has second column \(3\) times its first, so its rank is \(1\). \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}\) in Example 10 in Column Space and Null Space has every row a multiple of \((1, -2, -2)\), so its rank is \(1\) too, and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A} {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + (-2)(-2) + (-2)(-2) & 1 \cdot 3 + (-2)(-6) + (-2)(-6) \\ 3 \cdot 1 + (-6)(-2) + (-6)(-2) & 3 \cdot 3 + (-6)(-6) + (-6)(-6) \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 9 & 27 \\ 27 & 81 \end{bmatrix}, \end{aligned} \]

whose second column is \(3\) times its first, so its rank is \(1\).

Corollary 1 (The rank is at most the smaller dimension) For any \(m \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\), \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) \le \min\mathopen{}\left\{m, n\right\}\mathclose{}\).

Proof. \(\mathbf{A}\) has \(n\) columns, so at most \(n\) of them are linearly independent, and \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) \le n\) (Definition 2 in Subspaces and Rank). Likewise \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\) has \(m\) columns, so \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) \le m\) (Theorem 4).

Example 6 (A \(2 \times 3\) matrix has rank at most \(2\)) By Corollary 1, every \(2 \times 3\) matrix has rank at most \(\min\mathopen{}\left\{2, 3\right\}\mathclose{} = 2\), even though it has \(3\) columns. The matrix \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) of Remark 1 in Subspaces and Rank reaches that bound: its first two columns \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\) are linearly independent, so its rank is \(2\).

Exercise 5 (Finding the rank of a Gram matrix without computing it) A Gram matrix can be larger than the matrix it comes from, but Theorem 4 says it has the same rank. Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Find \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}}\) without computing \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\).

Hint

Find \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}}\) from its two rows, using Theorem 4 once more.

Solution 5. Both rows of \(\mathbf{A}\), \((1, 0, 2)\) and \((0, 1, 1)\), are linearly independent: \(c_1\,(1, 0, 2) + c_2\,(0, 1, 1) = (c_1, c_2, 2 c_1 + c_2)\) is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_3\) only if \(c_1 = c_2 = 0\). So \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}} = 2\), and by Theorem 4

\[ {\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}} = {\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}} = {\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\right)\mathclose{}} = 2, \]

even though \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\) is \(3 \times 3\).

Hint

Use Corollary 1 with \(m = 5\) and \(n = 3\).

Solution 6. By Corollary 1, the rank is at most \(\min\mathopen{}\left\{5, 3\right\}\mathclose{} = 3\). A rank of \(3\) is reached, for example by the \(5 \times 3\) matrix with rows \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_1}^{\top}, {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_2}^{\top}, {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_3}^{\top}, {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_3}^{\top}, {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_3}^{\top}\), whose three columns are linearly independent.

Theorem 5 (Multiplying on the left cannot increase the rank) If \(\mathbf{A}\) is \(m \times n\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) is \(n \times k\), then

\[ \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B}) \le \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{B}). \]

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B}) &= \operatorname{rank}({(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B})}^{\top}) && \text{(}\href{#thm-rank-transpose}{\text{Theorem~4}}\text{)} \\ &= \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-transpose-product}{\text{Theorem~6 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &\le \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{B}}^{\top}) && \text{(}\href{#thm-rank-product}{\text{Theorem~3}}\text{, with } {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \text{ on the left)} \\ &= \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{B}). && \text{(}\href{#thm-rank-transpose}{\text{Theorem~4}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 7 (A product of rank 1 with a factor of rank 2) Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{A} \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 0 \\ 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 & 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

\(\mathbf{A}\) has rank \(2\), because its columns \((1, 0)\) and \((1, 1)\) are linearly independent. \(\mathbf{B}\) has rank \(1\), because its second column is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_2\) and its first is not, and \(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B}\) has rank \(1\) for the same reason. So \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B}) = 1 \le \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{B}) = 1\) (Theorem 5), and the bound of Theorem 3, \(2\), is not the tighter one.

Exercise 7 (Finding how large the rank of a product can be from its right factor) Theorem 5 shows how large the rank of a matrix product can be, given the rank of its right factor. Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be any \(3 \times 2\) matrix, and let

\[ \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Find the number that, by Theorem 5, \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right)\mathclose{}}\) cannot exceed, whatever \(\mathbf{A}\) is.

Hint

Find \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{B}\right)\mathclose{}}\) first.

Solution 7. Each of the two columns of \(\mathbf{B}\) is \((1, 1)\), so its column space is \(\operatorname{span}\mathopen{}\left\{(1, 1)\right\}\mathclose{}\), and Theorem 1 gives \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{B}\right)\mathclose{}} = 1\). By Theorem 5, \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right)\mathclose{}} \le 1\) for every \(3 \times 2\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\).

Corollary 2 (Multiplying by a matrix of full rank does not change the rank) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be \(m \times n\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) be \(n \times k\).

  1. If \(\mathbf{A}\) has full column rank (Definition 3 in Subspaces and Rank), then \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B}) = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{B})\).
  2. If \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{B}) = n\), then \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B}) = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\).

Proof. Part 1. Because \(\mathbf{A}\) has full column rank, \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_m\) only for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_n\) (Theorem 3 in Column Space and Null Space). So for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^k\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A} \mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_m &\iff \mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_n && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-column-null-space.qmd#thm-independent-null-space}{\text{Theorem~3 in Column Space and Null Space}}\text{, with } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = \mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

so \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{B})\) (Definition 3 in Column Space and Null Space), and the two matrices have the same nullity (Definition 1). Both have \(k\) columns, so by Theorem 2 they have the same rank.

Part 2. \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{B})\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (Theorem 1 in Column Space and Null Space) of dimension \(n\) (Theorem 1), so \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{B}) = \mathbb{R}^n\) (Theorem 6 in Bases and Dimension). Every vector of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B})\) is \(\mathbf{A}\,(\mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) for some \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) (Definition 1 in Column Space and Null Space), and \(\mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) takes every vector of \(\mathbb{R}^n\), so \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B}) = \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) and the two matrices have the same rank (Theorem 1).

Example 8 (Ranks of products with a full-rank factor) Let \(\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), which has rank \(1\).

  • Part 1: \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) has two linearly independent columns, so it has full column rank, and \(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) has rank \(1 = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{B})\).
  • Part 2: let \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), which has rank \(1\), and let \(\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), which has rank \(2\). Then \(\mathbf{A} \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) has rank \(1 = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\).

Exercise 8 (Finding the rank of a product with a full-column-rank factor) Corollary 2 says that a left factor with full column rank keeps the rank of the right factor. Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4\end{bmatrix}. \]

Find \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right)\mathclose{}}\) without multiplying.

Hint

Check that \(\mathbf{A}\) has full column rank, and find \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{B}\right)\mathclose{}}\).

Solution 8. Columns of \(\mathbf{A}\), \((1, 0, 1)\) and \((0, 1, 1)\), are linearly independent: the first two entries of \(c_1\,(1, 0, 1) + c_2\,(0, 1, 1)\) are \(c_1\) and \(c_2\). So \(\mathbf{A}\) has full column rank. The second column of \(\mathbf{B}\) is twice the first, so \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{B}\right)\mathclose{}} = 1\). By part 1 of Corollary 2,

\[ {\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right)\mathclose{}} = {\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{B}\right)\mathclose{}} = 1. \]

Theorem 6 (A centered data matrix has rank at most \(n - 1\)) Let \(\mathbf{X}\) be an \(n \times p\) matrix with rows \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_1}^{\top}, \ldots, {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_n}^{\top}\), let \(\bar{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i\) be the mean of the rows, and let \(\mathbf{X}_c = \mathbf{X}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}\,{\bar{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}^{\top}\) be the matrix whose row \(i\) is \({(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i - \bar{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}})}^{\top}\), where \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}\) is the ones vector of length \(n\) (Definition 19 in Vectors). Then \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}_c) \le n - 1\).

Proof. The entries of each column of \(\mathbf{X}_c\) sum to zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_c &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}- \mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}\right)\mathclose{}{\bar{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}^{\top} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-distrib}{\text{Theorem~2 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}_i}^{\top} - n\,{\bar{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}^{\top} && \text{(} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}} = n \text{)} \\ &= n\,{\bar{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}^{\top} - n\,{\bar{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}}^{\top} && \text{(definition of } \bar{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \text{)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p}^{\top}. \end{aligned} \]

Transposing, \({\mathbf{X}_c}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p\). The ones vector is nonzero, so it is in the null space of the \(p \times n\) matrix \({\mathbf{X}_c}^{\top}\), and \(\operatorname{nullity}({\mathbf{X}_c}^{\top}) \ge 1\). By Theorem 2, applied to the \(n\) columns of \({\mathbf{X}_c}^{\top}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}_c) &= \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{X}_c}^{\top}) && \text{(}\href{#thm-rank-transpose}{\text{Theorem~4}}\text{)} \\ &= n - \operatorname{nullity}({\mathbf{X}_c}^{\top}) && \text{(}\href{#thm-rank-nullity}{\text{Theorem~2}}\text{)} \\ &\le n - 1. && \text{(} \operatorname{nullity}({\mathbf{X}_c}^{\top}) \ge 1 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 9 (Two points in three dimensions) \(\mathbf{X}= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) has \(\bar{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = (2, 2, 2)\), so

\[ \mathbf{X}_c = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The second row is \(-1\) times the first, so \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}_c) = 1 = n - 1\), and the column sums are zero: \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_c = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_3}^{\top}\).

Corollary 3 (A sample covariance matrix from \(n \le p\) points is singular) With \(\mathbf{X}_c\) as in Theorem 6, the sample covariance matrix

\[ \mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{n - 1}\,{\mathbf{X}_c}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_c \]

is \(p \times p\) with \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{S}) \le n - 1\). So if \(n \le p\), then \(\mathbf{S}\) is not invertible.

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{S}) &= \operatorname{rank}({\mathbf{X}_c}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_c) && \text{(multiplying by } \tfrac{1}{n-1} \ne 0 \text{ does not change the rank)} \\ &= \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}_c) && \text{(}\href{#thm-rank-transpose}{\text{Theorem~4}}\text{)} \\ &\le n - 1. && \text{(}\href{#thm-centered-data-rank}{\text{Theorem~6}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

If \(n \le p\), then \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{S}) \le n - 1 < p\), so \(\mathbf{S}\) does not have full column rank, and it is not invertible by Theorem 1 in Similarity and Diagonalization.

Example 10 (As many points as dimensions) The \(n = 3\) points \((1, 0, 0)\), \((0, 1, 0)\) and \((0, 0, 1)\) in \(p = 3\) dimensions have \(\bar{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} = \frac{1}{3}(1, 1, 1)\), so

\[ \mathbf{X}_c = \mathbf{I}_3 - \tfrac{1}{3}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}}^{\top}, \qquad \mathbf{S} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{X}_c = \tfrac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \]

using \({\mathbf{X}_c}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_c = \mathbf{X}_c\) (the centering matrix is a symmetric projection). Then \(\mathbf{S}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_3\) and \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{S}) = 2 = n - 1 < 3\). With one more point than dimensions (\(n = p + 1\)), the rank bound allows \(\mathbf{S}\) to have full rank \(p\).

Exercise 9 (Finding how large the rank of centered data can be) Centering a data matrix subtracts the mean vector from every row, and Theorem 6 shows how large the rank of the result can be. Suppose that \(\mathbf{X}\) holds \(3\) observations of \(4\) features, as a \(3 \times 4\) matrix, and \(\mathbf{X}_c\) is its centered version.

Find the number that, by Theorem 6, \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{X}_c\right)\mathclose{}}\) cannot exceed.

Hint

Compare the number from Theorem 6 with the one from Corollary 1.

Solution 9. By Theorem 6 with \(n = 3\), \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{X}_c\right)\mathclose{}} \le 3 - 1 = 2\). That number is smaller than \(\min\mathopen{}\left\{3, 4\right\}\mathclose{} = 3\) from Corollary 1: the three centered rows add up to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_4\), so they are linearly dependent.

Exercise 10 (Deciding whether a sample covariance matrix is invertible) A study records \(6\) features for each of \(4\) people, and forms the \(6 \times 6\) sample covariance matrix \(\mathbf{S}\) defined in Corollary 3.

Decide whether \(\mathbf{S}\) is invertible.

Hint

Compare \(n\) with \(p\).

Solution 10. Here \(n = 4\) and \(p = 6\), so \(n \le p\). By Corollary 3, \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{S}\right)\mathclose{}} \le 4 - 1 = 3 < 6\), so \(\mathbf{S}\) is not invertible.

Definition 2 (Rank factorization) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be an \(m \times n\) matrix with \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = r \ge 1\). A rank factorization [3] of \(\mathbf{A}\) is a product

\[ \underbrace{\mathbf{A}}_{m \times n} = \underbrace{\mathbf{C}}_{m \times r}\,\underbrace{\mathbf{R}}_{r \times n}. \]

Example 11 (A rank factorization of a rank-one matrix) The matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) in Example 1 in Column Space and Null Space has rank \(1\) (Example 1), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot(-2) & 1 \cdot(-2) \\ 3 \cdot 1 & 3 \cdot(-2) & 3 \cdot(-2) \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -2 \\ 3 & -6 & -6 \end{bmatrix}, \end{aligned} \]

so \(\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\) (\(2 \times 1\)) and \(\mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\) (\(1 \times 3\)) are a rank factorization. It is not unique: \(\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} & -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\) give the same product. A product \(\mathbf{C} \mathbf{R}\) with \(\mathbf{C}\) of size \(2 \times 2\) is not a rank factorization of this \(\mathbf{A}\), because the inner dimension must equal \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = 1\).

Theorem 7 (Every nonzero matrix has a rank factorization) Every \(m \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) with \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = r \ge 1\) has a rank factorization (Definition 2). One is given by taking the columns of \(\mathbf{C}\) to be any basis of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\).

Proof. \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) has dimension \(r\) (Theorem 1), so it has a basis \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_r\) (Theorem 4 in Bases and Dimension); let \(\mathbf{C}\) be the \(m \times r\) matrix with these columns. Each column \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j\) of \(\mathbf{A}\) is in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j = r_{1j} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_1 + \cdots + r_{rj} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_r\) for some numbers \(r_{ij}\), because the basis spans \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\). Let \(\mathbf{R}\) be the \(r \times n\) matrix with entries \(r_{ij}\), whose column \(j\) is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{r}}\vphantom{r}}_j = (r_{1j}, \ldots, r_{rj})\). Then column \(j\) of \(\mathbf{C} \mathbf{R}\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{C} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{r}}\vphantom{r}}_j &= r_{1j} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_1 + \cdots + r_{rj} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}_r && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-subspaces.qmd#thm-matvec-columns}{\text{Theorem~3 in Subspaces and Rank}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}_j, && \text{(choice of the } r_{ij} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

so \(\mathbf{C} \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{A}\).

Example 12 (Building a rank factorization from a basis) For \(\mathbf{A}\) in Example 1 in Column Space and Null Space, \((1, 3)\) spans \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\) (Example 2 in Column Space and Null Space), and it is linearly independent because it is nonzero (\(c\,(1, 3) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_2\) forces \(c = 0\)), so it is a basis of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\). The columns of \(\mathbf{A}\) are \((1, 3) = 1 \cdot(1, 3)\), \((-2, -6) = -2 \cdot(1, 3)\) and \((-2, -6) = -2 \cdot(1, 3)\), so the construction in Theorem 7 gives \(\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\), the factorization in Example 11.

Exercise 11 (Finding a rank factorization of a rank-one matrix) A rank factorization writes an \(m \times n\) matrix with rank \(r\) as a matrix product of an \(m \times r\) and an \(r \times n\) matrix. Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}. \]

Find a rank factorization of \(\mathbf{A}\).

Hint

In \(\mathbf{A}\), the second column is twice the first, so \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}} = 1\).

Solution 11. Columns of \(\mathbf{A}\) are \((2, 1)\) and \((4, 2) = 2\,(2, 1)\), so \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}} = 1\), and

\[ \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2 \cdot 1 & 2 \cdot 2 \\ 1 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{A}. \]

So \(\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}\) (\(2 \times 1\)) and \(\mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\end{bmatrix}\) (\(1 \times 2\)) are a rank factorization.

Exercise 12 (Building a rank factorization from a basis of the column space) Theorem 7 builds a rank factorization from a basis of the column space. Let

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 1 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}, \]

in which column \(3\) is column \(1\) plus column \(2\).

Find a rank factorization \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{R}\) in which \(\mathbf{C}\) holds columns 1 and 2 of \(\mathbf{A}\).

Hint

Column \(j\) of \(\mathbf{R}\) says how much of column \(1\) and of column \(2\) of \(\mathbf{C}\) add up to column \(j\) of \(\mathbf{A}\).

Solution 12. Columns 1 and 2 of \(\mathbf{A}\), \((1, 2, 1)\) and \((2, 4, 0)\), are linearly independent: the third entry of \(c_1\,(1, 2, 1) + c_2\,(2, 4, 0)\) is \(c_1\), and then the first is \(2 c_2\). They span \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{A})\), since the third column is the first plus the second, so \({\operatorname{rank}\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}} = 2\). Each column of \(\mathbf{A}\) in terms of them:

\[ \begin{aligned} (1, 2, 1) &= 1 \cdot(1, 2, 1) + 0 \cdot(2, 4, 0), \\ (2, 4, 0) &= 0 \cdot(1, 2, 1) + 1 \cdot(2, 4, 0), \\ (3, 6, 1) &= 1 \cdot(1, 2, 1) + 1 \cdot(2, 4, 0). \end{aligned} \]

So

\[ \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}, \]

and multiplying gives back \(\mathbf{A}\).
