Newton’s Method

Published

Last modified: 2026-11-12 21:47:31 (PST)

NoteSource

This section is adapted from the Newton-Raphson and gradient descent parts of Zhou [1], used under the MIT License (see the license text in Linear Algebra). The source motivates the Newton step informally, as the step that minimizes a quadratic approximation; here Theorem 1 proves that the step is that approximation’s exact minimizer. In the source’s zigzag example the starting value of the objective is \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b^2 + b)\), not \(\tfrac{1}{2}(1 + b^2)\) as the source writes; the ratio of successive values is unaffected. In the same example the source’s formula for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)}\) has \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)}\) on its right side where \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)}\) is meant. These parts of the source are not part of this section:

  • the quadratic convergence theorem for Newton’s method in general, and its remedies for instability (a positive definite substitute for the Hessian, and shortening the Newton step by a search along its direction)
  • its remark that Hessians are expensive to compute
  • the convergence-rate theorem for strongly convex functions
  • gradient descent with momentum, and its optimal parameters
  • Nesterov acceleration, and stochastic gradient descent
  • its code and animations

1 Iterative methods

DefDefinition 1 (Iterative method)

Definition 1 (Iterative method) An iterative method [2] starts from a point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)}\) and computes \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t+1)}\) from \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}\) by a fixed rule, for \(t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\).

DefDefinition 2 (Iterate)

Definition 2 (Iterate) In an iterative method (Definition 1), the points \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}\) are its iterates.

DefDefinition 3 (Error of an iterate)

Definition 3 (Error of an iterate) For a point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) that an iterative method (Definition 1) is meant to reach, \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{}\) is the error of the iterate \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}\) (Definition 2).

DefDefinition 4 (Convergence of a sequence of vectors)

Definition 4 (Convergence of a sequence of vectors) A sequence \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)}, \ldots\) in \(\mathbb{R}^p\) converges to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* \in \mathbb{R}^p\) if the sequence of distances \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{}\) converges to \(0\). For \(p = 1\), \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert x^{(t)} - x^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \mathopen{}\left|x^{(t)} - x^*\right|\mathclose{}\), and this is the convergence of a sequence of numbers to \(x^*\).

ExmExample 1 (Halving a vector)

Example 1 (Halving a vector) The iterative method \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t+1)} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}\) from \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} = {(4, 2)}^{\top}\) has iterates \({(4, 2)}^{\top}, {(2, 1)}^{\top}, {(1, 0.5)}^{\top}, \ldots\), that is, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} = 2^{-t}\,{(4, 2)}^{\top}\). With \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), the error of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert 2^{-t}\,{(4, 2)}^{\top} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= 2^{-t}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert{(4, 2)}^{\top}\right\rVert\mathclose{} && \text{(norm of a multiple)} \\ &= 2^{-t} \sqrt{20}, && \text{(} 4^2 + 2^2 = 20 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

which converges to \(0\), so the iterates converge to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

2 Newton’s method and the quadratic approximation

DefDefinition 5 (Newton’s method for minimization)

Definition 5 (Newton’s method for minimization) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) have first and second partial derivatives, and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)}\) be a starting point. Newton’s method [3] computes, for \(t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\), as long as \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)})\) is invertible,

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t+1)} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} - \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)})\right]\mathclose{}^{-1} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}). \]

DefDefinition 6 (Newton step)

Definition 6 (Newton step) In Newton’s method (Definition 5), the vector \(-\mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)})\right]\mathclose{}^{-1} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)})\) is the Newton step at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}\).

ExmExample 2 (Newton’s method for a cubic)

Example 2 (Newton’s method for a cubic) Let \(f(x) = \tfrac{1}{3} x^3 - 4x\), so \(f'(x) = x^2 - 4\) and \(f''(x) = 2x\). For \(x^{(t)} \ne 0\), one Newton step gives

\[ \begin{aligned} x^{(t+1)} &= x^{(t)} - \frac{(x^{(t)})^2 - 4}{2 x^{(t)}} && \text{(Newton's method with } p = 1 \text{)} \\ &= \frac{2 (x^{(t)})^2 - (x^{(t)})^2 + 4}{2 x^{(t)}} && \text{(common denominator)} \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \mathopen{}\left(x^{(t)} + \frac{4}{x^{(t)}}\right)\mathclose{}. && \text{(simplify)} \end{aligned} \]

From \(x^{(0)} = 2.5\):

  • \[ \begin{aligned} x^{(1)} &= \tfrac{1}{2} (2.5 + 1.6) \\ &= 2.05; \end{aligned} \]

  • \[ \begin{aligned} x^{(2)} &= \tfrac{1}{2} (2.05 + 1.951219\ldots) \\ &= 2.000610 \end{aligned} \]

    (to six decimals);

  • \(x^{(3)} = 2.0000001\) (to seven decimals).

The iterates (Definition 2) approach \(2\), the local minimizer of \(f\) (a stationary point, since \(f'(2) = 0\), with \(f''(2) = 4 > 0\); Theorem 7 in Critical Points and Optimality Conditions).

DefDefinition 7 (Quadratic approximation, quadratic model, second-order Taylor polynomial)

Definition 7 (Quadratic approximation, quadratic model, second-order Taylor polynomial) Let \(f\) be as in Definition 5 and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) be a point, with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) and \(\mathbf{H} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\). The quadratic approximation of \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the function of a step \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\)

\[ {q\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\right)\mathclose{}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}, \]

also called the quadratic model or the second-order Taylor polynomial of \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).

ExmExample 3 (The model at \(x = 2.5\))

Example 3 (The model at \(x = 2.5\)) For \(f(x) = \tfrac{1}{3} x^3 - 4x\) at \(x = 2.5\) (Example 2),

\[ \begin{aligned} g &= 2.5^2 - 4 \\ &= 2.25 \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} H &= [2 \cdot 2.5] \\ &= [5], \end{aligned} \]

so \({q\mathopen{}\left(\delta\right)\mathclose{}} = f(2.5) + 2.25\,\delta+ 2.5\,\delta^2\). For \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \tfrac{1}{2} (x_1^2 + 3 x_2^2)\), whose Hessian is \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\) at every point, the model at any \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) equals \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}})\) exactly (Example 12 in Critical Points and Optimality Conditions with \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3/2 \end{bmatrix}\)).

RemarkRemark 1 (The quadratic approximation and Taylor’s theorem)

Remark 1 (The quadratic approximation and Taylor’s theorem). When the second partial derivatives of \(f\) are continuous, Theorem 6 in Critical Points and Optimality Conditions says that \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}})\) equals the expression for \({q\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\right)\mathclose{}}\) in Definition 7 with the Hessian evaluated at a point between \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\).

LemTheorem 1 (The Newton step minimizes the quadratic approximation)

Theorem 1 (The Newton step minimizes the quadratic approximation) Let \(q\) be the quadratic approximation of \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), with \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) positive definite. Its unique minimizer is the Newton step \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* = -\mathbf{H}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\).

ProofProof

Proof. \(\mathbf{H}\) is symmetric, by the definition of positive definite, and invertible, so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^*\) exists and \(\mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* = -\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\). For any \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\), first

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* &= -\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} && \text{(} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* = -\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \text{)} \\ &= -\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}, && \text{(a } 1 \times 1 \text{ matrix equals its transpose)} \end{aligned} \]

and \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^*{}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^*\) in the same way, using \({\mathbf{H}}^{\top} = \mathbf{H}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} {q\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}})}^{\top} \mathbf{H} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}) && \text{(definition of } q\text{)} \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} + \tfrac{1}{2} \mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^*{}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^*{}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(distribute)} \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^*{}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} && \text{(both cross terms equal } -\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} \text{)} \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* + \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^*{}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^* + \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} && \text{(cancel } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} \text{)} \\ &= {q\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^*\right)\mathclose{}} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}, && \text{(definition of } q\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

which is greater than \({q\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}^*\right)\mathclose{}}\) for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1}\), since \(\mathbf{H}\) is positive definite.

ExmExample 4 (The Newton step at \(x = 2.5\))

Example 4 (The Newton step at \(x = 2.5\)) For the model \({q\mathopen{}\left(\delta\right)\mathclose{}} = f(2.5) + 2.25\,\delta+ 2.5\,\delta^2\) of Example 3, \(H = [5]\) is positive definite, so by Theorem 1 its minimizer is

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta^* &= -H^{-1} g \\ &= -2.25 / 5 \\ &= -0.45. \end{aligned} \]

Setting the derivative \(2.25 + 5 \delta\) to \(0\) gives the same \(\delta^*\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} 2.5 - 0.45 &= 2.05 \\ &= x^{(1)} \end{aligned} \]

(Example 2).

3 The Newton step as a root of the linear approximation of the gradient

DefDefinition 8 (Linear approximation of the gradient)

Definition 8 (Linear approximation of the gradient) Let \(f\) be as in Newton’s method and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) be a point. The linear approximation of the gradient of \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the \(p \times 1\) vector-valued function \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}_{q}}\) of a step \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) whose \(j\)th entry, for \(j = 1, \ldots, p\), is the function whose graph is the tangent plane to the graph of the partial derivative \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), evaluated at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\):

\[ \mathopen{}\left[{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}_{q}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\right)\mathclose{}}\right]\mathclose{}_j \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}. \tag{1}\]

ExmExample 5 (The linear approximation of the derivative of a cubic at \(x = -2.5\))

Example 5 (The linear approximation of the derivative of a cubic at \(x = -2.5\)) For \(f(x) = \tfrac{1}{3} x^3 - 4x\), with \(f'(x) = x^2 - 4\) and \(f''(x) = 2x\) (Example 2), \(p = 1\), so the linear approximation of the gradient at \(x = -2.5\) is the tangent line to \(f'\) there:

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(\delta) &= f'(-2.5) + f''(-2.5)\,\delta && \text{(}\href{#eq-gradient-linear-approximation}{\text{Equation~1}}\text{ with } p = 1 \text{)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left((-2.5)^2 - 4\right)\mathclose{} + 2 (-2.5)\,\delta && \text{(substitute } f' \text{ and } f'' \text{)} \\ &= 2.25 - 5 \delta. && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \tag{2}\]

ExmExample 6 (The tangent line to \(f\) is not the linear approximation of the gradient)

Example 6 (The tangent line to \(f\) is not the linear approximation of the gradient) For the cubic \(f(x) = \tfrac{1}{3} x^3 - 4x\) at \(x = -2.5\) (Example 5), the tangent line to \(f\) itself is

\[ \begin{aligned} f(-2.5) + f'(-2.5)\,\delta &= \mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{1}{3} (-2.5)^3 - 4 (-2.5)\right)\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left((-2.5)^2 - 4\right)\mathclose{} \delta && \text{(substitute } f \text{ and } f' \text{)} \\ &= \tfrac{115}{24} + 2.25\,\delta. && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \tag{3}\]

It is not the linear approximation of the gradient: it approximates \(f\), not \(f'\). Its root, \(\delta= -\tfrac{115}{54}\), is a step toward a zero of \(f\), not toward a stationary point of \(f\).

LemLemma 1 (The linear approximation of the gradient uses the Hessian)

Lemma 1 (The linear approximation of the gradient uses the Hessian) Let \(f\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), the gradient \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\) and the Hessian \(\mathbf{H}\) be as in the quadratic approximation, and let the second partial derivatives of \(f\) be continuous at every point of an open ball around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\). Then the linear approximation of the gradient of \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the gradient plus the Hessian times the step:

\[ {{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}_{q}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\right)\mathclose{}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} + \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}. \tag{4}\]

ProofProof

Proof. For each \(i\) and \(j\), the \(i\)th entry of \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}\) is \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{}\), which is \(\mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}\right]\mathclose{}_{ij}\) by the definitions of the Hessian and of the derivative of a vector-valued function. So, for each \(j\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left[{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}_{q}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\right)\mathclose{}}\right]\mathclose{}_j &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}\right]\mathclose{}_{ij}\,\delta_i && \text{(}\href{#eq-gradient-linear-approximation}{\text{Equation~1}}\text{, as a sum)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\right]\mathclose{}_j + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}\right]\mathclose{}_{ij}\,\delta_i && \text{(definition of } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \text{)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\right]\mathclose{}_j + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}\right]\mathclose{}_{ji}\,\delta_i && \text{(} \mathbf{H} \text{ is symmetric)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\right]\mathclose{}_j + \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\right]\mathclose{}_j && \text{(matrix-vector product)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} + \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\right]\mathclose{}_j. && \text{(vector addition)} \end{aligned} \tag{5}\]

The third line uses the symmetry of the Hessian, whose hypothesis the lemma assumes.

ExmExample 7 (A gradient that equals its linear approximation)

Example 7 (A gradient that equals its linear approximation) For \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \tfrac{1}{2} (x_1^2 + 3 x_2^2)\), the second function of Example 3, the gradient is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} = {(x_1,\ 3 x_2)}^{\top}\), and the Hessian is \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\) at every point. By Lemma 1, the linear approximation of the gradient at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} {{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}_{q}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\right)\mathclose{}} &= \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ 3 x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \delta_1 \\ \delta_2 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(}\href{#eq-gradient-linear-approximation-hessian}{\text{Equation~4}}\text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ 3 x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \delta_1 \\ 3 \delta_2 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(matrix-vector product)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} x_1 + \delta_1 \\ 3 x_2 + 3 \delta_2 \end{bmatrix} && \text{(vector addition)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} x_1 + \delta_1 \\ 3 (x_2 + \delta_2) \end{bmatrix}, && \text{(factor out } 3 \text{)} \end{aligned} \tag{6}\]

which is the gradient of \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\): the gradient of this \(f\) is linear, so its linear approximation is exact.

LemTheorem 2 (The Newton step is the root of the linear approximation of the gradient)

Theorem 2 (The Newton step is the root of the linear approximation of the gradient) Let \(f\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\) and the Hessian \(\mathbf{H}\) be as in Lemma 1, with \(\mathbf{H}\) invertible. The only step \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\) at which the linear approximation of the gradient is zero is the Newton step:

\[ {{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}_{q}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\right)\mathclose{}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1} \iff \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}= -\mathbf{H}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}. \tag{7}\]

ProofProof

Proof. By Lemma 1, \({{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}_{q}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\right)\mathclose{}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1}\) exactly when

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1} &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} + \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}} && \text{(}\href{#eq-gradient-linear-approximation-hessian}{\text{Equation~4}}\text{)} \\ -\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} &= \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}} && \text{(subtract } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \text{ from both sides)} \\ -\mathbf{H}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}. && \text{(multiply both sides by } \mathbf{H}^{-1} \text{ on the left)} \end{aligned} \tag{8}\]

Each line implies the next and, because \(\mathbf{H}\) is invertible, the one before, so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}= -\mathbf{H}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\) is the only solution.

ExmExample 8 (The Newton step at \(x = -2.5\) as a root)

Example 8 (The Newton step at \(x = -2.5\) as a root) At \(x = -2.5\), the linear approximation of the gradient of the cubic \(f(x) = \tfrac{1}{3} x^3 - 4x\) is \(\ell(\delta) = 2.25 - 5 \delta\) (Example 5). Setting it to \(0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} 0 &= 2.25 - 5 \delta && \text{(}\href{#eq-gradient-linear-approximation-cubic}{\text{Equation~2}}\text{)} \\ 5 \delta&= 2.25 && \text{(add } 5 \delta\text{ to both sides)} \\ \delta&= 0.45, && \text{(divide both sides by } 5 \text{)} \end{aligned} \tag{9}\]

so the next iterate is \(-2.5 + 0.45 = -2.05\), the point where the tangent line in Figure 2 crosses \(0\). The Newton’s method iteration \(x^{(t+1)} = \tfrac{1}{2} \mathopen{}\left(x^{(t)} + 4 / x^{(t)}\right)\mathclose{}\) of Example 2 gives the same iterate. Here \(H = [-5]\) is not positive definite, so Theorem 1 does not describe this step, but Theorem 2 does.

RemarkRemark 2 (Two ways to read the Newton step)

Remark 2 (Two ways to read the Newton step). Theorem 1 reads the Newton step as the minimizer of the quadratic approximation, which needs the Hessian \(\mathbf{H}\) to be positive definite. Theorem 2 reads it as the root of the linear approximation of the gradient, which needs only \(\mathbf{H}\) invertible. Newton’s method solves \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1}\) by replacing the gradient with its linear approximation at each iterate, so it looks for a stationary point of any kind, not only a minimizer.

4 Convergence

DefDefinition 9 (Quadratic convergence)

Definition 9 (Quadratic convergence) A sequence \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)}, \ldots\) in \(\mathbb{R}^p\) that converges to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) converges quadratically [4] if there is a constant \(C\) with

\[ \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t+1)} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} \le C\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 \qquad \text{for every } t. \]

ExmExample 9 (Halving the error is not quadratic convergence)

Example 9 (Halving the error is not quadratic convergence) \(x^{(t)} = 2^{-t}\) converges to \(0\), but it does not converge quadratically: the bound would need \(2^{-(t+1)} \le C\,(2^{-t})^2 = C\,2^{-2t}\), that is, \(2^{t - 1} \le C\), which fails once \(t > 1 + \log_2 C\). Each step only halves the error.

ExmExample 10 (The error squares at each step)

Example 10 (The error squares at each step) For the iteration \(x^{(t+1)} = \tfrac{1}{2} (x^{(t)} + 4 / x^{(t)})\) of Example 2,

\[ \begin{aligned} x^{(t+1)} - 2 &= \frac{1}{2} \mathopen{}\left(x^{(t)} + \frac{4}{x^{(t)}}\right)\mathclose{} - 2 && \text{(the iteration)} \\ &= \frac{(x^{(t)})^2 + 4 - 4 x^{(t)}}{2 x^{(t)}} && \text{(common denominator)} \\ &= \frac{(x^{(t)} - 2)^2}{2 x^{(t)}}. && \text{(the numerator is a perfect square)} \end{aligned} \]

So near \(2\) the error is roughly squared and divided by \(4\) at each step: the errors are \(0.05\), then \(0.00061\), then \(0.000000093\), and \(0.05^2 / (2 \cdot 2.05) \approx 0.00061\). The iterates converge quadratically (Definition 9): \(C = \tfrac{1}{2}\) works for every \(x^{(t)} \ge 1\), since then \(\tfrac{1}{2 x^{(t)}} \le \tfrac{1}{2}\), and every iterate from \(2.5\) is at least \(2\), since the identity makes \(x^{(t+1)} - 2 \ge 0\) whenever \(x^{(t)} > 0\). Zhou [1] states such a bound for Newton’s method in general; it holds from starting points close enough to a minimizer where the Hessian is positive definite, when near that minimizer the Hessian changes by at most a constant times the distance moved, which is not proved here.

ExmExample 11 (Newton’s method can find a maximum)

Example 11 (Newton’s method can find a maximum) From \(x^{(0)} = -2.5\) the same iteration gives \(-2.05\), \(-2.000610\), \(-2.0000001\), approaching \(-2\). But \(-2\) is not a minimizer of \(f\): it is a stationary point of \(-f\), whose second derivative at \(-2\) is \(-f''(-2) = 4 > 0\), so it is a strict local minimizer of \(-f\) (Theorem 7 in Critical Points and Optimality Conditions), that is, \(f\) is strictly larger at \(-2\) than at all nearby points. LemTheorem 3 (Newton’s method minimizes a quadratic in one step)

Theorem 3 (Newton’s method minimizes a quadratic in one step) Let \(\mathbf{S}\) be a \(p \times p\) positive definite matrix, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\), and \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\). From any \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)}\), Newton’s method (Definition 5) gives \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)} = \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\), the unique minimizer of \(f\).

ProofProof

Proof. Partial derivatives of a sum or of a constant multiple are the sum or multiple of the partial derivatives, so the derivative of a quadratic form applies to \(\tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and the derivative of a linear combination to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \tfrac{1}{2} (2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} \\ &= \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}; \end{aligned} \]

by the Hessian of a quadratic form with \(\tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{S}\) in place of \(\mathbf{S}\), and because the gradient \(-\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\) of the linear term is constant, \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \mathbf{S}\), which is invertible. So

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)} &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} - \mathbf{S}^{-1} (\mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}) && \text{(definition of Newton's method)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} - \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} + \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} && \text{(distribute)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} + \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} && \text{(} \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{I}_p \text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}. && \text{(cancel)} \end{aligned} \]

The gradient at \(\mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\) is \(\mathbf{S} \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1}\), and \(f\) is strictly convex (Theorem 6 in Convexity in Several Variables, part 2), so \(\mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\) is a global minimizer (Corollary 2 in Convexity in Several Variables). It is the only one: a strictly convex function has at most one global minimizer, since the midpoint of two distinct global minimizers, which have equal values, would have a strictly smaller value (Definition 4 in Convexity in Several Variables with \(t = \tfrac{1}{2}\)).

ExmExample 12 (One step to the minimizer)

Example 12 (One step to the minimizer) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \tfrac{1}{2} (x_1^2 + 3 x_2^2)\), so \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\). From \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} = {(3, 1)}^{\top}\) the gradient is \(\nabla f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)}) = \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} = {(3, 3)}^{\top}\), and \(\mathbf{S}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/3 \end{bmatrix}\), so the Newton step is \(-\mathbf{S}^{-1} {(3, 3)}^{\top} = -{(3, 1)}^{\top}\) and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)} &= {(3, 1)}^{\top} - {(3, 1)}^{\top} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}, \end{aligned} \]

the minimizer.
