1 Optimality conditions in several variables

NoteSource

This section is adapted from the multivariate calculus and optimality-condition parts of Zhou [1], used under the MIT License (see the license text in Linear Algebra). The source states the second-order Taylor approximation and the optimality conditions without proof; here they are proved from the one-variable Taylor theorem, and the approximation is made exact by evaluating the Hessian at a point between \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\). The source calls points with zero gradient critical points; here they are stationary points, because the calculus notes give “critical point” a wider meaning. These parts of the source are not part of this section:

  • its one-variable Taylor example and its plots
  • matrix calculus
  • convexity
  • Lagrange multipliers
  • Newton’s method and gradient descent

A local minimizer of a differentiable function on an open interval is a flat point, and, at a flat point, a positive second derivative guarantees a strict local minimum. This section extends both facts to a function \(f\) of a \(p \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\): the gradient \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) (gradient) takes the place of the first derivative, and a \(p \times p\) matrix of second partial derivatives takes the place of the second; both facts are proved in this section, for every \(p \ge 1\). Throughout, \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}})\) means the gradient evaluated at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}\).

LemTheorem 1 (A minimizer has derivative zero)

Theorem 1 (A minimizer has derivative zero) Let \(g\) be a real-valued function on an open interval containing \(c\), and let \(g\) be differentiable at \(c\). If \(c\) is a local minimizer of \(g\), then \(g'(c) = 0\). [2]

ProofProof

Proof. Take \(\delta> 0\) as in the definition of a local minimizer, shrunk if needed so that \((c - \delta, c + \delta)\) lies inside the interval; then \(g(c) \le g(c + h)\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left|h\right|\mathclose{} < \delta\). For \(0 < h < \delta\), the difference quotient is

\[ \frac{g(c + h) - g(c)}{h} \ge 0, \]

because its numerator is at least \(0\) and its denominator is positive. As \(h \to 0\) from the right, these quotients tend to \(g'(c)\) (the two-sided limit exists, so the one-sided limit equals it), and a limit of numbers that are all at least \(0\) is at least \(0\); so \(g'(c) \ge 0\). For \(-\delta< h < 0\), the numerator is still at least \(0\) but the denominator is negative, so the quotient is at most \(0\), and in the same way, with \(h \to 0\) from the left, \(g'(c) \le 0\). Together, \(g'(c) = 0\).

ExmExample 1 (Applying the test, and its limits)

Example 1 (Applying the test, and its limits)  

  • \(g(x) = (x - 2)^2\) has its minimizer at \(c = 2\), and

    \[ \begin{aligned} g'(2) &= 2 (2 - 2) \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

  • The converse fails: \(g(x) = x^3\) has

    \[ \begin{aligned} g'(0) &= 3 \cdot 0^2 \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

    but \(0\) is not a local minimizer, since

    \[ \begin{aligned} g(-h) &= -h^3 \\ &< 0 \\ &= g(0) \end{aligned} \]

    for every \(h > 0\).

  • Differentiability is needed: \(g(x) = \mathopen{}\left|x\right|\mathclose{}\) has its minimizer at \(0\), but the difference quotient \(\mathopen{}\left|h\right|\mathclose{} / h\) is \(1\) for \(h > 0\) and \(-1\) for \(h < 0\), so it has no limit and \(g'(0)\) does not exist.

DefDefinition 1 (Stationary point)

Definition 1 (Stationary point) Let \(f\) be a scalar-valued function of a \(p \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\). A point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}\) at which the gradient exists and

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1} \]

is a stationary point of \(f\).

ExmExample 2 (A stationary point, and a point that is not one)

Example 2 (A stationary point, and a point that is not one) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 2 x_1\). Its gradient is \({(2 x_1 - 2,\ 2 x_2)}^{\top}\), which is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\) exactly when \(x_1 = 1\) and \(x_2 = 0\). So \({(1, 0)}^{\top}\) is its only stationary point, and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is not one: the gradient there is \({(-2, 0)}^{\top}\).

RemarkRemark 1 (Stationary points in one variable)

Remark 1 (Stationary points in one variable). For \(p = 1\), a stationary point is a flat point.

LemTheorem 2 (First-order necessary condition)

Theorem 2 (First-order necessary condition) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\). If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) is a local minimizer of \(f\) and the gradient of \(f\) exists at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) is a stationary point of \(f\) (Definition 1).

ProofProof

Proof. Take \(\delta> 0\) as in the definition of a local minimizer. Fix \(i \in \mathopen{}\left\{1, \ldots, p\right\}\mathclose{}\), let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i\) be the vector with \(1\) in entry \(i\) and \(0\) elsewhere, and let \(g_i(t) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* + t\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i)\) for \(t \in \mathbb{R}\). For \(\mathopen{}\left|t\right|\mathclose{} < \delta\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* + t\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i) - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left|t\right|\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i\right\rVert\mathclose{} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left|t\right|\mathclose{} \\ &< \delta, \end{aligned} \]

so

\[ \begin{aligned} g_i(0) &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) \\ &\le f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* + t\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i) \\ &= g_i(t): \end{aligned} \]

\(0\) is a local minimizer of \(g_i\). Moving \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) from \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) along \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i\) changes only \(x_i\), so \(g_i'(0)\) is the partial derivative \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\), which exists because the gradient does. By Theorem 1,

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) &= g_i'(0) \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

The equation \(g_i'(0) = 0\) holds for every \(i\), so every entry of the gradient at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) is \(0\) (vector derivative).

ExmExample 3 (Using the condition to locate, and to rule out, minimizers)

Example 3 (Using the condition to locate, and to rule out, minimizers)  

  • For \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 2 x_1\) of Example 2, any local minimizer must be the stationary point \({(1, 0)}^{\top}\). It is one: completing the square,

    \[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= (x_1 - 1)^2 + x_2^2 - 1 \\ &\ge -1 \\ &= f(1, 0). \end{aligned} \]

  • For \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = e^{2x_1 + x_2} - x_1\) of Example, the second entry of the gradient is \(e^{2x_1 + x_2} > 0\), so \(f\) has no stationary point, and so no local minimizer.

  • The converse fails: \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 - x_2^2\) has gradient \({(2 x_1,\ -2 x_2)}^{\top}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is a stationary point, but

    \[ \begin{aligned} f(0, t) &= -t^2 \\ &< 0 \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) \end{aligned} \]

    for every \(t \ne 0\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is not a local minimizer.

ExmExample 4 (A stationary point of the sum of squared errors, on real data)

Example 4 (A stationary point of the sum of squared errors, on real data) The Auto data record the horsepower and fuel economy (miles per gallon) of 392 car models sold between 1970 and 1982. They come from the StatLib library at Carnegie Mellon University and ship with the book An Introduction to Statistical Learning, in the Python package ISLP (ISLP.load_data("Auto")) and the R package ISLR2 (ISLR2::Auto). This site has a copy at data/auto.csv, and both code tabs below read that file.

Let \(y_i\) be the miles per gallon and \(x_i\) the horsepower of car \(i\). To predict miles per gallon from horsepower with a line, write \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= {(\beta_{0}, \beta_{1})}^{\top}\) and let \(\mathbf{X}\) be the matrix whose row \(i\) is \((1, x_i)\). The sum of squared errors is

\[ f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}) = \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2, \]

a scalar-valued function of the \(2 \times 1\) vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\). Its gradient is \(-2\,{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}})\). By Theorem 2, a minimizer of \(f\) must make this gradient \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), which is the equation \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= {\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\). The code below solves that equation and evaluates the gradient at the solution and at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

auto = pd.read_csv("data/auto.csv")
y = auto["mpg"].to_numpy(dtype=float)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(auto)), auto["horsepower"].to_numpy(dtype=float)])

def grad(beta):
    return -2 * X.T @ (y - X @ beta)

beta_hat = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)
grad_hat = grad(beta_hat)
grad_zero = grad(np.zeros(2))

pd.DataFrame(
    {"beta": [beta_hat, np.zeros(2)], "gradient": [grad_hat, grad_zero]},
    index=["solution", "zero vector"],
)

LemTheorem 3 (Taylor’s theorem with a second-order remainder)

Theorem 3 (Taylor’s theorem with a second-order remainder) Let \(a < b\), and let \(g\) be a real-valued function on \([a, b]\) whose derivative \(g'\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) and whose second derivative \(g''\) exists at every point of \((a, b)\). Then there is a \(\tau\in (a, b)\) with [3]

\[ g(b) = g(a) + g'(a)\,(b - a) + \frac{1}{2}\,g''(\tau)\,(b - a)^2. \]

This theorem is the case \(n = 2\) of [4], Theorem 5.15; its proof uses the mean value theorem, which these notes do not develop.

ExmExample 5 (The remainder point for \(e^x\))

Example 5 (The remainder point for \(e^x\)) Take \(g(x) = e^x\) on \([0, 1]\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} g' &= g'' \\ &= g. \end{aligned} \]

Theorem 3 says \(e = 1 + 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\,e^{\tau}\) for some \(\tau\in (0, 1)\). Solving,

\[ \begin{aligned} e^{\tau} &= 2 (e - 2) \\ &\approx 2 \times 0.71828 \\ &= 1.43656, \end{aligned} \]

so \(\tau= \log 1.43656 \approx 0.362\) (natural logarithm), which is in \((0, 1)\).

LemTheorem 4 (Second-order Taylor theorem in several variables)

Theorem 4 (Second-order Taylor theorem in several variables) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) have first and second partial derivatives that are continuous on \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (continuity), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\). Then there is a \(\tau\in (0, 1)\) with [3]

\[ f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}) = f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}) + {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} + \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \tau\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}. \]

ProofProof

Proof. Let \(g(t) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\) for \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), the values of \(f\) along the line through \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}\) in the direction \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\). The inner function \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}(t) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\) has entries \(y_j = z_j + t h_j\), so \(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} y_j = h_j\), and its derivative (vector-valued derivative) is the \(1 \times p\) matrix \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\). By the vector chain rule (vector chain rule, with its input \(t\) of length \(1\), its inner function \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}(t) = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\), and its outer function \(f\)),

\[ \begin{aligned} g'(t) &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}) && \text{(vector chain rule)} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^ph_j\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}). && \text{(matrix product)} \end{aligned} \]

The second step writes out the matrix product. \(g'\) is continuous (continuity): \(t \mapsto \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\) is continuous, since \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + s \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}) - (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \mathopen{}\left|s - t\right|\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\), each \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f\) is continuous by assumption, and compositions, sums and constant multiples of continuous functions are continuous. Each \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f\) has continuous partial derivatives, the second partial derivatives of \(f\), so the same chain-rule computation applies to it: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \mathopen{}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\right]\mathclose{} = \sum_{i=1}^ph_i\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\right)\mathclose{}\). So

\[ \begin{aligned} g''(t) &= \sum_{j=1}^ph_j \sum_{i=1}^ph_i\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(differentiate each term of } g'(t) \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^p\sum_{j=1}^ph_i\,\mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\right]\mathclose{}_{ij}\,h_j && \text{(definition of the Hessian; reorder the finite sums)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}. && \text{(matrix product)} \end{aligned} \]

The last step is again the matrix product. Now apply Theorem 3 to \(g\) on \([0, 1]\): there is a \(\tau\in (0, 1)\) with

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}) &= g(1) && \text{(definition of } g \text{)} \\ &= g(0) + g'(0)\,(1 - 0) + \frac{1}{2}\,g''(\tau)\,(1 - 0)^2 && \text{(}\href{#thm-taylor-1d}{\text{Theorem~3}}\text{, with } a = 0, b = 1 \text{)} \\ &= g(0) + g'(0) + \frac{1}{2}\,g''(\tau) && \text{(} 1 - 0 = 1 \text{)} \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}) + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}) + \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \tau\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} && \text{(substitute } g(0), g'(0), g''(\tau) \text{)} \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}) + {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} + \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + \tau\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}. && \text{(a } 1 \times 1 \text{ matrix equals its transpose)} \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 2 (Strict local minimizer)

Definition 2 (Strict local minimizer) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\). A point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) is a strict local minimizer of \(f\) if there is a number \(\delta> 0\) such that \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) < f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) with \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\).

ExmExample 7 (Strict and non-strict local minimizers)

Example 7 (Strict and non-strict local minimizers)  

  • \(f(x) = (x - 2)^2\) has

    \[ \begin{aligned} f(2) &= 0 \\ &< (x - 2)^2 \\ &= f(x) \end{aligned} \]

    for every \(x \ne 2\), so \(2\) is a strict local minimizer (any \(\delta> 0\) works).

  • A constant function \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 0\) has \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) \le f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) for all \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), so every point is a local minimizer, but no point is a strict one: \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) < f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) never holds.

LemLemma 1 (A quadratic form is at least the smallest eigenvalue times the squared length)

Lemma 1 (A quadratic form is at least the smallest eigenvalue times the squared length) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be a symmetric \(p \times p\) matrix, and let \(\lambda_{\min}\) be the smallest of its eigenvalues (eigendecomposition). Then \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} \ge \lambda_{\min}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2\) for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\).

ProofProof

Proof. Write \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{\Lambda} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\) with \(\mathbf{Q}\) orthogonal and \(\mathbf{\Lambda} = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_p)\) (spectral theorem), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{\Lambda} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} && \text{(substitute the eigendecomposition)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}^{\top} \mathbf{\Lambda} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} && \text{(} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q} = {({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}})}^{\top} \text{, transpose of a product)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^p\lambda_i\,y_i^2 && \text{(} \mathbf{\Lambda} \text{ is diagonal)} \\ &\ge \sum_{i=1}^p\lambda_{\min}\,y_i^2 && \text{(} \lambda_i \ge \lambda_{\min} \text{ and } y_i^2 \ge 0 \text{)} \\ &= \lambda_{\min}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(the squared length is the sum of squares)} \\ &= \lambda_{\min}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2. && \text{(orthogonal matrices preserve length)} \end{aligned} \]

The second step is the transpose of a product. The last step applies orthogonal matrices preserve length to \({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\), which is orthogonal because

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \mathbf{Q} &= \mathbf{Q} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} \\ &= \mathbf{I}_p \end{aligned} \]

(orthogonal matrix).

ExmExample 8 (Checking the bound)

Example 8 (Checking the bound) \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) has eigenvalues \(3\) and \(1\) (eigenvalue example), so \(\lambda_{\min} = 1\). At \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} = {(1, 0)}^{\top}\), \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} = 2 \ge 1 \cdot 1\). At \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} = {(1, -1)}^{\top}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} &= 2 - 1 - 1 + 2 \\ &= 2 \\ &= 1 \cdot 2: \end{aligned} \]

the bound holds with equality, since this \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\) is an eigenvector for \(\lambda_{\min}\).

LemLemma 2 (A quadratic form with small entries is small)

Lemma 2 (A quadratic form with small entries is small) Let \(\mathbf{E}\) be a \(p \times p\) matrix whose entries all satisfy \(\mathopen{}\left|e_{ij}\right|\mathclose{} \le m\). Then \(\mathopen{}\left|{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{E} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right|\mathclose{} \le p\,m\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2\) for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\).

ProofProof

Proof. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{(\mathopen{}\left|h_1\right|\mathclose{}, \ldots, \mathopen{}\left|h_p\right|\mathclose{})}^{\top}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{(1, \ldots, 1)}^{\top}\), both in \(\mathbb{R}^p\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left|{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{E} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right|\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left|\sum_{i=1}^p\sum_{j=1}^ph_i\,e_{ij}\,h_j\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(matrix product)} \\ &\le \sum_{i=1}^p\sum_{j=1}^p\mathopen{}\left|h_i\right|\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left|e_{ij}\right|\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left|h_j\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(triangle inequality for numbers)} \\ &\le m \sum_{i=1}^p\sum_{j=1}^p\mathopen{}\left|h_i\right|\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left|h_j\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(} \mathopen{}\left|e_{ij}\right|\mathclose{} \le m \text{)} \\ &= m\,\mathopen{}\left(\sum_{i=1}^p\mathopen{}\left|h_i\right|\mathclose{}\right)\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(the double sum factors)} \\ &= m\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}})^2 && \text{(dot product)} \\ &\le m\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(Cauchy-Schwarz, squared)} \\ &= m\,p\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2. && \text{(} \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{1}}\vphantom{1}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 = p \text{ and } \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 = \textstyle\sum_i h_i^2 \text{, which equals } \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

The steps use the matrix product, the dot product and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

ExmExample 9 (Checking the bound)

Example 9 (Checking the bound) Let \(\mathbf{E} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 & -0.2 \\ -0.2 & 0.1 \end{bmatrix}\), so \(m = 0.2\) works, and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} = {(1, 1)}^{\top}\), so \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 = 2\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{E} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} &= 0.1 - 0.2 - 0.2 + 0.1 \\ &= -0.2, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left|-0.2\right|\mathclose{} &= 0.2 \\ &\le 2 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 2 \\ &= 0.8. \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 5 (Second-order sufficient condition)

Theorem 5 (Second-order sufficient condition) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) have first and second partial derivatives that are continuous on \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (continuity). If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) is a stationary point of \(f\) (Definition 1) and \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\) is positive definite, then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) is a strict local minimizer of \(f\) (Definition 2).

ProofProof

Proof. \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\) is positive definite, and so symmetric, so its smallest eigenvalue \(\lambda\) is positive (definiteness and eigenvalues). Each of the \(p^2\) entries of \(\mathbf{H}_f\) is continuous at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) (continuity): for \(\varepsilon= \lambda/ (2p)\) there is a \(\delta_{ij} > 0\) with \(\mathopen{}\left|\mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\right]\mathclose{}_{ij} - \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\right]\mathclose{}_{ij}\right|\mathclose{} < \lambda/ (2p)\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta_{ij}\). Let \(\delta\) be the smallest of these \(p^2\) numbers.

Now let \(0 < \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\). By Theorem 4 there is a \(\tau\in (0, 1)\) for which the first step of the next display holds; let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* + \tau\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\) and \(\mathbf{E} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\). Since \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} = \tau\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\), every entry of \(\mathbf{E}\) has absolute value less than \(\lambda/ (2p)\), so Lemma 2 applies with \(m = \lambda/ (2p)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) &= {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} + \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} && \text{(}\href{#thm-taylor-mv}{\text{Theorem~4}}\text{)} \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} && \text{(} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* \text{ is stationary)} \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} + \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{E}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} && \text{(} \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) = \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) + \mathbf{E} \text{; distribute)} \\ &\ge \frac{1}{2}\,\lambda\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 + \frac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{E}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} && \text{(}\href{#lem-qf-eigen-bound}{\text{Lemma~1}}\text{)} \\ &\ge \frac{1}{2}\,\lambda\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 - \frac{1}{2}\,\mathopen{}\left|{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{E}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right|\mathclose{} && \text{(a number is at least minus its absolute value)} \\ &\ge \frac{1}{2}\,\lambda\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 - \frac{1}{2}\,p\,\frac{\lambda}{2p}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(}\href{#lem-qf-entry-bound}{\text{Lemma~2}}\text{, } m = \lambda/ (2p) \text{)} \\ &= \frac{\lambda}{4}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(arithmetic)} \\ &> 0. && \text{(} \lambda> 0 \text{ and } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

So \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) < f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) whenever \(0 < \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\) (take \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\)).

ExmExample 10 (Classifying stationary points)

Example 10 (Classifying stationary points)  

  • \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 2 x_1\) has the stationary point \({(1, 0)}^{\top}\) (Example 2), and its Hessian is

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= 2 \mathbf{I}_2, \end{aligned} \]

    which is positive definite, so \({(1, 0)}^{\top}\) is a strict local minimizer.

  • \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 - x_2^2\) has the stationary point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) and Hessian \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\), which is not positive definite (\({(0, 1)}^{\top}\) gives \(-2\)), and indeed \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is not a local minimizer (Example 3).

ExmExample 11 (The condition is sufficient, not necessary)

Example 11 (The condition is sufficient, not necessary) \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^4\) and \(g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 - x_2^4\) both have gradient \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) and the same Hessian there, \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), since

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_2} (\pm x_2^4)\right)\mathclose{} &= \pm 12 x_2^2 \\ &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

at \(x_2 = 0\). That Hessian is not positive definite (\({(0, 1)}^{\top}\) gives \(0\)), so Theorem 5 says nothing about either function. In fact \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is a strict local minimizer of \(f\), since \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) > 0 = f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}})\) for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), but not a local minimizer of \(g\), since

\[ \begin{aligned} g(0, t) &= -t^4 \\ &< 0 \\ &= g(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) \end{aligned} \]

for \(t \ne 0\).

DefDefinition 3 (Local maximizer)

Definition 3 (Local maximizer) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\). A point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* \in \mathbb{R}^p\) is a local maximizer of \(f\) if there is a number \(\delta> 0\) such that \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) \ge f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) for all \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) with \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\).

ExmExample 12 (A local maximizer)

Example 12 (A local maximizer) Let \(f(x) = 1 - x^2\). For every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} f(0) - f(x) &= 1 - (1 - x^2) && \text{(substitute)} \\ &= x^2 && \text{(simplify)} \\ &\ge 0, && \text{(a square)} \end{aligned} \]

so \(f(0) \ge f(x)\) for all \(x\), and \(0\) is a local maximizer of \(f\) with any \(\delta> 0\).

DefDefinition 4 (Saddle point)

Definition 4 (Saddle point) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\). A stationary point of \(f\) that is neither a local minimizer nor a local maximizer of \(f\) is a saddle point of \(f\) [5].

ExmExample 13 (A saddle point)

Example 13 (A saddle point) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 - x_2^2\). Its gradient is \({(2 x_1,\ -2 x_2)}^{\top}\), which is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\) only at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\) is a stationary point. For every \(t \ne 0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} f(t, 0) &= t^2 && \text{(substitute)} \\ &> 0 && \text{(} t \ne 0 \text{)} \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}), && \text{(substitute)} \\ f(0, t) &= -t^2 && \text{(substitute)} \\ &< 0 && \text{(} t \ne 0 \text{)} \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}). && \text{(substitute)} \end{aligned} \]

So every ball around \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\) contains a point with a larger value of \(f\) and a point with a smaller one. Hence \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\) is neither a local minimizer nor a local maximizer, and it is a saddle point.

DefDefinition 5 (Convex set)

Definition 5 (Convex set) A set \(K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p\) is convex if [6] for all \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in K\) and all \(t \in [0, 1]\), the point \(t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ (1 - t) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) is in \(K\): \(K\) contains the whole line segment between any two of its points.

ExmExample 14 (Convex sets)

Example 14 (Convex sets)  

  • \(\mathbb{R}^p\) is convex, and so is any set with at most one point.

  • The closed ball (closed ball) \(B = \mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}: \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \le r\right\}\mathclose{}\) is convex: for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in B\) and \(t \in [0, 1]\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ (1 - t) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} &\le \mathopen{}\left\lVert t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left\lVert(1 - t) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} && \text{(triangle inequality)} \\ &= t\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} + (1 - t)\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} && \text{(norm of a multiple; } t, 1 - t \ge 0 \text{)} \\ &\le t\,r + (1 - t)\,r && \text{(} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in B \text{)} \\ &= r. && \text{(arithmetic)} \end{aligned} \]

    The first step is the triangle inequality, and the second uses the norm of a multiple.

ExmExample 15 (A set that is not convex)

Example 15 (A set that is not convex) \(K = \mathopen{}\left\{x \in \mathbb{R}: \mathopen{}\left|x\right|\mathclose{} \ge 1\right\}\mathclose{}\) is not convex: \(-1\) and \(1\) are in \(K\), but with \(t = \tfrac{1}{2}\) the point \(\tfrac{1}{2}(-1) + \tfrac{1}{2}(1) = 0\) is not.

DefDefinition 6 (Half-space)

Definition 6 (Half-space) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\) be a nonzero vector and \(c\) a real number. The set \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p : \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \ge c\right\}\mathclose{}\) is a (closed) half-space: the points on one side of the boundary \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}: \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = c\right\}\mathclose{}\), together with the boundary. In \(\mathbb{R}^2\), a half-space is called a half-plane.

ExmExample 16 (The right half-plane)

Example 16 (The right half-plane) With \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{a}}\vphantom{a}} = {(1, 0)}^{\top}\) and \(c = 0\), the half-plane \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^2 : x_1 \ge 0\right\}\mathclose{}\) is the set of points on or to the right of the \(x_2\)-axis. \({(1, 5)}^{\top}\) is in it, since \(1 \ge 0\); \({(0, -3)}^{\top}\) is in it, on the boundary; and \({(-1, 0)}^{\top}\) is not, since \(-1 < 0\).

LemTheorem 6 (An intersection of convex sets is convex)

Theorem 6 (An intersection of convex sets is convex) If \(K_1\) and \(K_2\) are convex subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (Definition 5), then \(K_1 \cap K_2\) is convex.

ProofProof

Proof. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in K_1 \cap K_2\) and \(t \in [0, 1]\). Then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in K_1\), so \(t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ (1 - t) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in K_1\), since \(K_1\) is convex; in the same way \(t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ (1 - t) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in K_2\). So \(t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ (1 - t) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in K_1 \cap K_2\).

ExmExample 17 (Intersecting a ball with a half-plane)

Example 17 (Intersecting a ball with a half-plane) The closed unit ball (unit ball) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is convex (Example 14), and so is the half-plane (Definition 6) \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}: x_1 \ge 0\right\}\mathclose{}\): if \(x_1 \ge 0\) and \(y_1 \ge 0\), then \(t x_1 + (1 - t) y_1 \ge 0\) for \(t \in [0, 1]\). By Theorem 6, the closed half-disk \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}: \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} \le 1,\ x_1 \ge 0\right\}\mathclose{}\) is convex. A union need not be convex: \(\mathopen{}\left\{x \in \mathbb{R}: x \le -1\right\}\mathclose{}\) and \(\mathopen{}\left\{x \in \mathbb{R}: x \ge 1\right\}\mathclose{}\) are convex, but their union is the set of Example 15.

DefDefinition 7 (Strictly convex function)

Definition 7 (Strictly convex function) A function \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) is strictly convex if \(f(t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ (1 - t) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) < t f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + (1 - t) f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\) for all \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^p\) and all \(t \in (0, 1)\).

DefDefinition 8 (Concave function)

Definition 8 (Concave function) A function \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) is concave if \(-f\) is convex.

DefDefinition 9 (Strictly concave function)

Definition 9 (Strictly concave function) A function \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) is strictly concave if \(-f\) is strictly convex (Definition 7).

ExmExample 18 (Strictly convex, and convex but not strictly)

Example 18 (Strictly convex, and convex but not strictly)  

  • \(f(x) = x^2\) is strictly convex:

    \[ \begin{aligned} t x^2 + (1 - t) y^2 - (t x + (1 - t) y)^2 &= t x^2 + (1 - t) y^2 - t^2 x^2 - 2 t (1 - t) x y - (1 - t)^2 y^2 && \text{(expand the square)} \\ &= t (1 - t) x^2 - 2 t (1 - t) x y + t (1 - t) y^2 && \text{(} t - t^2 = t (1 - t) \text{, } (1 - t) - (1 - t)^2 = t (1 - t) \text{)} \\ &= t (1 - t) (x - y)^2, && \text{(factor)} \end{aligned} \]

    which is positive when \(x \ne y\) and \(t \in (0, 1)\). At \(t \in \mathopen{}\left\{0, 1\right\}\mathclose{}\) or \(x = y\) it is \(0\), so \(x^2\) is also convex.

  • \(f(x) = 2x + 1\) is convex but not strictly convex:

    \[ \begin{aligned} f(t x + (1 - t) y) &= 2 t x + 2 (1 - t) y + 1 \\ &= t f(x) + (1 - t) f(y) \end{aligned} \]

    for every \(x, y, t\), so the inequality holds, but never strictly. It is also concave.

LemTheorem 7 (The maximum of convex functions is convex)

Theorem 7 (The maximum of convex functions is convex) If \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) are convex functions on \(\mathbb{R}^p\), then \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\max\mathopen{}\left\{f_1(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}), f_2(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\}\mathclose{}\) is convex.

ProofProof

Proof. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) and \(t \in [0, 1]\). For \(i = 1, 2\),

\[ \begin{aligned} f_i(t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ (1 - t) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) &\le t f_i(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + (1 - t) f_i(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) && \text{(} f_i \text{ is convex)} \\ &\le t f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + (1 - t) f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}). && \text{(} f_i \le f \text{, and } t, 1 - t \ge 0 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Both \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) at \(t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ (1 - t) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) are at most \(t f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + (1 - t) f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\), so their maximum \(f(t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ (1 - t) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\) is too.

ExmExample 19 (A maximum is convex; a minimum need not be)

Example 19 (A maximum is convex; a minimum need not be)  

  • \(f_1(x) = x\) is convex, by the computation for \(2x + 1\) in Example 18 with \(2\) and \(1\) replaced by \(1\) and \(0\), and \(f_2(x) = x^2\) is convex by the same example, so \(\max\mathopen{}\left\{x, x^2\right\}\mathclose{}\) is convex by Theorem 7.

  • The minimum of two convex functions need not be convex: \(g(x) = \min\mathopen{}\left\{x^2, (x - 2)^2\right\}\mathclose{}\) has

    \[ \begin{aligned} g(0) &= g(2) \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

    but at the midpoint

    \[ \begin{aligned} g(1) &= \min\mathopen{}\left\{1, 1\right\}\mathclose{} \\ &= 1 \\ &> \tfrac{1}{2} g(0) + \tfrac{1}{2} g(2) \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 10 (Epigraph)

Definition 10 (Epigraph) The epigraph [7] of \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) is the set of points on or over its graph,

\[ \operatorname{epi} f \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathopen{}\left\{(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, s) : \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p,\ s \in \mathbb{R},\ f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \le s\right\}\mathclose{}. \]

A pair \((\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, s)\) is treated as the \((p + 1)\)-vector with entries \(x_1, \ldots, x_p, s\), so \(\operatorname{epi} f \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{p+1}\), and sums and multiples of pairs are taken entry by entry.

ExmExample 20 (The epigraph of \(x^2\))

Example 20 (The epigraph of \(x^2\)) For \(f(x) = x^2\), \(\operatorname{epi} f = \mathopen{}\left\{(x, s) : x^2 \le s\right\}\mathclose{}\), the points \((x, s)\) on the parabola \(s = x^2\) or on the side of it where \(s\) is larger. \((1, 2)\) is in it, since \(1^2 \le 2\); \((2, 1)\) is not, since \(2^2 > 1\).

LemTheorem 8 (Convexity through the epigraph)

Theorem 8 (Convexity through the epigraph) \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) is convex if and only if its epigraph (Definition 10) is convex (Definition 5).

ExmExample 21 (Two epigraphs)

Example 21 (Two epigraphs)  

  • The epigraph of \(f(x) = \mathopen{}\left|x\right|\mathclose{}\) is \(\mathopen{}\left\{(x, s) : \mathopen{}\left|x\right|\mathclose{} \le s\right\}\mathclose{}\), a wedge. It is the intersection of the half-planes \(\mathopen{}\left\{(x, s) : s - x \ge 0\right\}\mathclose{}\) and \(\mathopen{}\left\{(x, s) : s + x \ge 0\right\}\mathclose{}\). Each half-plane is convex: if \(s_1 - x_1 \ge 0\) and \(s_2 - x_2 \ge 0\), then \((t s_1 + (1 - t) s_2) - (t x_1 + (1 - t) x_2) = t (s_1 - x_1) + (1 - t)(s_2 - x_2) \ge 0\), and the same with \(+\) for the other. So the wedge is convex (Theorem 6), and \(\mathopen{}\left|x\right|\mathclose{}\) is convex by Theorem 8.
  • The epigraph of \(g(x) = -x^2\) is not convex: \((-1, -1)\) and \((1, -1)\) are in it, since \(g(\pm 1) = -1\), but their midpoint \((0, -1)\) is not, since \(g(0) = 0 > -1\). So \(-x^2\) is not convex.

Definition 11 (Tangent plane) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) have a gradient at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) (gradient). The tangent plane [8] to the graph of \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) is the graph of the function

which takes the value \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) and has the same gradient as \(f\) there.

ExmExample 22 (A tangent line and a tangent plane)

Example 22 (A tangent line and a tangent plane)  

  • For \(f(x) = x^2\) at \(y = 1\), \(f(1) = 1\) and \(f'(1) = 2\), so the tangent line is

    \[ \begin{aligned} s &= 1 + 2\,(x - 1) && \text{(substitute } f(1) \text{ and } f'(1) \text{)} \\ &= 1 + 2x - 2 && \text{(distribute)} \\ &= 2x - 1. && \text{(} 1 - 2 = -1 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

  • For \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= {(1, 1)}^{\top}\), \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) = 2\) and the gradient is \({(2 y_1,\ 2 y_2)}^{\top} = {(2, 2)}^{\top}\), so the tangent plane is

    \[ \begin{aligned} s &= 2 + 2\,(x_1 - 1) + 2\,(x_2 - 1) && \text{(substitute } f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) \text{ and the gradient)} \\ &= 2 + 2 x_1 - 2 + 2 x_2 - 2 && \text{(distribute)} \\ &= 2 x_1 + 2 x_2 - 2. && \text{(} 2 - 2 - 2 = -2 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 9 (First-order characterization of convexity)

Theorem 9 (First-order characterization of convexity) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) have first partial derivatives that are continuous on \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (continuity). Then \(f\) is convex if and only if

\[ f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \ge f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) + {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) \qquad \text{for all } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in \mathbb{R}^p: \tag{1}\]

every tangent plane (Definition 11) lies on or under the graph. If the inequality is strict whenever \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\), then \(f\) is strictly convex (Definition 7).

ProofProof

Proof. Only if. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\), let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\), and let \(g(t) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}+ t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}})\). The inner function \(t \mapsto \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}+ t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}\) has derivative \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}}^{\top}\) (vector-valued derivative), so the vector chain rule (vector chain rule) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} g'(0) &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}}^{\top}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) && \text{(vector chain rule)} \\ &= {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}. && \text{(a } 1 \times 1 \text{ matrix equals its transpose)} \end{aligned} \]

For \(t \in (0, 1]\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}+ t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} = t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ (1 - t) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} g(t) - g(0) &= f(t \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ (1 - t) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) && \text{(definition of } g \text{)} \\ &\le t f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + (1 - t) f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) && \text{(} f \text{ is convex)} \\ &= t\,(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})), && \text{(collect terms)} \end{aligned} \]

and dividing by \(t > 0\), \(\dfrac{g(t) - g(0)}{t} \le f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) - f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\). Example 23 (Tangent lines under a parabola) For \(f(x) = x^2\), the right side of Equation 1 is \(y^2 + 2y (x - y) = 2xy - y^2\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) - (2xy - y^2) &= x^2 - 2xy + y^2 \\ &= (x - y)^2 \\ &\ge 0, \end{aligned} \]

with equality only at \(x = y\), confirming that \(x^2\) is strictly convex. For \(g(x) = -x^2\) the inequality fails: at \(y = 0\) the tangent line is \(s = 0\), and \(g(1) = -1 < 0\).

LemTheorem 10 (Second-derivative test for convexity)

Theorem 10 (Second-derivative test for convexity) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) have first and second partial derivatives that are continuous on \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (continuity).

  1. If \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) is positive semidefinite for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), then \(f\) is convex.
  2. If \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) is positive definite for every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), then \(f\) is strictly convex (Definition 7).
ProofProof

Proof. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\). By Theorem 4 with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\), there is a \(\tau\in (0, 1)\) such that, with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}+ \tau(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\),

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) + {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) + \frac{1}{2}\,{(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}})\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) && \text{(}\href{#thm-taylor-mv}{\text{Theorem~4}}\text{)} \\ &\ge f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}) + {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}})\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}). && \text{(} \mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) \text{ is positive semidefinite)} \end{aligned} \]

This inequality is Equation 1, so \(f\) is convex by Theorem 9. Under the hypothesis of part 2 and with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}\), the quadratic-form term is positive, so the inequality is strict, and \(f\) is strictly convex by the last sentence of Theorem 9.

ExmExample 24 (Using the Hessian to show convexity)

Example 24 (Using the Hessian to show convexity)  

  • For a symmetric positive semidefinite \(\mathbf{A}\), \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) has Hessian \(2 \mathbf{A}\) (Hessian of a quadratic form), which is positive semidefinite because \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} (2 \mathbf{A}) \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} = 2\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} \ge 0\); so \(f\) is convex.

  • \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 2 x_1\) has Hessian \(2 \mathbf{I}_2\) (Example 10), which is positive definite, so \(f\) is strictly convex.

  • \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = e^{2x_1 + x_2} - x_1\) has Hessian \(e^{u} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) with \(u = 2x_1 + x_2\) (Example), and

    \[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} &= e^{u} (4 h_1^2 + 4 h_1 h_2 + h_2^2) \\ &= e^{u} (2 h_1 + h_2)^2 \\ &\ge 0, \end{aligned} \]

    so \(f\) is convex. The Hessian is not positive definite (\(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{h}}\vphantom{h}} = {(1, -2)}^{\top}\) gives \(0\)), so part 2 does not apply, and in fact \(f\) is not strictly convex: along \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= t\,{(1, -2)}^{\top}\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} u &= 2t - 2t \\ &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

    and \(f = 1 - t\) is linear in \(t\), so the convexity inequality holds with equality there.

ExmExample 25 (Strictly convex with a singular Hessian)

Example 25 (Strictly convex with a singular Hessian) Part 2 is sufficient but not necessary. \(f(x) = x^4\) has

\[ \begin{aligned} f''(0) &= 12 \cdot 0^2 \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

so \(\mathbf{H}_f(0) = [0]\) is singular and not positive definite. Yet \(f\) is strictly convex, by the strict form of Equation 1: for \(x \ne y\),

\[ \begin{aligned} x^4 - y^4 - 4 y^3 (x - y) &= (x - y)(x^3 + x^2 y + x y^2 + y^3) - 4 y^3 (x - y) && \text{(factor } x^4 - y^4 \text{)} \\ &= (x - y)(x^3 + x^2 y + x y^2 - 3 y^3) && \text{(collect the } y^3 \text{ terms)} \\ &= (x - y)^2 (x^2 + 2 x y + 3 y^2) && \text{(} (x - y)(x^2 + 2 x y + 3 y^2) = x^3 + x^2 y + x y^2 - 3 y^3 \text{)} \\ &= (x - y)^2 \mathopen{}\left((x + y)^2 + 2 y^2\right)\mathclose{}, && \text{(complete the square)} \end{aligned} \]

which is positive: \((x - y)^2 > 0\), and \((x + y)^2 + 2 y^2 = 0\) would need \(y = 0\) and then

\[ \begin{aligned} x &= 0 \\ &= y. \end{aligned} \]

Corollary 1 (Stationary points of a convex function are global minimizers) If \(f\) is as in Theorem 9 and convex, then every stationary point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) of \(f\) (Definition 1) is a global minimizer.

ProofProof

Proof. For every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &\ge f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) + {\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*)\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) && \text{(}\href{#eq-supporting-hyperplane}{\text{Equation~1}}\text{ with } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* \text{)} \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}}^{\top} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) && \text{(} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* \text{ is stationary)} \\ &= f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*). && \text{(} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = 0 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 26 (A convex function, and a non-convex one)

Example 26 (A convex function, and a non-convex one)  

  • \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 2 x_1\) is convex by Theorem 10, since its Hessian is \(2 \mathbf{I}_2\) (Example 10), so its stationary point \({(1, 0)}^{\top}\) (Example 2) is a global minimizer, as completing the square showed in Example 3.
  • Without convexity, a stationary point need not be a minimizer at all: \(x_1^2 - x_2^2\) has the stationary point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), which is not a minimizer (Example 3).
  • The corollary says nothing about whether a minimizer exists: \(e^{2x_1 + x_2} - x_1\) is convex (Example 24) but has no stationary point, and so no local minimizer (Example 3).

DefDefinition 12 (Feasible set)

Definition 12 (Feasible set) In a problem that looks for a best point among some allowed points of \(\mathbb{R}^p\), the set \(F \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p\) of allowed points is the feasible set, [9] and its points are feasible.

DefDefinition 13 (Constraints)

Definition 13 (Constraints) When the feasible set \(F\) (Definition 12) is the set of points that satisfy some equations or inequalities, those equations or inequalities are the constraints.

ExmExample 27 (A line as a feasible set)

Example 27 (A line as a feasible set) With the single constraint \(3 x_1 + 4 x_2 = 5\), the feasible set is the line \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^2 : 3 x_1 + 4 x_2 = 5\right\}\mathclose{}\). \({(1, 0.5)}^{\top}\) is feasible, since

\[ \begin{aligned} 3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 0.5 &= 3 + 2 \\ &= 5; \end{aligned} \]

\(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) is not feasible, since \(3 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 0 = 0 \ne 5\).

DefDefinition 14 (Objective function (objective))

Definition 14 (Objective function (objective)) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) and let \(F \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p\) be a feasible set (Definition 12). In the problem of minimizing \(f\) over \(F\), \(f\) is the objective function (or objective).

DefDefinition 15 (Minimizer over a feasible set)

Definition 15 (Minimizer over a feasible set) With \(f\) and \(F\) as in Definition 14, a minimizer of \(f\) over \(F\) is a feasible \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) with \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) \le f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) for every feasible \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\): a global minimizer of \(f\) with its domain restricted to \(F\).

DefDefinition 16 (Minimization problem)

Definition 16 (Minimization problem) With \(f\) and \(F\) as in Definition 14, the minimization problem [10] is to find a minimizer of \(f\) over \(F\) (Definition 15). When \(F\) is given by constraints (Definition 13), the problem is to minimize \(f\) subject to them.

ExmExample 28 (Minimizing over a line)

Example 28 (Minimizing over a line) In the problem of minimizing \(x_1^2 + x_2^2\) subject to \(3 x_1 + 4 x_2 = 5\), the objective function is \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2\), and the feasible set is the line of Example 27. The feasible point \({(1, 0.5)}^{\top}\) has

\[ \begin{aligned} f(1, 0.5) &= 1 + 0.25 \\ &= 1.25. \end{aligned} \]

\(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) minimizes \(f\) over all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\), with \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) = 0\), but it is not a minimizer over the feasible set, because it is not feasible.

Here the objective (Definition 14) is a quadratic \(\tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) with \(\mathbf{S}\) positive definite, and the constraints (Definition 13) are \(m\) linear equations \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\), where \(\mathbf{A}\) is a \(p \times m\) matrix and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \in \mathbb{R}^m\). The only stationary point of the objective alone (Definition 1) is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1}\) (its gradient is \(\mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), and \(\mathbf{S}\) is invertible), which may not satisfy the constraints. A Lagrange multiplier adds one unknown per constraint, and the minimizer over the feasible set (Definition 12) is then found among the stationary points of a new function of the extended set of unknowns.

DefDefinition 17 (Lagrangian)

Definition 17 (Lagrangian) For the problem of minimizing \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) over \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) subject to \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\), with \(\mathbf{A}\) a \(p \times m\) matrix and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \in \mathbb{R}^m\), the Lagrangian [11] is the function of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}\in \mathbb{R}^m\)

\[ L(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}}^{\top} \mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right)\mathclose{}. \]

DefDefinition 18 (Lagrange multipliers)

Definition 18 (Lagrange multipliers) In the Lagrangian \(L(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}})\) (Definition 17), the entries of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}\) are the Lagrange multipliers [11], one per constraint.

ExmExample 29 (The gradients of the Lagrangian of a quadratic)

Example 29 (The gradients of the Lagrangian of a quadratic) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) with \(\mathbf{S}\) symmetric and constant. By the transpose of a product, \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}}^{\top} {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} = {(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}})}^{\top}\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} L(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}) &= \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}}^{\top} {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} && \text{(distribute)} \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} + {(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}})}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}, && \text{(transpose of a product)} \end{aligned} \]

and, since \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}}^{\top} \mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right)\mathclose{}\) is \(1 \times 1\) and so equals its transpose,

\[ L(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} + {\mathopen{}\left({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}. \]

The partial derivatives of a sum are the sums of the partial derivatives, entry by entry. Using the first of these forms, derivative of a quadratic form for the first term, derivative of a linear combination for the second (\(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}\) is constant in \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\)), and constant with respect to a vector for the third,

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} L(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}) &= \tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{} + \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \mathopen{}\left({(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}})}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{} - \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} \mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(sum and constant multiple)} \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2} \mathopen{}\left(2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right)\mathclose{} + \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1} && \text{(the three derivatives)} \\ &= \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}. && \text{(simplify)} \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 11 (Minimizing a positive definite quadratic under linear constraints)

Theorem 11 (Minimizing a positive definite quadratic under linear constraints) Let \(\mathbf{S}\) be a \(p \times p\) positive definite matrix, \(\mathbf{A}\) a \(p \times m\) matrix with linearly independent columns, and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \in \mathbb{R}^m\). Then the \(m \times m\) matrix \(\mathbf{M} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathbf{A}\) is invertible, and \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) has exactly one minimizer over the \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) with \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\), namely

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* = \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathbf{A} \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}. \]

With \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}^* \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}-\mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\), the pair \((\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}^*)\) solves Equation 2, and the minimum value is \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top} \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\).

The hypotheses hold: \(2 \mathbf{I}_2\) is positive definite, and the single column \({(3, 4)}^{\top}\) is nonzero, so it is linearly independent. Also \(\mathbf{S}^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{I}_2\). Then, from the formulas of Theorem 11,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{M} &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\,\tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{I}_2\,\mathbf{A} \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2}\,(3^2 + 4^2) \\ &= \tfrac{25}{2}, \\ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* &= \tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{I}_2 \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{2}{25}\right)\mathclose{} (5) \\ &= \tfrac{5}{25} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.8 \end{bmatrix}, \\ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}^* &= -\mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{2}{25}\right)\mathclose{} (5) \\ &= -0.4, \\ f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*) &= \tfrac{1}{2} (5) \mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{2}{25}\right)\mathclose{} (5) \\ &= 1. \end{aligned} \]

Checks:

  • the constraint:

    \[ \begin{aligned} 3 (0.6) + 4 (0.8) &= 1.8 + 3.2 \\ &= 5; \end{aligned} \]

  • the first block of Equation 2:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* + \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}^* &= {(1.2, 1.6)}^{\top} + {(3, 4)}^{\top} (-0.4) \\ &= {(1.2, 1.6)}^{\top} - {(1.2, 1.6)}^{\top} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}; \end{aligned} \]

  • the value:

    \[ \begin{aligned} 0.6^2 + 0.8^2 &= 0.36 + 0.64 \\ &= 1. \end{aligned} \]

Geometrically, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) is the point of the line nearest the origin, at distance \(1\).

ExmExample 31 (The hypotheses are needed)

Example 31 (The hypotheses are needed)  

  • If \(\mathbf{S}\) is not positive definite there may be no minimizer: with \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\) and the constraint \(x_1 = 1\) (\(\mathbf{A} = {(1, 0)}^{\top}\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} = [1]\)), \(f(1, x_2) = \tfrac{1}{2} (1 - x_2^2)\) decreases without bound as \(x_2\) grows.

  • If the columns of \(\mathbf{A}\) are dependent, \(\mathbf{M}\) is not invertible: with \(\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{I}_2\) and \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{M} &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \end{aligned} \]

    whose columns are equal. So \(\mathbf{M} {(1, -1)}^{\top} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\), and \(\mathbf{M}\) is not invertible (a square matrix is invertible exactly when its null space is zero). The two constraints both read \(x_1 + x_2 = b_i\), so they have no solution at all when \(b_1 \ne b_2\). When \(b_1 = b_2\) a unique minimizer still exists,

    \[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= x_2 \\ &= b_1 / 2, \end{aligned} \]

    but the formula of Theorem 11 cannot produce it.

DefDefinition 19 (Sensitivity)

Definition 19 (Sensitivity) Suppose a minimization problem (Definition 16) depends on a vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \in \mathbb{R}^m\), and let \(f^*(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}})\) be its minimum value, as a function of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\). When the gradient \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}} f^*(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}})\) exists (gradient), it is the sensitivity of the minimum value to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\): by partial derivative, its entry \(i\) is the limit, as a change in \(b_i\) alone shrinks to \(0\), of the resulting change in the minimum value divided by the change in \(b_i\).

ExmExample 32 (The sensitivity of a one-point problem)

Example 32 (The sensitivity of a one-point problem) Minimize \(x^2\) subject to \(x = b\), for a number \(b\). The feasible set (Definition 12) is \(\mathopen{}\left\{b\right\}\mathclose{}\), so the minimum value is \(f^*(b) = b^2\), and its sensitivity to \(b\) is \(\frac{d }{d b} f^*(b) = 2b\). At \(b = 3\) the sensitivity is \(6\): moving \(b\) from \(3\) to \(3.1\) raises the minimum value from \(9\) to \(9.61\), an increase of \(0.61\), close to \(6 \times 0.1 = 0.6\).

Its sensitivity to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\) (Definition 19) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}} f^*(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}) &= \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} \\ &= -\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}^*. \end{aligned} \]

ProofProof

Proof. \(\mathbf{M}^{-1}\) is symmetric and does not depend on \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\), so derivative of a quadratic form applies with \(\mathbf{M}^{-1}\) in place of \(\mathbf{S}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}} f^*(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}) &= \tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}} \mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top} \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(constant multiple)} \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2} \mathopen{}\left(2 \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(derivative of a quadratic form)} \\ &= \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} && \text{(simplify)} \\ &= -\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}^*. && \text{(definition of } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\lambda}}\vphantom{\lambda}}^* \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 33 (Moving the line)

Example 33 (Moving the line) In Example 30 with a general right side \(b\) in place of \(5\), \(\mathbf{M}^{-1} = \tfrac{2}{25}\) and

DefDefinition 23 (Convergence of a sequence of vectors)

Definition 23 (Convergence of a sequence of vectors) A sequence \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)}, \ldots\) in \(\mathbb{R}^p\) converges to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* \in \mathbb{R}^p\) if the sequence of distances \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{}\) converges to \(0\). ExmExample 34 (Halving a vector)

Example 34 (Halving a vector) The iterative method \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t+1)} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}\) from \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} = {(4, 2)}^{\top}\) has iterates \({(4, 2)}^{\top}, {(2, 1)}^{\top}, {(1, 0.5)}^{\top}, \ldots\), that is, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} = 2^{-t}\,{(4, 2)}^{\top}\). With \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^* = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), the error of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left\lVert 2^{-t}\,{(4, 2)}^{\top} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\rVert\mathclose{} &= 2^{-t}\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert{(4, 2)}^{\top}\right\rVert\mathclose{} && \text{(norm of a multiple)} \\ &= 2^{-t} \sqrt{20}, && \text{(} 4^2 + 2^2 = 20 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

which converges to \(0\), so the iterates converge to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\).

DefDefinition 24 (Newton’s method for minimization)

Definition 24 (Newton’s method for minimization) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) have first and second partial derivatives, and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)}\) be a starting point. \[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t+1)} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} - \mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)})\right]\mathclose{}^{-1} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}). \]

DefDefinition 25 (Newton step)

Definition 25 (Newton step) In Newton’s method (Definition 24), the vector \(-\mathopen{}\left[\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)})\right]\mathclose{}^{-1} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)})\) is the Newton step at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}\).

ExmExample 35 (Newton’s method for a cubic)

Example 35 (Newton’s method for a cubic) Let \(f(x) = \tfrac{1}{3} x^3 - 4x\), so \(f'(x) = x^2 - 4\) and \(f''(x) = 2x\). For \(x^{(t)} \ne 0\), one Newton step gives

\[ \begin{aligned} x^{(t+1)} &= x^{(t)} - \frac{(x^{(t)})^2 - 4}{2 x^{(t)}} && \text{(Newton's method with } p = 1 \text{)} \\ &= \frac{2 (x^{(t)})^2 - (x^{(t)})^2 + 4}{2 x^{(t)}} && \text{(common denominator)} \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \mathopen{}\left(x^{(t)} + \frac{4}{x^{(t)}}\right)\mathclose{}. && \text{(simplify)} \end{aligned} \]

From \(x^{(0)} = 2.5\):

  • \[ \begin{aligned} x^{(1)} &= \tfrac{1}{2} (2.5 + 1.6) \\ &= 2.05; \end{aligned} \]

  • \[ \begin{aligned} x^{(2)} &= \tfrac{1}{2} (2.05 + 1.951219\ldots) \\ &= 2.000610 \end{aligned} \]

    (to six decimals);

  • \(x^{(3)} = 2.0000001\) (to seven decimals).

The iterates (Definition 21) approach \(2\), the local minimizer of \(f\) (a stationary point, since \(f'(2) = 0\), with \(f''(2) = 4 > 0\); Theorem 5).

Definition 26 (Quadratic model (second-order Taylor polynomial)) Let \(f\) be as in Definition 24 and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) be a point, with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) and \(\mathbf{H} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\). The quadratic model of \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the function of a step \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\)

\[ q(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) + \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\delta}}\vphantom{\delta}}, \]

also called the second-order Taylor polynomial of \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\).

+ {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}}^{\top} \mathbf{H} ExmExample 37 (The Newton step at \(x = 2.5\))

Example 37 (The Newton step at \(x = 2.5\)) For the model \(q(\delta) = f(2.5) + 2.25\,\delta+ 2.5\,\delta^2\) of Example 36, \(H = [5]\) is positive definite, so by Theorem 12 its minimizer is

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta^* &= -H^{-1} g \\ &= -2.25 / 5 \\ &= -0.45. \end{aligned} \]

Setting the derivative \(2.25 + 5 \delta\) to \(0\) gives the same \(\delta^*\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} 2.5 - 0.45 &= 2.05 \\ &= x^{(1)} \end{aligned} \]

(Example 35).

DefDefinition 27 (Quadratic convergence)

Definition 27 (Quadratic convergence) A sequence \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)}, \ldots\) in \(\mathbb{R}^p\) that converges (Definition 23) to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\) converges quadratically [14] if there is a constant \(C\) with

\[ \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t+1)} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} \le C\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 \qquad \text{for every } t. \]

ExmExample 38 (Halving the error is not quadratic convergence)

Example 38 (Halving the error is not quadratic convergence) \(x^{(t)} = 2^{-t}\) converges to \(0\), but it does not converge quadratically: the bound would need \(2^{-(t+1)} \le C\,(2^{-t})^2 = C\,2^{-2t}\), that is, \(2^{t - 1} \le C\), which fails once \(t > 1 + \log_2 C\). Each step only halves the error.

ExmExample 39 (The error squares at each step)

Example 39 (The error squares at each step) For the iteration \(x^{(t+1)} = \tfrac{1}{2} (x^{(t)} + 4 / x^{(t)})\) of Example 35,

\[ \begin{aligned} x^{(t+1)} - 2 &= \frac{1}{2} \mathopen{}\left(x^{(t)} + \frac{4}{x^{(t)}}\right)\mathclose{} - 2 && \text{(the iteration)} \\ &= \frac{(x^{(t)})^2 + 4 - 4 x^{(t)}}{2 x^{(t)}} && \text{(common denominator)} \\ &= \frac{(x^{(t)} - 2)^2}{2 x^{(t)}}. && \text{(the numerator is a perfect square)} \end{aligned} \]

So near \(2\) the error is roughly squared and divided by \(4\) at each step: the errors are \(0.05\), then \(0.00061\), then \(0.000000093\), and \(0.05^2 / (2 \cdot 2.05) \approx 0.00061\). The iterates converge quadratically (Definition 27): \(C = \tfrac{1}{2}\) works for every \(x^{(t)} \ge 1\), since then \(\tfrac{1}{2 x^{(t)}} \le \tfrac{1}{2}\), and every iterate from \(2.5\) is at least \(2\), since the identity makes \(x^{(t+1)} - 2 \ge 0\) whenever \(x^{(t)} > 0\). Zhou [1] states such a bound for Newton’s method in general; it holds from starting points close enough to a minimizer where the Hessian is positive definite, when near that minimizer the Hessian changes by at most a constant times the distance moved, which these notes do not prove.

ExmExample 40 (Newton’s method can find a maximum)

Example 40 (Newton’s method can find a maximum) From \(x^{(0)} = -2.5\) the same iteration gives \(-2.05\), \(-2.000610\), \(-2.0000001\), approaching \(-2\). But \(-2\) is not a minimizer of \(f\): it is a stationary point of \(-f\), whose second derivative there is \(-f''(-2) = 4 > 0\), so it is a strict local minimizer of \(-f\) (Theorem 5), that is, \(f\) is strictly larger at \(-2\) than at all nearby points. Newton’s method looks only for a stationary point, and here the Hessian \(f''(x) = 2x\) is negative along the way, so the quadratic model has no minimizer (with \(H < 0\), \(q(\delta) = f + g\,\delta+ \tfrac{1}{2} H \delta^2\) decreases without bound) and Theorem 12 does not apply: the positive definite hypothesis there is needed.

LemTheorem 13 (Newton’s method minimizes a quadratic in one step)

Theorem 13 (Newton’s method minimizes a quadratic in one step) Let \(\mathbf{S}\) be a \(p \times p\) positive definite matrix, \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\), and \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\). From any \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)}\), Newton’s method (Definition 24) gives \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)} = \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\), the unique minimizer of \(f\).

ProofProof

Proof. Partial derivatives of a sum or of a constant multiple are the sum or multiple of the partial derivatives, so by derivative of a quadratic form for \(\tfrac{1}{2}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and derivative of a linear combination for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \tfrac{1}{2} (2 \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} \\ &= \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}; \end{aligned} \]

by Hessian of a quadratic form with \(\tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{S}\) in place of \(\mathbf{S}\), and because the gradient \(-\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\) of the linear term is constant (constant with respect to a vector), \(\mathbf{H}_f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \mathbf{S}\), which is invertible (positive definite inverse). So

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)} &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} - \mathbf{S}^{-1} (\mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}) && \text{(definition of Newton's method)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} - \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} + \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} && \text{(distribute)} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} + \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} && \text{(} \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{I}_p \text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}. && \text{(cancel)} \end{aligned} \]

The gradient at \(\mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\) is \(\mathbf{S} \mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1}\), and \(f\) is strictly convex (Theorem 10, part 2), so \(\mathbf{S}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}}\) is a global minimizer (Corollary 1). It is the only one: a strictly convex function has at most one global minimizer, since the midpoint of two distinct global minimizers, which have equal values, would have a strictly smaller value (Definition 7 with \(t = \tfrac{1}{2}\)).

ExmExample 41 (One step to the minimizer)

Example 41 (One step to the minimizer) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \tfrac{1}{2} (x_1^2 + 3 x_2^2)\), so \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{c}}\vphantom{c}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\). From \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} = {(3, 1)}^{\top}\) the gradient is \(\mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} = {(3, 3)}^{\top}\), and \(\mathbf{S}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/3 \end{bmatrix}\), so the Newton step is \(-\mathbf{S}^{-1} {(3, 3)}^{\top} = -{(3, 1)}^{\top}\) and

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)} &= {(3, 1)}^{\top} - {(3, 1)}^{\top} \\ &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}, \end{aligned} \]

the minimizer.

DefDefinition 28 (Gradient descent)

Definition 28 (Gradient descent) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) have first partial derivatives, let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)}\) be a starting point, and let \(s^{(0)}, s^{(1)}, \ldots\) be positive step lengths. Gradient descent [15] computes, for \(t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\),

\[ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t+1)} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} - s^{(t)}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}). \]

ExmExample 42 (Fixed steps on a parabola)

Example 42 (Fixed steps on a parabola) For \(f(x) = x^2\), \(f'(x) = 2x\), so gradient descent with fixed step length \(s\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} x^{(t+1)} &= x^{(t)} - 2 s\,x^{(t)} \\ &= (1 - 2s)\,x^{(t)}, \end{aligned} \]

and \(x^{(t)} = (1 - 2s)^t x^{(0)}\).

  • With \(s = 0.25\), \(x^{(t)} = 0.5^t x^{(0)}\), which tends to the minimizer \(0\).
  • With \(s = 0.5\), the first step lands on \(0\), since \(1 - 2s = 0\).
  • With \(s = 1.5\), \(x^{(t)} = (-2)^t x^{(0)}\), which moves away from \(0\) for any \(x^{(0)} \ne 0\): too long a step can make gradient descent fail.
RemarkRemark 4 (Gradient descent as Newton’s method with a simpler Hessian)

Remark 4 (Gradient descent as Newton’s method with a simpler Hessian). Gradient descent (Definition 28) is Newton’s method (Definition 24) with the Hessian replaced by \(\tfrac{1}{s^{(t)}} \mathbf{I}_p\), so it needs no second derivatives.

DefDefinition 29 (Line search)

Definition 29 (Line search) In gradient descent (Definition 28), a line search [16] is a rule that chooses the step length \(s^{(t)}\) by examining the values of \(f\) along the line \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} - s\,\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}) : s > 0\right\}\mathclose{}\).

DefDefinition 30 (Exact line search)

Definition 30 (Exact line search) Exact line search is the line search (Definition 29) that chooses \(s^{(t)}\) to minimize \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} - s\,\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}))\) over \(s > 0\).

ExmExample 43 (Exact line search on a parabola)

Example 43 (Exact line search on a parabola) For \(f(x) = x^2\) and \(x^{(t)} = 2\), the derivative is \(f'(2) = 4\), so the line is \(\mathopen{}\left\{2 - 4s : s > 0\right\}\mathclose{}\) and the values along it are

\[ \begin{aligned} \phi(s) &= f(2 - 4s) && \text{(the value at step length } s \text{)} \\&= (2 - 4s)^2 && (f(x) = x^2) \end{aligned} \]

\(\phi(s) \ge 0\) for every \(s\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \phi(0.5) &= (2 - 2)^2 \\ &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

so exact line search chooses \(s^{(t)} = 0.5\), which lands on the minimizer \(x^{(t+1)} = 0\) in one step.

DefDefinition 31 (Descent direction)

Definition 31 (Descent direction) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) have a gradient \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\). A direction \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}}\) (direction) is a descent direction for \(f\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) if \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} < 0\). When \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1}\), \(-\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\) is a descent direction, since \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot (-\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}) =-\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 < 0\).

ExmExample 44 (Descent directions for \(x_1^2 + x_2^2\) at \({(1, 0)}^{\top}\))

Example 44 (Descent directions for \(x_1^2 + x_2^2\) at \({(1, 0)}^{\top}\)) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = x_1^2 + x_2^2\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= {(1, 0)}^{\top}\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} &= {(2 x_1,\ 2 x_2)}^{\top} \\ &= {(2, 0)}^{\top}. \end{aligned} \]

  • \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} = {(-1, 1)}^{\top}\) is a descent direction:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} &= -2 + 0 \\ &= -2 \\ &< 0. \end{aligned} \]

  • \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} = {(0, 1)}^{\top}\) is not: \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{d}}\vphantom{d}} = 0\).

  • \(-\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} = {(-2, 0)}^{\top}\) is: \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot (-\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}) = -4 < 0\).

LemTheorem 14 (A short enough step downhill decreases the function)

Theorem 14 (A short enough step downhill decreases the function) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) have first partial derivatives that are continuous on \(\mathbb{R}^p\) (continuity), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\) be a point with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1}\). Then there is an \(\bar{s} > 0\) with \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- s \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}) < f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) for every \(s \in (0, \bar{s})\): a short enough step in the descent direction \(-\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\) (Definition 31) decreases \(f\).

ProofProof

Proof. Let \(h(s) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- s \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}})\). The inner function \(s \mapsto \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- s \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\) has derivative \(-{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}}^{\top}\) (vector-valued derivative), so by the vector chain rule (vector chain rule)

\[ \begin{aligned} h'(s) &= -{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}}^{\top}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- s \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}), && \text{(chain rule)} \\ h'(0) &= -{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}}^{\top}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(set } s = 0 \text{)} \\ &= -\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} && \text{(definition of } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \text{)} \\ &= -\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(squared length)} \\ &< 0. && \text{(} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{p \times 1} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Since \(h'(0) = \lim_{s \to 0} (h(s) - h(0)) / s\) is negative, taking \(\varepsilon= \mathopen{}\left|h'(0)\right|\mathclose{}\) in the definition of the limit gives an \(\bar{s} > 0\) such that \((h(s) - h(0)) / s < h'(0) + \mathopen{}\left|h'(0)\right|\mathclose{} = 0\) for \(0 < s < \bar{s}\). Multiplying by \(s > 0\), \(h(s) < h(0)\), that is, \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- s \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}) < f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\).

ExmExample 45 (How short is short enough)

Example 45 (How short is short enough) In Example 42, \(f(x) = x^2\) at \(x \ne 0\) has \(g = 2x\), and \(f(x - 2 s x) = (1 - 2s)^2 x^2 < x^2\) exactly when \(\mathopen{}\left|1 - 2s\right|\mathclose{} < 1\), that is, when \(0 < s < 1\). So Theorem 14 holds with \(\bar{s} = 1\), and the step \(s = 1.5\) of that example was too long.

ExmExample 46 (Gradient descent zigzags)

Example 46 (Gradient descent zigzags) Let \(f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \tfrac{1}{2} (x_1^2 + b\,x_2^2)\) with \(b > 0\), so \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = {(x_1,\ b x_2)}^{\top}\) and one gradient step of length \(s\) from \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) gives \({((1 - s) x_1,\ (1 - b s) x_2)}^{\top}\). For exact line search (Definition 30), let \(\phi(s) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}f\) at that point:

\[ \begin{aligned} \phi(s) &= \tfrac{1}{2} \mathopen{}\left((1 - s)^2 x_1^2 + b (1 - b s)^2 x_2^2\right)\mathclose{}, && \text{(definition of } f \text{)} \\ \phi'(s) &= -(1 - s) x_1^2 - b^2 (1 - b s) x_2^2 && \text{(chain rule, term by term)} \\ &= -x_1^2 + s\,x_1^2 - b^2 x_2^2 + b^3 s\,x_2^2 && \text{(expand the products)} \\ &= s\,(x_1^2 + b^3 x_2^2) - (x_1^2 + b^2 x_2^2). && \text{(collect the terms in } s \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\), \(x_1^2 + b^3 x_2^2 > 0\), so \(\phi'\) is increasing in \(s\) with a single zero, and \(\phi\) is minimized at \(s^* = \dfrac{x_1^2 + b^2 x_2^2}{x_1^2 + b^3 x_2^2}\). From \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)} = {(b, 1)}^{\top}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} s^{(0)} &= \frac{b^2 + b^2}{b^2 + b^3} && \text{(substitute } x_1 = b, x_2 = 1 \text{)} \\ &= \frac{2}{1 + b}, && \text{(cancel } b^2 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

and, with \(r \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\tfrac{b - 1}{b + 1}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} 1 - s^{(0)} &= \frac{1 + b - 2}{1 + b} \\ &= r, && \text{(common denominator)} \\ 1 - b\,s^{(0)} &= \frac{1 + b - 2b}{1 + b} \\ &= -r, && \text{(common denominator)} \\ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)} &= {\mathopen{}\left(r\,b,\ -r \cdot 1\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} \\ &= r\,{(b, -1)}^{\top}. && \text{(the gradient step from } {(b, 1)}^{\top} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

For \(b \ne 1\) the same holds at every step, by induction (when \(b = 1\), \(r = 0\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)}\) is already the minimizer): if \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} = {(c\,b,\ \pm c)}^{\top}\) for a number \(c \ne 0\), then \(s^*\), which depends only on \(x_1^2 = c^2 b^2\) and \(x_2^2 = c^2\), is again \(\tfrac{2}{1 + b}\) (the factor \(c^2\) cancels), and the step multiplies the first entry by \(r\) and the second by \(-r\). So

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)} &= {\mathopen{}\left(b\,r^t,\ (-r)^t\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top}, \\ f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}) &= \tfrac{1}{2} (b^2 + b)\,r^{2t} \\ &= r^{2t} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(0)}). \end{aligned} \]

For \(b \ne 1\), exactly one entry changes sign at every step: the second when \(b > 1\) (then \(r > 0\)), and the first when \(b < 1\) (then \(r < 0\)). So the iterates zigzag toward the minimizer \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{2 \times 1}\).

  • With \(b = 3\), \(r = \tfrac{1}{2}\): \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(1)} = {(1.5, -0.5)}^{\top}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(2)} = {(0.75, 0.25)}^{\top}\), and \(f\) falls from \(6\) to \(1.5\) to \(0.375\), a quarter at each step.
  • With \(b = 1\), \(r = 0\) and one step reaches the minimizer.
  • With \(b = 0.01\), \(r^2 = (0.99 / 1.01)^2 \approx 0.96\), so \(f\) shrinks by only about \(4\%\) per step. Newton’s method reaches the minimizer of any such \(f\) in one step (Theorem 13).
DefDefinition 32 (Armijo condition)

Definition 32 (Armijo condition) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) have first partial derivatives, let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p\), and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}} f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\). A step length \(s > 0\) satisfies the Armijo condition [17] with constant \(c \in (0, 1)\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) if

\[ f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- s \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}) \le f(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) - c\, s\, \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2. \]

ExmExample 47 (The Armijo condition on a parabola)

Example 47 (The Armijo condition on a parabola) Let \(f(x) = x^2\) and \(x = 2\), so \(g = f'(2) = 4\) and \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert g\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 = 16\). With \(c = 1/4\), the condition on a step length \(s > 0\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} (2 - 4s)^2 &\le 4 - 4s && \text{(} f(2 - 4s) \le f(2) - c\, s\, \mathopen{}\left\lVert g\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 \text{)} \\ 4 - 16 s + 16 s^2 &\le 4 - 4s && \text{(expand the square)} \\ 16 s^2 &\le 12 s && \text{(add } 16 s - 4 \text{ to both sides)} \\ s &\le 3/4. && \text{(divide by } 16 s > 0 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

So \(s = 1/2\) satisfies the condition: \(f(0) = 0 \le 4 - 2 = 2\). And \(s = 1\) does not: \(f(-2) = 4 > 4 - 4 = 0\).

DefDefinition 33 (Backtracking line search)

Definition 33 (Backtracking line search) Let \(s_0 > 0\) be an initial step length, \(\beta \in (0, 1)\) a shrink factor, and \(c \in (0, 1)\). Backtracking line search [17] is the line search that chooses \(s^{(t)}\) to be the largest of \(s_0, \beta s_0, \beta^2 s_0, \ldots\) that satisfies the Armijo condition with constant \(c\) at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^{(t)}\).

ExmExample 48 (Backtracking on a parabola)

Example 48 (Backtracking on a parabola) For \(f(x) = x^2\) at \(x = 2\) with \(c = 1/4\), the Armijo condition holds exactly when \(s \le 3/4\) (Example 47). With \(s_0 = 1\) and \(\beta = 1/2\), the trial step lengths are \(1, 1/2, 1/4, \ldots\). The first, \(s = 1\), exceeds \(3/4\) and is rejected. The second, \(s = 1/2\), is at most \(3/4\) and is accepted. So backtracking chooses \(s^{(0)} = 1/2\), after two evaluations of \(f\).
