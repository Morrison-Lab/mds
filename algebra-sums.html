Convexity, Infimum and Sums

Published

Last modified: 2026-10-28 17:04:35 (PDT)

1 Convex functions

ExrExercise 1 (A value compared with an average of values)

Exercise 1 (A value compared with an average of values) Let \(f(x) = (x - 2)^2\), and take the points \(x = 0\) and \(y = 4\) with \(t = \tfrac{1}{2}\).

  1. Compute \(f(t x + (1 - t) y)\).
  2. Compute \(t f(x) + (1 - t) f(y)\).
  3. Is \(f(t x + (1 - t) y) \le t f(x) + (1 - t) f(y)\)?
SolutionSolution 1

Solution 1.

  1. With \(t = \tfrac{1}{2}\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} t x + (1 - t) y &= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 4 \\ &= 2, \end{aligned} \]

    so

    \[ \begin{aligned} f(2) &= (2 - 2)^2 \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

  2. \[ \begin{aligned} f(0) &= (0 - 2)^2 \\ &= 4 \end{aligned} \]

    and

    \[ \begin{aligned} f(4) &= (4 - 2)^2 \\ &= 4, \end{aligned} \]

    so \(\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 4 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 4 = 4\).

  3. Yes: \(0 \le 4\). The value of \(f\) at the midpoint of \(0\) and \(4\) is below the average of its values at the two endpoints.

DefDefinition 1 (Line segment)

Definition 1 (Line segment) Let \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}^p\). The line segment from \(x\) to \(y\) [1] is the set of points \[ \mathopen{}\left\{t x + (1 - t) y : t \in [0, 1]\right\}\mathclose{}. \] The value \(t = 1\) gives \(x\), the value \(t = 0\) gives \(y\), and \(t = \tfrac{1}{2}\) gives the midpoint \(\tfrac{1}{2}(x + y)\).

ExmExample 1 (A line segment in \(\mathbb{R}\))

Example 1 (A line segment in \(\mathbb{R}\)) For \(p = 1\), \(x = 0\) and \(y = 4\), the line segment from \(x\) to \(y\) is the interval \([0, 4]\). The value \(t = \tfrac{1}{4}\) gives the point \(\tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 0 + \tfrac{3}{4} \cdot 4 = 3\).

DefDefinition 2 (Chord)

Definition 2 (Chord) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) be a function, and let \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}^p\). The chord of \(f\) from \(x\) to \(y\) [2] is the line segment (Definition 1) in \(\mathbb{R}^{p+1}\) from the point \((x, f(x))\) to the point \((y, f(y))\) of the graph of \(f\): \[ \mathopen{}\left\{\mathopen{}\left(t x + (1 - t) y,\ t f(x) + (1 - t) f(y)\right)\mathclose{} : t \in [0, 1]\right\}\mathclose{}. \] Above the point \(t x + (1 - t) y\), the chord has height \(t f(x) + (1 - t) f(y)\).

ExmExample 2 (A chord of \(x^2\))

Example 2 (A chord of \(x^2\)) For \(f(x) = x^2\), \(x = -1\) and \(y = 3\), the chord runs from \((-1, f(-1)) = (-1, 1)\) to \((3, f(3)) = (3, 9)\). With \(t = \tfrac{1}{2}\), it passes through \(\mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot (-1) + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 3,\ \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 9\right)\mathclose{} = (1, 5)\), so above the point \(1\) it has height \(5\).

DefDefinition 3 (Convex function)

Definition 3 (Convex function) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) be a function. \(f\) is convex [3] if \[ f(t x + (1 - t) y) \le t f(x) + (1 - t) f(y) \] for all \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}^p\) and all \(t \in [0, 1]\).

RemarkRemark 1 (Chords lie on or above the graph)

Remark 1 (Chords lie on or above the graph). The point \(t x + (1 - t) y\) lies on the line segment from \(x\) to \(y\), and the right-hand side is the height above that point of the chord of \(f\) from \(x\) to \(y\). So \(f\) is convex when every chord lies on or above the graph.

For example, take \(f(x) = x^2\), \(x = -1\), \(y = 3\), and \(t = \tfrac{1}{2}\). The point is \(\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot (-1) + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 3 = 1\), where the graph has height \(f(1) = 1\) and the chord has height

\[ \begin{aligned} \tfrac{1}{2} f(-1) + \tfrac{1}{2} f(3) &= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 9 \\ &= 5. \end{aligned} \]

The chord is above the graph: \(1 \le 5\).

ExmExample 3 (A function that is not convex)

Example 3 (A function that is not convex) The function in Exercise 2 in Equalities, Inequalities and Minimizers, \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x\), is not convex. Take \(x = -2\), \(y = 0\), \(t = \tfrac{1}{2}\). The point is \(\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot (-2) + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0 = -1\), where the graph has height

\[ \begin{aligned} f(-1) &= -1 + 3 \\ &= 2, \end{aligned} \]

but the chord has height

\[ \begin{aligned} \tfrac{1}{2} f(-2) + \tfrac{1}{2} f(0) &= \tfrac{1}{2} (-8 + 6) + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0 \\ &= -1, \end{aligned} \]

and \(2 \le -1\) is false.

ExmExample 4 (A convex and a non-convex function)

Example 4 (A convex and a non-convex function) \(f(x) = (x - 2)^2\) is convex (Definition 3): by expanding the square, \(f(t x + (1 - t) y) - t f(x) - (1 - t) f(y) = -t (1 - t) (x - y)^2 \le 0\). Its local minimizer \(x^* = 2\) is also a global minimizer, since

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &= (x - 2)^2 \\ &\ge 0 \\ &= f(2) \end{aligned} \]

for every \(x\).

Without convexity, a local minimizer need not be global: \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x\) is not convex (Example 3), and it has a local minimizer at \(x^* = 1\) that is not global (Exercise 2 in Equalities, Inequalities and Minimizers).

LemTheorem 1 (Local minimizers of convex functions are global)

Theorem 1 (Local minimizers of convex functions are global) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) be a convex function. Every local minimizer of \(f\) is a global minimizer of \(f\) over \(\mathbb{R}^p\).

ProofProof

Proof. Let \(x^*\) be a local minimizer of \(f\), so there is a \(\delta> 0\) with \(f(x^*) \le f(x)\) whenever \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert x - x^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\) (Definition 20 in Equalities, Inequalities and Minimizers). Suppose \(x^*\) is not a global minimizer (Definition 19 in Equalities, Inequalities and Minimizers). Then some \(y \in \mathbb{R}^p\) has \(f(y) < f(x^*)\), and in particular \(y \ne x^*\).

Let \(t = \min{\mathopen{}\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{\delta}{2 \mathopen{}\left\lVert y - x^*\right\rVert\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}}\), so \(t \in (0, 1)\), and let \(z = t y + (1 - t) x^*\). Then \(z - x^* = t (y - x^*)\), so \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert z - x^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} = t \mathopen{}\left\lVert y - x^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} \le \delta/ 2 < \delta\).

By convexity, \[ f(z) \le t f(y) + (1 - t) f(x^*) < t f(x^*) + (1 - t) f(x^*) = f(x^*), \] where the strict inequality uses \(t > 0\) and \(f(y) < f(x^*)\). So \(f(z) < f(x^*)\) with \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert z - x^*\right\rVert\mathclose{} < \delta\), which contradicts \(x^*\) being a local minimizer. Hence \(x^*\) is a global minimizer.

LemTheorem 2 (Jensen’s inequality for a weighted average)

Theorem 2 (Jensen’s inequality for a weighted average) Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}\) be a convex function [4]. Let \(x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}^p\), and let \(w_1, \ldots, w_n \ge 0\) be weights with \(\sum_{i=1}^nw_i = 1\). Then \[ f\mathopen{}\left(\sum_{i=1}^nw_i x_i\right)\mathclose{} \le \sum_{i=1}^nw_i f(x_i). \]

ProofProof

Proof. The proof is by induction on \(n\).

For \(n = 1\) the weight is \(w_1 = 1\), and both sides equal \(f(x_1)\).

Now let \(n \ge 2\), and assume the inequality holds for \(n - 1\) points. If \(w_n = 1\), the other weights are all \(0\), and both sides equal \(f(x_n)\). Otherwise let

\[ \begin{aligned} s &= 1 - w_n \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} w_i \\ &> 0 \end{aligned} \]

and \[ y = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{w_i}{s} x_i. \] The weights \(w_i / s\) are non-negative and sum to \(1\), so the induction assumption gives \[ f(y) \le \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{w_i}{s} f(x_i). \] Since \(\sum_{i=1}^nw_i x_i = s y + (1 - s) x_n\) and \(s \in (0, 1]\), the definition of a convex function (Definition 3) gives \[ f\mathopen{}\left(\sum_{i=1}^nw_i x_i\right)\mathclose{} = f(s y + (1 - s) x_n) \le s f(y) + (1 - s) f(x_n). \] Substituting the bound on \(f(y)\), and using \(1 - s = w_n\), \[ s f(y) + (1 - s) f(x_n) \le \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} w_i f(x_i) + w_n f(x_n) = \sum_{i=1}^nw_i f(x_i). \]

ExmExample 5 (The average of the squares is at least the square of the average)

Example 5 (The average of the squares is at least the square of the average) Take \(f(x) = x^2\). It is convex by Definition 3: for \(t \in [0, 1]\), \[ t x^2 + (1 - t) y^2 - \mathopen{}\left(t x + (1 - t) y\right)\mathclose{}^2 = t (1 - t) (x - y)^2 \ge 0. \] Take the three points \(x_1 = 1\), \(x_2 = 2\), \(x_3 = 6\), and the equal weights

\[ \begin{aligned} w_1 &= w_2 \\ &= w_3 \\ &= \tfrac{1}{3}. \end{aligned} \]

The weighted average of the points is their ordinary average, \(\tfrac{1}{3}(1 + 2 + 6) = 3\), so the left side of Theorem 2 is \(f(3) = 9\). The right side is the average of the squares, \(\tfrac{1}{3}(1 + 4 + 36) = \tfrac{41}{3}\). The inequality holds: \(9 \le \tfrac{41}{3}\).

The gap is \(\tfrac{41}{3} - 9 = \tfrac{14}{3}\). That is the average squared distance of the points from their average, \(\tfrac{1}{3}\mathopen{}\left((1-3)^2 + (2-3)^2 + (6-3)^2\right)\mathclose{} = \tfrac{14}{3}\), which statistics calls the variance of the three points (about their mean, defined later). For \(f(x) = x^2\), Jensen’s inequality says this average squared distance is never negative.

RemarkRemark 2 (The inequality reverses for concave functions)

Remark 2 (The inequality reverses for concave functions). If \(f\) is concave, then \(-f\) is convex, and applying Theorem 2 to \(-f\) reverses the inequality: \(f\mathopen{}\left(\sum_i w_i x_i\right)\mathclose{} \ge \sum_i w_i f(x_i)\). For \(f(x) = -x^2\) and the points in Example 5, \(f(3) = -9 \ge -\tfrac{41}{3}\).

2 Infimum and supremum

DefDefinition 4 (Upper bound)

Definition 4 (Upper bound) Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\). A real number \(u\) is an upper bound for \(A\) [5] if \(a \le u\) for all \(a \in A\).

DefDefinition 5 (Lower bound)

Definition 5 (Lower bound) Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\). A real number \(\ell\) is a lower bound for \(A\) [5] if \(\ell\le a\) for all \(a \in A\).

DefDefinition 6 (Bounded above)

Definition 6 (Bounded above) A set \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) is bounded above if it has an upper bound (Definition 4). A real-valued function is bounded above if its image is.

DefDefinition 7 (Bounded below)

Definition 7 (Bounded below) A set \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) is bounded below if it has a lower bound (Definition 5). A real-valued function is bounded below if its image is.

DefDefinition 8 (Bounded)

Definition 8 (Bounded) A set \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) is bounded if it is both bounded above (Definition 6) and bounded below (Definition 7). A real-valued function is bounded if its image is.

ExmExample 6 (Bounded and unbounded sets)

Example 6 (Bounded and unbounded sets)  

  • For \(A = (0, 1]\), \(1\), \(2\), and \(100\) are upper bounds, and \(0\) and \(-5\) are lower bounds, so \(A\) is bounded. \(0.5\) is not an upper bound, because \(0.6 \in A\) and \(0.6 > 0.5\).
  • The natural numbers \(\mathbb{N}= \mathopen{}\left\{1, 2, 3, \ldots\right\}\mathclose{}\) are bounded below, by \(1\), but not bounded above: for any real number \(u\), some natural number \(n\) satisfies \(n > u\).
LemTheorem 3 (Completeness of the real numbers)

Theorem 3 (Completeness of the real numbers) Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) be nonempty [6].

  • If \(A\) is bounded above, the set of upper bounds of \(A\) has a minimum.
  • If \(A\) is bounded below, the set of lower bounds of \(A\) has a maximum.
ProofProof

Proof. The first statement is the least-upper-bound property of \(\mathbb{R}\) [7], Definition 1.8, p. 4, and Theorem 1.19, p. 8. For the second, let \(-A = \mathopen{}\left\{-a : a \in A\right\}\mathclose{}\). Negating both sides of an inequality reverses it (Theorem 4 in Equalities, Inequalities and Minimizers), so \(\ell\) is a lower bound of \(A\) exactly when \(-\ell\) is an upper bound of \(-A\). The set \(-A\) is nonempty and bounded above, so by the first statement its upper bounds have a minimum \(u\), and then \(-u\) is the largest lower bound of \(A\).

ExmExample 7 (Completeness for an interval)

Example 7 (Completeness for an interval) For \(A = (1, 2]\), the lower bounds of \(A\) are the numbers \(\ell\le 1\), and the largest of them is \(1\). The upper bounds of \(A\) are the numbers \(u\ge 2\), and the smallest of them is \(2\).

DefDefinition 9 (Infimum, greatest lower bound)

Definition 9 (Infimum, greatest lower bound) Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) be nonempty and bounded below (Definition 7). The infimum of \(A\) [8], written \(\inf A\), is the greatest real number \(t\) satisfying \(t \le a\) for all \(a \in A\):

\[\inf A \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\max\mathopen{}\left\{t \in \mathbb{R}: \forall a \in A,\ t \le a\right\}\mathclose{}\]

If \(A\) is nonempty but not bounded below, we write \(\inf A = -\infty\) by convention.

RemarkRemark 3 (Existence of the infimum, and when it is a minimum)

Remark 3 (Existence of the infimum, and when it is a minimum). The maximum in Definition 9 always exists, by the completeness of the real numbers (Theorem 3). For example, for \(A = (1, 2]\), the numbers \(t\) with \(t \le a\) for all \(a \in A\) are those with \(t \le 1\), and the largest of them is \(1\), so \(\inf A = 1\).

If the infimum belongs to \(A\), it equals the minimum: \(\inf A = \min A\). For example,

\[ \begin{aligned} \inf [1, 2] &= 1 \\ &= \min [1, 2]. \end{aligned} \]

For \(A = (1, 2]\), the infimum \(1\) is not in \(A\), and \(A\) has no minimum.

ExmExample 8 (Numerical examples of infimum)

Example 8 (Numerical examples of infimum)  

  • \(\inf\{1, 2, 3\} = 1\), since \(1\) is the smallest element.

  • \[ \begin{aligned} \inf(0.5, 1] &= 0.5 \\ &= \min[0.5, 1]: \end{aligned} \]

    for intervals open below, the infimum equals the minimum of the corresponding closed-below interval, even though \(0.5 \notin (0.5, 1]\). More generally,

    \[ \begin{aligned} \inf(c, b] &= \min[c, b] \\ &= c \end{aligned} \]

    for any \(c < b\).

  • \(\inf\{t \ge 0 : t > 0.5\} = 0.5\), even though \(0.5\) itself is not in the set.

  • \(\inf\{-1, -2, -3, \ldots\} = -\infty\), because no real number is less than or equal to every element of that set.

DefDefinition 10 (Supremum, least upper bound)

Definition 10 (Supremum, least upper bound) Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) be nonempty and bounded above (Definition 6). The supremum of \(A\) [8], written \(\sup A\), is the smallest real number \(t\) satisfying \(a \le t\) for all \(a \in A\):

\[\sup A \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\min\mathopen{}\left\{t \in \mathbb{R}: \forall a \in A,\ a \le t\right\}\mathclose{}\]

If \(A\) is nonempty but not bounded above, we write \(\sup A = +\infty\) by convention.

RemarkRemark 4 (Existence of the supremum, and when it is a maximum)

Remark 4 (Existence of the supremum, and when it is a maximum). The minimum in Definition 10 always exists, by the completeness of the real numbers (Theorem 3). For example, for \(A = [1, 2)\), the numbers \(t\) with \(a \le t\) for all \(a \in A\) are those with \(t \ge 2\), and the smallest of them is \(2\), so \(\sup A = 2\).

If the supremum belongs to \(A\), it equals the maximum: \(\sup A = \max A\). For example,

\[ \begin{aligned} \sup [1, 2] &= 2 \\ &= \max [1, 2]. \end{aligned} \]

For \(A = [1, 2)\), the supremum \(2\) is not in \(A\), and \(A\) has no maximum.

ExmExample 9 (Numerical examples of supremum)

Example 9 (Numerical examples of supremum)  

  • \(\sup\{1, 2, 3\} = 3\), since \(3\) is the largest element.
  • \(\sup\{t \ge 0 : t < 0.5\} = 0.5\), even though \(0.5\) itself is not in the set.
  • \(\sup\{1, 2, 3, \ldots\} = +\infty\), because no real number is greater than or equal to every element of that set.

3 Sums

DefDefinition 11 (Term of a sum)

Definition 11 (Term of a sum) In a sum \(a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n\), each of the numbers or expressions \(a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n\) being added is a term of the sum.

ExmExample 10 (Terms of a sum)

Example 10 (Terms of a sum)  

  • The sum \(3 + 5 + 9\) has three terms: \(3\), \(5\) and \(9\).
  • The sum \(x^2 - 3x + 7 = x^2 + (-3x) + 7\) has three terms: \(x^2\), \(-3x\) and \(7\). A subtracted expression counts as a term with a minus sign.
DefDefinition 12 (Identity element)

Definition 12 (Identity element) Let \(S\) be a set, such as the real numbers \(\mathbb{R}\), and let \(\star\) be an operation that combines two elements \(a\) and \(b\) of \(S\) into an element \(a \star b\) of \(S\), such as addition (\(a + b\)) or multiplication (\(a \times b\)) of real numbers. An element \(u\) of \(S\) is an identity element for \(\star\) [9] if \(a \star u = a\) and \(u \star a = a\) for every \(a \in S\).

ExmExample 11 (Identity elements for addition and multiplication)

Example 11 (Identity elements for addition and multiplication)  

  • \(0\) is the identity element for addition: for example, \(5 + 0 = 5\) and \(0 + 5 = 5\) (Theorem 4).
  • \(1\) is the identity element for multiplication: for example, \(5 \times 1 = 5\) and \(1 \times 5 = 5\) (Theorem 7).
  • \(0\) is not an identity element for subtraction: \(5 - 0 = 5\), but \(0 - 5 = -5 \ne 5\).
DefDefinition 13 (Commutative operation)

Definition 13 (Commutative operation) An operation \(\star\) on a set \(S\) (Definition 12) is commutative [10] if \(a \star b = b \star a\) for all \(a, b \in S\): the order of the two inputs does not matter. Some sources call a commutative operation symmetric, as in the theorem titles “Sums are symmetric” and “Products are symmetric”.

ExmExample 12 (A commutative operation, and one that is not)

Example 12 (A commutative operation, and one that is not)  

  • Addition is commutative: for example,

    \[ \begin{aligned} 2 + 5 &= 7 \\ &= 5 + 2. \end{aligned} \]

  • Subtraction is not commutative: \(5 - 3 = 2\), but \(3 - 5 = -2\).

DefDefinition 14 (Associative operation)

Definition 14 (Associative operation) An operation \(\star\) on a set \(S\) (Definition 12) is associative [11] if \((a \star b) \star c = a \star (b \star c)\) for all \(a, b, c \in S\): which pair is combined first does not matter.

ExmExample 13 (An associative operation, and one that is not)

Example 13 (An associative operation, and one that is not)  

  • Multiplication is associative: for example,

    \[ \begin{aligned} (2 \times 3) \times 4 &= 6 \times 4 \\ &= 24 \end{aligned} \]

    and

    \[ \begin{aligned} 2 \times (3 \times 4) &= 2 \times 12 \\ &= 24. \end{aligned} \]

  • Subtraction is not associative:

    \[ \begin{aligned} (8 - 4) - 2 &= 4 - 2 \\ &= 2, \end{aligned} \]

    but

    \[ \begin{aligned} 8 - (4 - 2) &= 8 - 2 \\ &= 6. \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 4 (Adding zero changes nothing)

Theorem 4 (Adding zero changes nothing) \[a+0=a\]

LemTheorem 5 (Sums are symmetric)

Theorem 5 (Sums are symmetric) \[a+b = b+a\]

LemTheorem 6 (Sums are associative)

Theorem 6 (Sums are associative) When adding three numbers, it does not matter which pair you add first:

\[(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)\]

ExmExample 14 (Grouping a sum two ways)

Example 14 (Grouping a sum two ways) \[ \begin{aligned} (2 + 3) + 4 &= 5 + 4 \\ &= 9, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} 2 + (3 + 4) &= 2 + 7 \\ &= 9. \end{aligned} \]

6 Sums and products together

DefDefinition 15 (Distributes over)

Definition 15 (Distributes over) An operation \(\star\) on a set \(S\) distributes over an operation \(\diamond\) on \(S\) if

\[a \star (b \diamond c) = (a \star b) \diamond (a \star c)\]

for all \(a, b, c \in S\).

ExmExample 15 (Addition does not distribute over multiplication)

Example 15 (Addition does not distribute over multiplication) \[ \begin{aligned} 2 + (3 \times 4) &= 2 + 12 \\ &= 14, \end{aligned} \]

but

\[ \begin{aligned} (2 + 3) \times (2 + 4) &= 5 \times 6 \\ &= 30. \end{aligned} \]

So Definition 15 fails with \(\star = +\) and \(\diamond = \times\).

LemTheorem 11 (Multiplication is distributive)

Theorem 11 (Multiplication is distributive) \[a(b+c) = ab + ac\]

DefDefinition 16 (Distributive law)

Definition 16 (Distributive law) The distributive law [12] is the statement that multiplication distributes over addition (Definition 15): \(a \times (b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)\) (Theorem 11). We then say multiplication is distributive.

ExmExample 16 (Multiplication distributes over addition)

Example 16 (Multiplication distributes over addition) \[ \begin{aligned} 3 \times (4 + 5) &= 3 \times 9 \\ &= 27, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} (3 \times 4) + (3 \times 5) &= 12 + 15 \\ &= 27. \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 17 (Like terms)

Definition 17 (Like terms) Two terms of a sum are like terms [13] if they are the same product of variables, each to the same power, possibly multiplied by different constants: \(c_1 m\) and \(c_2 m\), where \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are constants and \(m\) is that product.

DefDefinition 18 (Collecting like terms)

Definition 18 (Collecting like terms) To collect like terms (Definition 17) is to replace their sum by one term, using the distributive law (Theorem 11): \(c_1 m + c_2 m = (c_1 + c_2) m\).

ExmExample 17 (Like and unlike terms)

Example 17 (Like and unlike terms)  

  • \(3ab\) and \(-5ab\) are like terms; collecting them gives

    \[ \begin{aligned} 3ab + (-5ab) &= (3 - 5) ab \\ &= -2ab. \end{aligned} \]

  • \(2x\) and \(2x^2\) are not like terms: \(x\) appears to different powers.

ExrExercise 2 (Expand a squared sum)

Exercise 2 (Expand a squared sum) Is \((3 + 4)^2\) equal to \(3^2 + 4^2\)? Then expand \((a + b)^2\) for any numbers \(a\) and \(b\), using only the distributive law and the rules above.

SolutionSolution 2

Solution 2. No:

\[ \begin{aligned} (3 + 4)^2 &= 7^2 \\ &= 49, \end{aligned} \]

while

\[ \begin{aligned} 3^2 + 4^2 &= 9 + 16 \\ &= 25. \end{aligned} \]

The difference, \(49 - 25 = 24\), is \(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4\).

To see where that extra term comes from, write the square as a product and apply the distributive law (Theorem 11) twice:

\[ \begin{aligned} (a + b)^2 &= (a + b)(a + b) && \text{(definition of a square)} \\ &= (a + b)\,a + (a + b)\,b && \text{(distributive law)} \\ &= (a^2 + ba) + (ab + b^2) && \text{(distributive law, twice)} \\ &= a^2 + ab + ab + b^2 && \text{(commutative and associative laws)} \\ &= a^2 + 2ab + b^2 && \text{(collect like terms, }\href{#def-collect-like-terms}{\text{Definition~18}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

The step “commutative and associative laws” uses Theorem 8 to write \(ba\) as \(ab\), and Theorem 6 to drop the parentheses.

LemTheorem 12 (Product of two sums (FOIL))

Theorem 12 (Product of two sums (FOIL)) For any numbers \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) and \(d\),

\[ (a + b)(c + d) = ac + ad + bc + bd. \]

The four products pair the First terms (\(ac\)), the Outer terms (\(ad\)), the Inner terms (\(bc\)) and the Last terms (\(bd\)), which gives this rule its name, FOIL.

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} (a + b)(c + d) &= (a + b)\,c + (a + b)\,d && \text{(distributive law, }\href{#thm-mult-distr}{\text{Theorem~11}}\text{)} \\ &= (ac + bc) + (ad + bd) && \text{(distributive law, twice)} \\ &= ac + ad + bc + bd && \text{(commutative and associative laws)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 18 (Checking FOIL)

Example 18 (Checking FOIL) With \(a = 1\), \(b = 2\), \(c = 3\) and \(d = 4\),

\[ \begin{aligned} (1 + 2)(3 + 4) &= 3 \cdot 7 \\ &= 21, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} 1 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 4 &= 3 + 4 + 6 + 8 \\ &= 21. \end{aligned} \]

Setting \(c = a\) and \(d = b\) gives \((a + b)^2 = a^2 + ab + ba + b^2\), which is the square of a sum once \(ab\) and \(ba\) are combined.

LemTheorem 13 (Square of a sum)

Theorem 13 (Square of a sum) For any numbers \(a\) and \(b\),

\[ (a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \]

ProofProof

Proof. By Solution 2.

RemarkRemark 5 (Square of a difference)

Remark 5 (Square of a difference). Replacing \(b\) by \(-b\) in Theorem 13 gives \((a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2\). For example, the square \((y - \hat y)^2\) of the difference between an observed value \(y\) and a prediction \(\hat y\) of it expands this way. With \(y = 5\) and \(\hat y= 3\),

\[ \begin{aligned} (5 - 3)^2 &= 2^2 \\ &= 4, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} 5^2 - 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 3 + 3^2 &= 25 - 30 + 9 \\ &= 4. \end{aligned} \]

7 Summation notation

ExrExercise 3 (Expand a sum)

Exercise 3 (Expand a sum) The expression \(\sum_{i=1}^{4} i^2\) is shorthand for a sum of four terms.

  1. Guess which four terms, and add them up.
  2. Write \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\mathopen{}\left(y_i - \hat y_i\right)\mathclose{}^2\) for \(N = 3\) without the \(\sum\) symbol.
SolutionSolution 3

Solution 3.

  1. Replace \(i\) by each of \(1, 2, 3, 4\) in turn, and add the results:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{4} i^2 &= 1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 + 4^2 \\ &= 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 \\ &= 30 \end{aligned} \]

  2. Replace \(i\) by each of \(1, 2, 3\) in turn inside the parentheses, add the three squares, and multiply the total by \(\frac{1}{3}\):

    \[ \frac{1}{3} \sum_{i=1}^{3} \mathopen{}\left(y_i - \hat y_i\right)\mathclose{}^2 = \frac{1}{3} \mathopen{}\left[ \mathopen{}\left(y_1 - \hat y_1\right)\mathclose{}^2 + \mathopen{}\left(y_2 - \hat y_2\right)\mathclose{}^2 + \mathopen{}\left(y_3 - \hat y_3\right)\mathclose{}^2 \right]\mathclose{} \]

DefDefinition 19 (Summation notation)

Definition 19 (Summation notation) Let \(m\) and \(n\) be integers with \(m \le n\), and let \(a_m, a_{m+1}, \ldots, a_n\) be numbers. The sum of \(a_m\) through \(a_n\) is

\[ \sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}a_m + a_{m+1} + \cdots + a_n \]

The numbers \(m\) and \(n\) are its lower and upper limits: the first and last values of the index.

DefDefinition 20 (Index of a sum)

Definition 20 (Index of a sum) The variable \(i\) in \(\sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i\) (Definition 19) is the index of the sum.

RemarkRemark 6 (The index is a placeholder)

Remark 6 (The index is a placeholder). The name of the index does not change the sum: \(\sum_{i=1}^na_i\) and \(\sum_{j=1}^na_j\) are the same number. For example,

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{3} i &= 1 + 2 + 3 \\ &= 6 \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{j=1}^{3} j &= 1 + 2 + 3 \\ &= 6. \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 21 (Empty sum)

Definition 21 (Empty sum) When the upper limit is less than the lower limit (\(n < m\)), the sum \(\sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i\) has no terms. By convention, such an empty sum equals \(0\). For example, \(\sum_{i=1}^{0} a_i = 0\).

ExrExercise 4 (Rearrange a sum)

Exercise 4 (Rearrange a sum) Let \(c\) be a number, and let \(a_1, a_2, a_3\) and \(b_1, b_2, b_3\) be numbers. Using Definition 19 and the rules of algebra above, show that:

  1. \(\sum_{i=1}^{3} c\, a_i = c \sum_{i=1}^{3} a_i\);
  2. \(\sum_{i=1}^{3} \mathopen{}\left(a_i + b_i\right)\mathclose{} = \sum_{i=1}^{3} a_i + \sum_{i=1}^{3} b_i\).

Does either argument depend on there being exactly three terms?

SolutionSolution 4

Solution 4. Each step below applies Definition 19, the distributive law (Theorem 11), or the commutative and associative laws of addition (Theorem 5 and Theorem 6).

  1. Expand the sum, then factor out \(c\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{3} c\, a_i &= c\, a_1 + c\, a_2 + c\, a_3 && \text{(expand the sum)} \\ &= c \mathopen{}\left(a_1 + a_2 + a_3\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(distributive law)} \\ &= c \sum_{i=1}^{3} a_i && \text{(collect the sum)} \end{aligned} \]

  2. Expand the sum, then regroup the terms:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{3} \mathopen{}\left(a_i + b_i\right)\mathclose{} &= \mathopen{}\left(a_1 + b_1\right)\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left(a_2 + b_2\right)\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left(a_3 + b_3\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(expand the sum)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left(a_1 + a_2 + a_3\right)\mathclose{} + \mathopen{}\left(b_1 + b_2 + b_3\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(commutative and associative laws)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{3} a_i + \sum_{i=1}^{3} b_i && \text{(collect the sum)} \end{aligned} \]

Neither argument uses the number of terms: the same steps work for any lower and upper limits.

LemTheorem 14 (A constant factor comes out of a sum)

Theorem 14 (A constant factor comes out of a sum) For any number \(c\) and numbers \(a_m, \ldots, a_n\),

\[ \sum_{i=m}^{n} c\, a_i = c \sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i \]

ProofProof

Proof. By Solution 4, part 1.

LemTheorem 15 (A sum of sums splits)

Theorem 15 (A sum of sums splits) For any numbers \(a_m, \ldots, a_n\) and \(b_m, \ldots, b_n\),

\[ \sum_{i=m}^{n} \mathopen{}\left(a_i + b_i\right)\mathclose{} = \sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i + \sum_{i=m}^{n} b_i \]

ProofProof

Proof. By Solution 4, part 2.

ExrExercise 5 (Sum over a set)

Exercise 5 (Sum over a set) Let \(A = \mathopen{}\left\{-1, 2, 5\right\}\mathclose{}\) and let \(f(x) = x^2\). The expression \(\sum_{x \in A} f(x)\) means: add up \(f(x)\) for each element \(x\) of \(A\).

Write \(\sum_{x \in A} f(x)\) without the \(\sum\) symbol.

SolutionSolution 5

Solution 5. Replace \(x\) by each element of \(A\) in turn, and add:

\[\sum_{x \in A} f(x) = f(-1) + f(2) + f(5)\]

ExrExercise 6 (Value of a sum over a set)

Exercise 6 (Value of a sum over a set) In the setting of Exercise 5, find the value of \(\sum_{x \in A} f(x)\).

SolutionSolution 6

Solution 6. Evaluate each term and add:

\[ \begin{aligned} f(-1) + f(2) + f(5) &= (-1)^2 + 2^2 + 5^2 \\ &= 1 + 4 + 25 \\ &= 30 \end{aligned} \]

ExrExercise 7 (List the terms in a different order)

Exercise 7 (List the terms in a different order) In the setting of Exercise 5, list the elements of \(A\) in a different order, and write the term for each element in that order.

SolutionSolution 7

Solution 7. In the order \(5, -1, 2\), the terms are \(25\), \(1\) and \(4\).

ExrExercise 8 (Add the terms in the new order)

Exercise 8 (Add the terms in the new order) In the setting of Exercise 7, add up the terms.

SolutionSolution 8

Solution 8. The total is \(25 + 1 + 4 = 30\).

ExrExercise 9 (Does the order change the total?)

Exercise 9 (Does the order change the total?) Say whether the total in Exercise 8 is the same as the total in Exercise 6.

SolutionSolution 9

Solution 9. The total is the same, because addition does not depend on the order of the terms.

DefDefinition 22 (Sum over a finite set)

Definition 22 (Sum over a finite set) Let \(A = \mathopen{}\left\{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_k\right\}\mathclose{}\) be a finite set with \(k \ge 1\) different elements. Let \(f\) be a function that gives a number \(f(x)\) for each element \(x\) of \(A\). The sum of \(f\) over \(A\) is

\[ \sum_{x \in A} f(x) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}f(x_1) + f(x_2) + \cdots + f(x_k) \]

If \(A\) has no elements, the sum has no terms, and by convention it equals \(0\).

RemarkRemark 7 (The order of the terms does not matter)

Remark 7 (The order of the terms does not matter). The order in which we list the elements of \(A\) does not change the sum. The reason is that addition is commutative and associative: we can reorder and regroup the terms of a finite sum without changing the total. For example, if \(A = \mathopen{}\left\{1, 2, 3\right\}\mathclose{}\) and \(f(x) = x^2\), listing \(A\) as \(1, 2, 3\) gives \(1 + 4 + 9 = 14\), and listing \(A\) as \(3, 1, 2\) gives \(9 + 1 + 4 = 14\). Exercise 6 and Exercise 8 show another example.

When \(A = \mathopen{}\left\{m, m+1, \ldots, n\right\}\mathclose{}\), this sum is the same as \(\sum_{i=m}^{n} f(i)\) from Definition 19.

RemarkRemark 8 (Leaving the set out)

Remark 8 (Leaving the set out). Some authors leave the set out and write \(\sum_{x} f(x)\). This shorthand means the sum over every value \(x\) can take, \(\sum_{x \in \mathcal{R}(x)} f(x)\), where \(\mathcal{R}(x)\) is the range of \(x\). For example, if \(x\) is the outcome of one roll of a six-sided die, \(\sum_{x} f(x)\) means \(\sum_{x \in \mathopen{}\left\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\right\}\mathclose{}} f(x)\). In these notes, we write the set out in full (see Limits of sums and notational shorthands).

ExmExample 19 (A sum over the empty set)

Example 19 (A sum over the empty set) For example, \(\sum_{x \in \mathopen{}\left\{\right\}\mathclose{}} x^2 = 0\).

8 Quotients

DefDefinition 23 (Quotient)

Definition 23 (Quotient) For real numbers \(a\) and \(b\) with \(b \neq 0\), the quotient of \(a\) by \(b\) is the result of dividing \(a\) by \(b\):

\[\frac{a}{b}\]

DefDefinition 24 (Fraction)

Definition 24 (Fraction) A quotient \(\frac{a}{b}\) (Definition 23) is also called a fraction.

DefDefinition 25 (Numerator)

Definition 25 (Numerator) In a fraction \(\frac{a}{b}\) (Definition 24), \(a\) is its numerator.

DefDefinition 26 (Denominator)

Definition 26 (Denominator) In a fraction \(\frac{a}{b}\) (Definition 24), \(b\) is its denominator.

ExmExample 20 (Numerator and denominator of a fraction)

Example 20 (Numerator and denominator of a fraction) The fraction \(\frac{6}{4}\) has numerator \(6\) and denominator \(4\).

ExmExample 21 (A quotient)

Example 21 (A quotient) The quotient of \(6\) by \(4\) is \(\frac{6}{4} = 1.5\). The quotient of \(6\) by \(0\) is undefined, because Definition 23 requires a nonzero denominator.

DefDefinition 27 (Follow-up)

Definition 27 (Follow-up) In a study that observes participants over time, a participant’s follow-up is the length of time the study observes that participant.

DefDefinition 28 (Person-years)

Definition 28 (Person-years) In a study that observes participants over time, the study’s person-years are the sum of the follow-up times (Definition 27) of all participants, measured in years.

ExmExample 22 (Counting person-years)

Example 22 (Counting person-years) If \(10\) people are each followed for \(2\) years, the study has \(10 \cdot 2 = 20\) person-years. If one person is followed for \(3\) years and another for \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) year, the study has \(3 + \tfrac{1}{2} = 3.5\) person-years.

DefDefinition 29 (Rate)

Definition 29 (Rate) A rate is a quotient of two quantities, usually with a denominator that measures time, such as weeks or person-years of follow-up (Definition 28). For example, \(12\) new cases in \(4\) weeks is a rate of \(\frac{12}{4} = 3\) new cases per week, and \(30\) cases over \(10{,}000\) person-years of follow-up is a rate of \(\frac{30}{10{,}000} = 0.003\) cases per person-year.

cf. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_(mathematics)

DefDefinition 30 (Ratios)

Definition 30 (Ratios) A ratio is a quotient in which the numerator and denominator are measured using the same unit scales.

cf. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratio

ExmExample 23 (A ratio, and a quotient that is not one)

Example 23 (A ratio, and a quotient that is not one)  

  • A board \(150\) cm long and one \(75\) cm long have length ratio \(\tfrac{150 \text{ cm}}{75 \text{ cm}} = 2\): both lengths are in centimeters, so the units cancel and the ratio has none.
  • A sample of mass \(300\) g and volume \(150\) cm\(^3\) gives the quotient \(\tfrac{300 \text{ g}}{150 \text{ cm}^3} = 2\) g per cm\(^3\), its density. The numerator and denominator are in different units, so this quotient is not a ratio.
DefDefinition 31 (Proportion)

Definition 31 (Proportion) In statistics, a proportion typically means a ratio where the numerator represents a subset of the denominator.

See https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_proportion.

See also https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportion_(mathematics) for other meanings.

ExmExample 24 (A proportion, and a ratio that is not one)

Example 24 (A proportion, and a ratio that is not one) In a clinic with \(120\) patients, \(30\) of whom smoke:

  • the proportion of patients who smoke is \(\tfrac{30}{120} = 0.25\): the \(30\) smokers are a subset of the \(120\) patients;
  • the ratio of smokers to non-smokers is \(\tfrac{30}{90} = \tfrac{1}{3}\). Both counts are of patients, so this quotient is a ratio, but it is not a proportion: the \(30\) smokers are not part of the \(90\) non-smokers.
DefDefinition 32 (Proportional)

Definition 32 (Proportional) Two functions \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) are proportional if their ratio \(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) does not depend on \(x\). (cf. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportionality_(mathematics))

ExmExample 25 (Proportional and non-proportional functions)

Example 25 (Proportional and non-proportional functions)  

  • \(f(x) = 6x^2\) and \(g(x) = 2x^2\) are proportional: for \(x \ne 0\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} \tfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} &= \tfrac{6x^2}{2x^2} \\ &= 3, \end{aligned} \]

    which does not depend on \(x\).

  • \(f(x) = x + 1\) and \(g(x) = x\) are not proportional: for \(x \ne 0\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} \tfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} &= \tfrac{x + 1}{x} \\ &= \tfrac{x}{x} + \tfrac{1}{x} \\ &= 1 + \tfrac{1}{x}, \end{aligned} \]

    which is \(2\) at \(x = 1\) and \(\tfrac{3}{2}\) at \(x = 2\).

Additional reference for elementary algebra: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_proportion#Mathematical_definition

9 Limits of sums and notational shorthands

DefDefinition 33 (Lower limit of a sum or integral)

Definition 33 (Lower limit of a sum or integral) In a sum \(\sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i\), the lower limit \(m\) is the first value of the index \(i\). In an integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), the lower limit \(a\) is the left end of the interval of integration. In both, the lower limit is written below the symbol.

DefDefinition 34 (Upper limit of a sum or integral)

Definition 34 (Upper limit of a sum or integral) In a sum \(\sum_{i=m}^{n} a_i\), the upper limit \(n\) is the last value of the index \(i\). In an integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), the upper limit \(b\) is the right end of the interval of integration. In both, the upper limit is written above the symbol.

DefDefinition 35 (Limits of integration)

Definition 35 (Limits of integration) In an integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), the lower limit (Definition 33) \(a\) and the upper limit (Definition 34) \(b\) together are the limits of integration.

ExmExample 26 (Reading off limits)

Example 26 (Reading off limits)  

  • In \(\sum_{i=2}^{5} i^2\), the lower limit is \(2\) and the upper limit is \(5\), so the sum is \(2^2 + 3^2 + 4^2 + 5^2 = 54\).
  • In \(\int_0^3 x\,dx\), the lower limit is \(0\) and the upper limit is \(3\).
RemarkRemark 9 (Two meanings of “limit”)

Remark 9 (Two meanings of “limit”). The lower and upper limits of a sum or integral (Definition 33, Definition 34) are not limits in the calculus sense, such as the limit of a function or the limit of a sequence. They are the ends of the values the index or variable runs over. The two meanings meet in an integral such as \(\int_0^{\infty} f(x)\,dx\), which means \(\lim_{b \to \infty} \int_0^b f(x)\,dx\): the upper limit \(b\) is sent to infinity by a calculus limit.

DefDefinition 36 (Notational shorthand)

Definition 36 (Notational shorthand) A notational shorthand is a way of writing an expression that leaves part of the expression out, such as the set a sum runs over or the limits of an integral, and relies on the reader to supply the missing part from context.

DefDefinition 37 (Full form)

Definition 37 (Full form) The full form of a notational shorthand (Definition 36) is the expression with every part written out.

NoteThese notes write the full form

In these notes, we avoid notational shorthands in permanent writing and write the full form of each expression, even where a shorthand would be shorter. A reader who supplies the missing part of a shorthand from a different context than the writer intended reads a different expression than the one the writer meant. Handwritten board work is often less complete, but it should still write out ranges and limits where it can.

We make one exception: writing two factors side by side means multiplying them, as in \(2x\) for \(2 \cdot x\) and \(ab\) for \(a \cdot b\). We still write the multiplication sign when the side-by-side form could be misread:

  • \(2 \cdot 3\), not \(23\), which reads as twenty-three;
  • \(a \cdot(b + c)\) when \(a\) could be a function, since \(a(b + c)\) could mean \(a\) evaluated at \(b + c\).
DefDefinition 38 (Range of a variable)

Definition 38 (Range of a variable) The range of a variable \(x\), written \(\mathcal{R}(x)\), is the set of values that \(x\) can take.

For example, if \(x\) is the number of heads in two tosses of a coin, then \(\mathcal{R}(x) = \mathopen{}\left\{0, 1, 2\right\}\mathclose{}\). A random variable \(X\) (defined in the Morrison Lab’s probability notes) is a function, and the set of values \(X\) can take, \(\mathcal{R}(X)\), is the image of \(X\). These notes use “range” only for variables: for a function, “range” can mean either the image or the codomain (Range). In statistics, “the range” of a dataset can also mean its largest value minus its smallest value; \(\mathcal{R}(x)\) is a set, not that difference.

Table 1 lists shorthands you may encounter in other sources, the full form each one abbreviates, and the part each one leaves out. In the table, \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\) and \(a_1, \ldots, a_n\) are \(n\) numbers, \(b_{ij}\) is the entry in row \(i\) and column \(j\) of an \(m \times n\) array, \(k\) is an integer with \(1 \le k \le n\), \(f\) is a function from \(\mathbb{R}\) to \(\mathbb{R}\), and, in the first row, \(\mathcal{R}(x)\) is a finite set.

RemarkRemark 10 (\(\int f(x)\,dx\) has two readings)

Remark 10 (\(\int f(x)\,dx\) has two readings). In calculus, \(\int f(x)\,dx\) with no limits is not a shorthand: it is the indefinite integral of \(f\), a family of functions rather than a number. Some sources, especially in probability, also write \(\int f(x)\,dx\) for the number \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx\). Writing the limits out tells the reader which of the two is meant.

ExmExample 27 (Writing out an index shorthand)

Example 27 (Writing out an index shorthand) Let \(n = 3\) and \((x_1, x_2, x_3) = (2, 5, 1)\). A source that writes \(\sum_{i} x_i\) means \(\sum_{i=1}^{3} x_i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{3} x_i &= x_1 + x_2 + x_3 && \text{(definition of summation notation)} \\ &= 2 + 5 + 1 && \text{(substitute the values)} \\ &= 8 && \text{(add)} \end{aligned} \]
