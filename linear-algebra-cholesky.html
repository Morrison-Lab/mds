1 Gram matrices, Schur complements and Cholesky

NoteSource

This section is adapted from Zhou [1], used under the MIT License (see the license text in Section  in Subspaces and Rank). The source’s eigenvalue and quadratic-form characterizations of definiteness are covered in these notes already (Theorem 2 in Definite Matrices). These parts of the source are left out:

  • its covariance test, which needs probability
  • its material on minimization
  • its positive semidefinite version of the Schur complement test
  • its pivot test
  • its Hadamard-product “Schur lemma”
  • its notes on the LU factorization and computational cost
  • its statement that a nonnegative combination of positive semidefinite matrices is positive semidefinite (Theorem 2 states the positive definite version)
  • its section on the ellipse \(a x^2 + 2 b x y + c y^2 = 1\), with its worked example of a positive definite and an indefinite matrix
  • its numerical example of the Cholesky factorization

The source leaves the Schur complement test unproved; it is proved here from a block expansion of the quadratic form, and the Cholesky factorization is derived from it.

LemTheorem 1 (Definite matrices are Gram matrices)

Theorem 1 (Definite matrices are Gram matrices) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be a \(p \times p\) matrix.

  1. \(\mathbf{A}\) is positive semidefinite (Definition 1 in Definite Matrices) exactly when \(\mathbf{A} = {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \mathbf{B}\) for some matrix \(\mathbf{B}\) with \(p\) columns.
  2. \(\mathbf{A}\) is positive definite (Definition 2 in Definite Matrices) exactly when \(\mathbf{A} = {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \mathbf{B}\) for some matrix \(\mathbf{B}\) with \(p\) linearly independent columns.
ProofProof

Proof. If \(\mathbf{A} = {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \mathbf{B}\). \(\mathbf{A}\) is symmetric, since

\[ \begin{aligned} {({\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \mathbf{B})}^{\top} &= {\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\,{({\mathbf{B}}^{\top})}^{\top} \\ &= {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \mathbf{B} \end{aligned} \]

(Theorem 6 in Matrix Multiplication, Definition 6 in Matrices). For any \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(substitute } \mathbf{A} \text{)} \\ &= {(\mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-transpose-product}{\text{Theorem~6 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{, }\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#eq-norm-dot-product}{\text{Equation~5 in Vectors}}\text{, squared)} \\ &\ge 0. && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-inner-products.qmd#thm-norm-properties}{\text{Theorem~1 in Inner Products and the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

If the columns of \(\mathbf{B}\) are linearly independent, then \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{B}) = \mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right\}\mathclose{}\) (\(\mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is the combination of the columns with coefficients \(x_j\), Theorem 3 in Subspaces and Rank, and only the zero combination is \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), Definition 1 in Subspaces and Rank), so for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), \(\mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) and \(\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 > 0\) (Theorem 1 in Inner Products and the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality, part 1).

Only if. Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be positive semidefinite. It is symmetric, so \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{\Lambda} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\) (Theorem 1 in Eigenvalues and Matrix Decompositions), and every \(\lambda_i \ge 0\) (Theorem 2 in Definite Matrices). Let \(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1}, \ldots, \sqrt{\lambda_p})\), which is symmetric, with \(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2} \mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2} = \mathbf{\Lambda}\) (the product of diagonal matrices multiplies their diagonals, as in the proof of Theorem 9 in Similarity and Diagonalization), and let \(\mathbf{B} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \mathbf{B} &= {({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top})}^{\top}\,{(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2})}^{\top}\,\mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-transpose-product}{\text{Theorem~6 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2} \mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2}\,{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrices.qmd#def-matrix-transpose}{\text{Definition~6 in Matrices}}\text{; } \mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2} \text{ is symmetric)} \\ &= \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{\Lambda} {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} && \text{(} \mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2} \mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2} = \mathbf{\Lambda} \text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{A}. && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-decompositions.qmd#thm-spectral}{\text{Theorem~1 in Eigenvalues and Matrix Decompositions}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

If \(\mathbf{A}\) is positive definite, it is positive semidefinite (Remark 1 in Definite Matrices), so the same \(\mathbf{B}\) gives \(\mathbf{A} = {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \mathbf{B}\); and every \(\lambda_i > 0\) (Theorem 2 in Definite Matrices), so \(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2}\) is diagonal with nonzero diagonal, and invertible (its inverse is \(\operatorname{diag}(1/\sqrt{\lambda_1}, \ldots, 1/\sqrt{\lambda_p})\)); \({\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\) is invertible with inverse \(\mathbf{Q}\) (Remark 6 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace). So \(\mathbf{B}\) is invertible (Theorem 1 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace), and its \(p\) columns are linearly independent (Theorem 1 in Similarity and Diagonalization, Definition 2 in Subspaces and Rank).

ExmExample 1 (Gram factorizations)

Example 1 (Gram factorizations)  

  • With \(\mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) (\(1 \times 2\)), \({\mathbf{R}}^{\top} \mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), the positive semidefinite matrix of Example 1 in Definite Matrices; the two columns of \(\mathbf{R}\), \(1\) and \(1\), are dependent, so part 2 does not apply to \(\mathbf{R}\); \({\mathbf{R}}^{\top} \mathbf{R}\) is in fact not positive definite (Example 4 in Definite Matrices).
  • With \(\mathbf{X}= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\), whose columns are independent (Example 2 in Subspaces and Rank), \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top} \mathbf{X}= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}\) (Example 1 in Design Matrices and Projections) is positive definite.
  • \(\mathbf{D} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) of Example 4 in Definite Matrices is symmetric but not positive semidefinite, so by part 1 it is not \({\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \mathbf{B}\) for any \(\mathbf{B}\).
LemTheorem 2 (Operations that preserve definiteness)

Theorem 2 (Operations that preserve definiteness)  

  1. If \(\mathbf{C}\) is a \(p \times p\) positive definite matrix (Definition 2 in Definite Matrices) and \(\mathbf{A}\) is a \(p \times k\) matrix with \(k\) linearly independent columns, then the \(k \times k\) matrix \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}\) is positive definite.
  2. If \(\mathbf{A}_1\) and \(\mathbf{A}_2\) are positive definite \(p \times p\) matrices and \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2 > 0\), then \(\alpha_1 \mathbf{A}_1 + \alpha_2 \mathbf{A}_2\) is positive definite.
ProofProof

Proof. Part 1. \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A}\) is symmetric:

\[ \begin{aligned} {({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A})}^{\top} &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\,{({\mathbf{A}}^{\top})}^{\top} \\ &= {\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A} \end{aligned} \]

(Theorem 6 in Matrix Multiplication, twice; \(\mathbf{C}\) symmetric; Definition 6 in Matrices). For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_k\), \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p\), since the columns of \(\mathbf{A}\) are independent (Theorem 3 in Subspaces and Rank, Definition 1 in Subspaces and Rank), and

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} \mathbf{C} \mathbf{A})\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= {(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}^{\top}\,\mathbf{C}\,(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-transpose-product}{\text{Theorem~6 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{, }\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-assoc}{\text{Theorem~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{)} \\ &> 0. && \text{(} \mathbf{C} \text{ positive definite, } \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Part 2. \(\alpha_1 \mathbf{A}_1 + \alpha_2 \mathbf{A}_2\) is symmetric: its transpose is \(\alpha_1 {\mathbf{A}_1}^{\top} + \alpha_2 {\mathbf{A}_2}^{\top} = \alpha_1 \mathbf{A}_1 + \alpha_2 \mathbf{A}_2\) (Theorem 5 in Matrix Multiplication, Definition 9 in Matrices, Definition 6 in Matrices). For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,(\alpha_1 \mathbf{A}_1 + \alpha_2 \mathbf{A}_2)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,(\alpha_1 \mathbf{A}_1)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}+ {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,(\alpha_2 \mathbf{A}_2)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-distrib}{\text{Theorem~2 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{, twice)} \\ &= \alpha_1\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}_1 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} + \alpha_2\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}_2 \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-generalized-inverses.qmd#thm-scalar-matmul}{\text{Theorem~1 in Generalized Inverses and Linear Systems}}\text{)} \\ &> 0. && \text{(each term is positive)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 2 (Building positive definite matrices)

Example 2 (Building positive definite matrices)  

  • With \(\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{I}_3\) and \(\mathbf{X}\) of Example 1, part 1 gives again that \({\mathbf{X}}^{\top} \mathbf{X}\) is positive definite.

  • With \(\alpha_1 = 2\), \(\mathbf{A}_1 = \mathbf{I}_2\) (Example 4 in Definite Matrices), \(\alpha_2 = 1\) and

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}_2 &= {\mathbf{X}}^{\top} \mathbf{X}\\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

    (Example 1), part 2 gives that \(2 \mathbf{I}_2 + {\mathbf{X}}^{\top} \mathbf{X}= \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 6 & 16 \end{bmatrix}\) is positive definite.

  • An aside beyond part 2: \(\mathbf{I}_2 + \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) is positive definite plus positive semidefinite; its quadratic form is \(x_1^2 + x_2^2 + (x_1 + x_2)^2 > 0\) for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), so it is positive definite, in line with its eigenvalues \(3\) and \(1\) (Example 1 in Eigenvalues and Matrix Decompositions).

  • Independence is needed in part 1: with \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), whose columns are equal, \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{I}_2\,\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), and \((1, -1)\) gives quadratic form \(0\).

LemTheorem 3 (A positive definite matrix is invertible)

Theorem 3 (A positive definite matrix is invertible) Every positive definite matrix (Definition 2 in Definite Matrices) is invertible (Definition 6 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace).

ProofProof

Proof. Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be a \(p \times p\) positive definite matrix, and let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) be a vector of length \(p\) with \(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-special-matrices.qmd#def-quadratic-form}{\text{Definition~13 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace}}\text{)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p && \text{(} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p \text{)} \\ &= 0. && \text{(every term of the inner product is } 0 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p\), then \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} > 0\) because \(\mathbf{A}\) is positive definite, which contradicts \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} = 0\). So \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p\), the null space \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\) is \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p\right\}\mathclose{}\), and \(\mathbf{A}\) is invertible by Theorem 1 in Similarity and Diagonalization.

ExmExample 3 (Positive definite matrices are invertible; the converse fails)

Example 3 (Positive definite matrices are invertible; the converse fails) \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) is positive definite, since

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= 2 x_1^2 + 2 x_1 x_2 + 2 x_2^2 && \text{(multiply out)} \\ &= x_1^2 + x_2^2 + (x_1 + x_2)^2, && \text{(expand the square)} \end{aligned} \]

which is \(0\) only when \(x_1 = x_2 = 0\). It is invertible, with \({\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\mathclose{}}^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\).

The converse fails. \(\mathbf{D} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) of Example 4 in Definite Matrices is not positive definite, yet it is invertible: \({\mathopen{}\left(\mathbf{D}\right)\mathclose{}}^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\).

LemTheorem 4 (Adding a positive multiple of the identity makes a matrix positive definite)

Theorem 4 (Adding a positive multiple of the identity makes a matrix positive definite) Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be a \(p \times p\) positive semidefinite matrix (Definition 1 in Definite Matrices) and let \(\alpha> 0\). Then \(\mathbf{A} + \alpha\mathbf{I}_p\) is positive definite (Definition 2 in Definite Matrices), so it is invertible (Theorem 3).

ProofProof

Proof. \(\mathbf{A} + \alpha\mathbf{I}_p\) is symmetric: its transpose is \({\mathbf{A}}^{\top} + \alpha\mathbf{I}_p = \mathbf{A} + \alpha\mathbf{I}_p\) (Theorem 5 in Matrix Multiplication, Definition 9 in Matrices, Definition 6 in Matrices). For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p\),

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,(\mathbf{A} + \alpha\mathbf{I}_p)\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} + \alpha\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top}\,\mathbf{I}_p\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-matmul-distrib}{\text{Theorem~2 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{, }\href{linear-algebra-generalized-inverses.qmd#thm-scalar-matmul}{\text{Theorem~1 in Generalized Inverses and Linear Systems}}\text{)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} + \alpha\,\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right\rVert\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#thm-identity}{\text{Theorem~4 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{, }\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#eq-norm-dot-product}{\text{Equation~5 in Vectors}}\text{, squared)} \\ &> 0. && \text{(} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}} \ge 0, \; \alpha> 0, \; \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_p \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 4 (Repairing a singular matrix)

DefDefinition 1 (Block matrix)

Definition 1 (Block matrix) Split the rows of an \(m \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) into the first \(k\) and the last \(m - k\), and its columns into the first \(l\) and the last \(n - l\), with \(1 \le k < m\) and \(1 \le l < n\). This cuts \(\mathbf{X}\) into four smaller matrices, its blocks:

\[ \mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{B} \\ \mathbf{C} & \mathbf{D} \end{bmatrix}, \]

where \(\mathbf{A}\) (\(k \times l\)) holds the entries \(x_{ij}\) with \(i \le k\) and \(j \le l\), \(\mathbf{B}\) (\(k \times (n - l)\)) those with \(i \le k\) and \(j > l\), \(\mathbf{C}\) (\((m - k) \times l\)) those with \(i > k\) and \(j \le l\), and \(\mathbf{D}\) (\((m - k) \times (n - l)\)) those with \(i > k\) and \(j > l\), each in its original order. A matrix written this way is a block matrix [2].

ExmExample 6 (Cutting a \(3 \times 3\) matrix into blocks)

Example 6 (Cutting a \(3 \times 3\) matrix into blocks) With

\[ \begin{aligned} k &= l \\ &= 1, \end{aligned} \]

\[ \mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 5 & 3 \\ 1 & 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{B} \\ \mathbf{C} & \mathbf{D} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{D} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}. \]

For instance, entry \((1, 2)\) of \(\mathbf{D}\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} x_{1 + 1, 2 + 1} &= x_{23} \\ &= 3. \end{aligned} \]

With

\[ \begin{aligned} k &= l \\ &= 2 \end{aligned} \]

instead, the leading \(2 \times 2\) block is \(\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\).

DefDefinition 2 (Leading block of a square matrix, top-left block)

Definition 2 (Leading block of a square matrix, top-left block) When \(\mathbf{X}\) is \(n \times n\) and \(1 \le k \le n\), the \(k \times k\) matrix of the entries \(x_{ij}\) with \(i, j \le k\) is the leading (or top-left) \(k \times k\) block of \(\mathbf{X}\); for \(k < n\) it is the block \(\mathbf{A}\) of Definition 1 with \(l = k\), and for \(k = n\) it is \(\mathbf{X}\) itself.

LemTheorem 5 (Leading blocks of a positive definite matrix are positive definite)

Theorem 5 (Leading blocks of a positive definite matrix are positive definite) Let \(\mathbf{X}\) be a \(p \times p\) positive definite matrix (Definition 2 in Definite Matrices), and for \(1 \le k \le p\) let \(\mathbf{X}_k\) be its leading \(k \times k\) block (Definition 1), with entries \(x_{ij}\) for \(i, j \le k\). Then \(\mathbf{X}_k\) is positive definite. Also, every diagonal entry of a positive definite matrix is positive.

ProofProof

Proof. \(\mathbf{X}_k\) is symmetric, because \(\mathbf{X}\) is. For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \in \mathbb{R}^k\) with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = (u_1, \ldots, u_k, 0, \ldots, 0) \in \mathbb{R}^p\), which is nonzero, with entries \(y_i = u_i\) for \(i \le k\) and \(y_i = 0\) for \(i > k\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}}^{\top} \mathbf{X}_k \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} &= \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sum_{j=1}^{k} u_i\,x_{ij}\,u_j && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#def-matvec-mult}{\text{Definition~8 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{, }\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~10 in Vectors}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sum_{j=1}^{k} y_i\,x_{ij}\,y_j && \text{(} y_i = u_i \text{ for } i \le k \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^p\sum_{j=1}^py_i\,x_{ij}\,y_j && \text{(each added term has a factor } y_i = 0 \text{ or } y_j = 0 \text{)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}^{\top} \mathbf{X} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#def-matvec-mult}{\text{Definition~8 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{, }\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~10 in Vectors}}\text{)} \\ &> 0. && \text{(} \mathbf{X} \text{ positive definite, } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

For the diagonal entry \(x_{ii}\), take \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i\) (Definition 21 in Vectors), which is nonzero:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i}^{\top} \mathbf{X} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i &= \sum_{r=1}^{p} \sum_{s=1}^{p} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i)_r\,x_{rs}\,(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{e}}\vphantom{e}}_i)_s && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#def-matvec-mult}{\text{Definition~8 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{, }\href{linear-algebra-vectors.qmd#def-dot-product}{\text{Definition~10 in Vectors}}\text{)} \\ &= x_{ii} && \text{(only the term with } r \text{ and } s \text{ both equal to } i \text{ is nonzero)} \\ &> 0. && \text{(} \mathbf{X} \text{ positive definite)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 7 (A quick necessary test)

Example 7 (A quick necessary test) \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) is positive definite (Example 2), and its diagonal entries \(2, 2\) and its \(1 \times 1\) leading block \([2]\) are positive. ExmExample 8 (The \(2 \times 2\) case)

Example 8 (The \(2 \times 2\) case) For \(\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{bmatrix}\) (\(k = 1\) and \(m = 1\)) and \(a \ne 0\), the theorem reads

\[ a u^2 + 2buw + cw^2 = a\,\mathopen{}\left(u + \tfrac{b}{a}\,w\right)\mathclose{}^2 + \mathopen{}\left(c - \tfrac{b^2}{a}\right)\mathclose{}\,w^2, \]

with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = (u, w)\): the familiar completing of the square. With

\[ \begin{aligned} a &= c \\ &= 5 \end{aligned} \]

and \(b = 4\):

\[ \begin{aligned} 5\,(u + 0.8\,w)^2 + 1.8\,w^2 &= 5\,(u^2 + 1.6\,uw + 0.64\,w^2) + 1.8\,w^2 \\ &= 5u^2 + 8uw + 3.2\,w^2 + 1.8\,w^2 \\ &= 5u^2 + 8uw + 5w^2. \end{aligned} \]

DefDefinition 3 (Schur complement)

Definition 3 (Schur complement) For a block matrix \(\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{B} \\ {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} & \mathbf{C} \end{bmatrix}\) as in Theorem 6 with \(\mathbf{A}\) invertible, the Schur complement [3] of \(\mathbf{A}\) in \(\mathbf{X}\) is the \(m \times m\) matrix \(\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{C} - {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B}\) defined in Theorem 6.

ExmExample 9 (Schur complements of \(2 \times 2\) matrices)

Example 9 (Schur complements of \(2 \times 2\) matrices)  

  • For \(\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\) with \(\mathbf{A} = [5]\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{S} &= 5 - 4 \cdot\tfrac{1}{5} \cdot 4 \\ &= \tfrac{25}{5} - \tfrac{16}{5} \\ &= \tfrac{9}{5}, \end{aligned} \]

    that is, \(\mathbf{S} = [\tfrac{9}{5}]\).

  • For \(\mathbf{D} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) with \(\mathbf{A} = [1]\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{S} &= [1 - 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2] \\ &= [-3]. \end{aligned} \]

  • For \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) the top-left block \([0]\) is not invertible, so there is no Schur complement of it.

LemTheorem 7 (Schur complement test)

Theorem 7 (Schur complement test) Let \(\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{B} \\ {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} & \mathbf{C} \end{bmatrix}\) be as in Theorem 6. Then \(\mathbf{X}\) is positive definite (Definition 2 in Definite Matrices) exactly when \(\mathbf{A}\) is positive definite and its Schur complement \(\mathbf{S}\) (Definition 3) is positive definite.

ProofProof

Proof. If. \(\mathbf{A}\) is positive definite, so invertible (Theorem 4 in Definite Matrices), and \(\mathbf{S}\) is defined. \(\mathbf{X}\) is symmetric. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} + \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\). By Theorem 6, \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} \mathbf{X} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}}}^{\top} \mathbf{A} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} + {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\), and both terms are at least \(0\). If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), the second term is positive. If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}\), so the first term is positive. Either way \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} \mathbf{X} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} > 0\).

Only if. \(\mathbf{A}\) is the top-left \(k \times k\) block of \(\mathbf{X}\), so it is positive definite (Theorem 5), and invertible (Theorem 4 in Definite Matrices). \(\mathbf{S}\) is symmetric:

\[ \begin{aligned} {({\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B})}^{\top} &= {\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\,{(\mathbf{A}^{-1})}^{\top}\,\mathbf{B} \\ &= {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B} \end{aligned} \]

(Theorem 6 in Matrix Multiplication, Definition 6 in Matrices for \({({\mathbf{B}}^{\top})}^{\top} = \mathbf{B}\), Corollary 1 in Special Matrices, Quadratic Forms and Trace), and \(\mathbf{C}\) is symmetric, so \(\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{C} - {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B}\) is symmetric (the transpose of a difference is the difference of the transposes, entry by entry, Definition 6 in Matrices). For \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), take \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}} = -\mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}\), so \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{z}}\vphantom{z}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} = (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{u}}\vphantom{u}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}) \ne \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\); then \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}}}^{\top} \mathbf{S} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{w}}\vphantom{w}} = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}}}^{\top} \mathbf{X} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{v}}\vphantom{v}} > 0\) (Theorem 6).

ExmExample 10 (Testing \(2 \times 2\) matrices)

Example 10 (Testing \(2 \times 2\) matrices) A symmetric \(\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{bmatrix}\) is positive definite exactly when \(a > 0\) and \(c - \tfrac{b^2}{a} > 0\), that is, \(a > 0\) and \(ac > b^2\) (a \(1 \times 1\) matrix \([s]\) is positive definite exactly when \(s > 0\), since \({x}^{\top} s x = s x^2\)).

  • \(\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\): \(5 > 0\) and \(\tfrac{9}{5} > 0\) (Example 9), so it is positive definite.

LemTheorem 8 (The inverse of a positive definite block matrix)

Theorem 8 (The inverse of a positive definite block matrix) Let \(\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{B} \\ {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} & \mathbf{C} \end{bmatrix}\) be as in Theorem 6, with \(\mathbf{X}\) positive definite. Then \(\mathbf{X}\) is invertible, and with the Schur complement \(\mathbf{S}\) of \(\mathbf{A}\), its inverse is the following, where \(\mathbf{P}\) is the block of the first \(k\) rows and columns of \(\mathbf{X}^{-1}\):

\[ \mathbf{X}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{P} & -\mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{S}^{-1} \\ -\mathbf{S}^{-1} {\mathbf{B}}^{\top} \mathbf{A}^{-1} & \mathbf{S}^{-1} \end{bmatrix}, \]

ProofProof

Proof. \(\mathbf{X}\) is invertible, because it is positive definite. By the Schur complement test, \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{S}\) are positive definite as well, and so both are invertible. Let \(\mathbf{W} = \begin{bmatrix} -\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B} \\ \mathbf{I}_m \end{bmatrix}\). Each entry of \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\) adds one group of \(k\) summands to one group of \(m\) summands, so \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\) can be computed one block row at a time:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} &= \begin{bmatrix} -\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{B} \\ -{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{C} \end{bmatrix} && \text{(one block row at a time)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} -\mathbf{I}_k\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{B} \\ \mathbf{S} \end{bmatrix} && \text{(} \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \mathbf{I}_k \text{, and definition of } \mathbf{S} \text{)} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{S} \end{bmatrix} && \text{(} \mathbf{I}_k\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying both sides on the left by \(\mathbf{X}^{-1}\) gives \(\mathbf{W}\) on the left, since \(\mathbf{X}^{-1}\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{I}_{k+m}\). Cut \(\mathbf{X}^{-1}\) into the four pieces \(\mathbf{P}\), \(\mathbf{Q}\), \(\mathbf{R}\) and \(\mathbf{T}\), with \(\mathbf{Q}\) and \(\mathbf{T}\) in the last \(m\) columns. Then the right side is \(\mathbf{X}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{S} \end{bmatrix}\), and the last \(m\) columns of \(\mathbf{X}^{-1}\) multiply \(\mathbf{S}\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} -\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B} \\ \mathbf{I}_m \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{S} \\ \mathbf{T}\mathbf{S} \end{bmatrix}. \]

So \(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{I}_m\), and \(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{S} = -\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\). Multiplying both equations on the right by \(\mathbf{S}^{-1}\) gives \(\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{S}^{-1}\), and \(\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}^{-1}\). \(\mathbf{X}^{-1}\) is symmetric, by the inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric, so \(\mathbf{R} = {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} = -\mathbf{S}^{-1}{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\), using \({(\mathbf{S}^{-1})}^{\top} = \mathbf{S}^{-1}\) and \({(\mathbf{A}^{-1})}^{\top} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\).

LemTheorem 9 (Cholesky factorization)

Theorem 9 (Cholesky factorization) A \(p \times p\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) is positive definite (Definition 2 in Definite Matrices) exactly when \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L} {\mathbf{L}}^{\top}\) for some lower triangular \(\mathbf{L}\) (Definition 2 in Solving Linear Systems and Least Squares) with positive diagonal entries. For a positive definite \(\mathbf{A}\) that \(\mathbf{L}\) is unique; it is the Cholesky factor [4] of \(\mathbf{A}\).

ProofProof

Proof. If. \({\mathbf{L}}^{\top}\) is upper triangular with positive diagonal (Definition 6 in Matrices), so \({\mathbf{L}}^{\top} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) only for \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) (Theorem 1 in Solving Linear Systems and Least Squares), and the columns of \({\mathbf{L}}^{\top}\) are linearly independent (Theorem 3 in Subspaces and Rank, Definition 1 in Subspaces and Rank). So \(\mathbf{A} = {({\mathbf{L}}^{\top})}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{L}}^{\top}\) (Definition 6 in Matrices) is positive definite (Theorem 1, part 2).

Only if, with uniqueness, by induction on \(p\). For \(p = 1\), \(\mathbf{A} = [a]\) with \(a > 0\) (Theorem 5), and \([c]\,[c] = [a]\) with \(c > 0\) holds exactly for \(c = \sqrt{a}\). For \(p > 1\), assume every \((p - 1) \times (p - 1)\) positive definite matrix has exactly one such factor, and split off the first row and column. \(\mathbf{A}\) is symmetric, so its first row is the transpose of its first column:

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top} \\ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} & \mathbf{A}_{22} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{L} = \begin{bmatrix} \ell_{11} & \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{1 \times (p-1)} \\ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} & \mathbf{L}_{22} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad {\mathbf{L}}^{\top} = \begin{bmatrix} \ell_{11} & {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}}^{\top} \\ \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}_{(p-1) \times 1} & {\mathbf{L}_{22}}^{\top} \end{bmatrix}, \]

with \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \in \mathbb{R}^{p-1}\), where \(b_i\) and \(g_i\) are entries \((i + 1, 1)\) of \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{L}\), and \((\mathbf{A}_{22})_{ij}\) and \((\mathbf{L}_{22})_{ij}\) are their entries \((i + 1, j + 1)\); \({\mathbf{L}}^{\top}\) has this form by Definition 6 in Matrices. A \(p \times p\) matrix \(\mathbf{L}\) is lower triangular with positive diagonal exactly when \(\ell_{11} > 0\), its first row is otherwise zero, and \(\mathbf{L}_{22}\) is lower triangular with positive diagonal; \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\) can be anything. So choosing \(\mathbf{L}\) means choosing \(\ell_{11} > 0\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}\) and such an \(\mathbf{L}_{22}\).

Entry \((i, j)\) of \(\mathbf{L} {\mathbf{L}}^{\top}\) is \(\sum_{r=1}^{p} \ell_{ir}\,\ell_{jr}\), with \(\ell_{ir}\) entry \((i, r)\) of \(\mathbf{L}\) (Definition 1 in Matrix Multiplication, Definition 6 in Matrices). Row \(1\) of \(\mathbf{L}\) is \((\ell_{11}, 0, \ldots, 0)\), and row \(i + 1\) is \((g_i, (\mathbf{L}_{22})_{i1}, \ldots, (\mathbf{L}_{22})_{i,p-1})\). So, for \(i, j = 1, \ldots, p - 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf{L} {\mathbf{L}}^{\top})_{11} &= \ell_{11}\,\ell_{11} + \sum_{r=2}^{p} 0 \cdot 0 \\ &= \ell_{11}^2, && \text{(rows 1 and 1)} \\ (\mathbf{L} {\mathbf{L}}^{\top})_{i+1,1} &= g_i\,\ell_{11} + \sum_{r=2}^{p} (\mathbf{L}_{22})_{i,r-1} \cdot 0 \\ &= \ell_{11}\,g_i, && \text{(rows } i + 1 \text{ and } 1 \text{)} \\ (\mathbf{L} {\mathbf{L}}^{\top})_{i+1,j+1} &= g_i\,g_j + \sum_{r=2}^{p} (\mathbf{L}_{22})_{i,r-1}\,(\mathbf{L}_{22})_{j,r-1} && \text{(rows } i + 1 \text{ and } j + 1 \text{)} \\ &= (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}}^{\top})_{ij} + (\mathbf{L}_{22} {\mathbf{L}_{22}}^{\top})_{ij}. && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrix-multiplication.qmd#def-matrix-mult}{\text{Definition~1 in Matrix Multiplication}}\text{, with } s = r - 1 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Entry \((1, j + 1)\) is \(\ell_{11}\,g_j\) by the same computation with the roles swapped, the transpose of the \((2, 1)\) block; it matches \({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top}\) exactly when the \((2, 1)\) block matches \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\), which is where the symmetry of \(\mathbf{A}\) is used. So \(\mathbf{L} {\mathbf{L}}^{\top} = \mathbf{A}\) says exactly

\[ \ell_{11}^2 = a_{11}, \qquad \ell_{11}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}, \qquad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}}^{\top} + \mathbf{L}_{22} {\mathbf{L}_{22}}^{\top} = \mathbf{A}_{22}. \]

\(a_{11} > 0\) (Theorem 5), so the first equation with \(\ell_{11} > 0\) forces \(\ell_{11} = \sqrt{a_{11}}\); the second then forces \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} = \tfrac{1}{\ell_{11}}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\); and the third becomes

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{L}_{22} {\mathbf{L}_{22}}^{\top} &= \mathbf{A}_{22} - \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}}^{\top} && \text{(subtract } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}}}^{\top} \text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{A}_{22} - \mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{1}{\ell_{11}}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right)\mathclose{} {\mathopen{}\left(\tfrac{1}{\ell_{11}}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\right)\mathclose{}}^{\top} && \text{(substitute } \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{g}}\vphantom{g}} \text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{A}_{22} - \tfrac{1}{\ell_{11}^2}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top} && \text{(}\href{linear-algebra-matrices.qmd#def-matrix-transpose}{\text{Definition~6 in Matrices}}\text{, }\href{linear-algebra-generalized-inverses.qmd#thm-scalar-matmul}{\text{Theorem~1 in Generalized Inverses and Linear Systems}}\text{)} \\ &= \mathbf{A}_{22} - \tfrac{1}{a_{11}}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top}. && \text{(} \ell_{11}^2 = a_{11} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Here \([a_{11}]\) is invertible with inverse \([1 / a_{11}]\), so with \(\mathbf{B} = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top}\) in Definition 3,

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\,[a_{11}]^{-1} \mathbf{B} &= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}\,\tfrac{1}{a_{11}}\,{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top} \\ &= \tfrac{1}{a_{11}}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}} {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{b}}\vphantom{b}}}^{\top} \end{aligned} \]

(Theorem 1 in Generalized Inverses and Linear Systems), and the right side is the Schur complement of \([a_{11}]\) in \(\mathbf{A}\). It is positive definite by Theorem 7 (with \(k = 1\); \(\mathbf{A}_{22}\) is symmetric because \(\mathbf{A}\) is). By the induction hypothesis it has exactly one factor \(\mathbf{L}_{22}\) of the required kind. So \(\mathbf{L}\) exists and is unique.

Example 12 (Two Cholesky factors)  

  • \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} \ell_{11} &= \sqrt{4} \\ &= 2, \end{aligned} \]

    \[ \begin{aligned} \ell_{21} &= 2/2 \\ &= 1, \end{aligned} \]

    and

    \[ \begin{aligned} \ell_{22}^2 &= 5 - 1^2 \\ &= 4, \end{aligned} \]

    so \(\ell_{22} = 2\): \(\mathbf{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), and

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{L} {\mathbf{L}}^{\top} &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 \cdot 2 & 2 \cdot 1 \\ 1 \cdot 2 & 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \mathbf{A}. \end{aligned} \]

  • \(\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\): \(\ell_{11} = \sqrt{5}\), \(\ell_{21} = 4/\sqrt{5}\), and

    \[ \begin{aligned} \ell_{22}^2 &= 5 - \tfrac{16}{5} \\ &= \tfrac{9}{5}, \end{aligned} \]

    the Schur complement of Example 9, so \(\ell_{22} = \tfrac{3}{\sqrt{5}}\).

  • For \(\mathbf{D} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) the method breaks down:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \ell_{22}^2 &= 1 - 4 \\ &= -3 \end{aligned} \]

    has no real solution, matching the failure of the Schur complement test (Example 10).
